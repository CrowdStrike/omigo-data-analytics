Statistical vs English Terminology

When everyday language collides with formal statistical meaning

1. "Normal"

Not common, but completely okay

  • English sense: "not normal" = deviant, broken, needs fixing — a negative judgment.
  • Statistical sense: the value simply has low probability under this distribution.
  • Still valid: a rare outcome is a legitimate member of the sample space, not an error.
  • Green eyes: roughly 2% of people — a tail value, not a medical condition.
  • By construction: about 5% of perfectly healthy data sits beyond 2σ on either side.
  • The damage: patient hears "your result isn't normal" and imports the English connotation.
  • Mismatch: the doctor spoke descriptively; the patient heard it normatively as "you're broken".
  • Correct framing: "3rd percentile — rare, but let's compare against clinical thresholds."
  • The rule: rare ≠ pathological, and infrequent ≠ wrong.

2. "Probability"

Set theory vs intuitive prediction

  • English sense: an intuitive guess — "probably going to rain" has no calculation behind it.
  • Formal sense: a measure on a sample space Ω obeying the Kolmogorov axioms.
  • The axioms: P(Ω) = 1, P(A) ≥ 0, and countable additivity over disjoint events.
  • Legitimate sources: frequency counts, or a Bayesian posterior updated from a stated prior.
  • The ambiguity: "70% chance this lifts retention" — from 100 tests, from a posterior, or from a hunch?
  • Usually the hunch: a confidence expression dressed in numerical clothing.
  • The damage: stakeholders read "70% probability" as a rigorous, data-backed claim.
  • Consequence: budget and roadmap priority get allocated against a number nobody can audit.
  • With a model: "P(success) = 0.7 given prior data" — and cite the data.
  • Without one: "I think this is likely to work, but that's a judgment call."
  • The rule: don't fake precision you cannot reproduce.

3. "Significant"

p < α vs important

  • English sense: important, meaningful, worth acting on.
  • Statistical sense: p < α — the result is unlikely under the null hypothesis.
  • What it measures: detectability of a difference, not its magnitude or its value.
  • Trivial yet significant: p = 0.001 on a 0.5% click lift is a real but negligible effect.
  • Sample-size effect: with enough users, any non-zero difference crosses the p threshold.
  • The damage: "statistically significant improvement" is read as "important improvement".
  • Consequence: a 0.5% lift ships and then costs more to maintain than it generates.
  • Correct framing: "Significant (p = 0.001) but only a 0.5% lift — below our 2% decision bar."
  • Always report: effect size and a confidence interval alongside every p-value.
