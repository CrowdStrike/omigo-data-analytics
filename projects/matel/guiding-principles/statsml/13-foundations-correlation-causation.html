Correlation vs Causation vs Co-occurrence

Three different relationships that look the same in data but mean completely different things for decisions. Confusing them = the most expensive mistake in data science.

1. The Three Relationships

Co-occurrence vs Correlation vs Causation
  • Co-occurrence: A and B happen at the same time/place. No directionality. No mechanism. Just "both present." Example: ice cream sales and drownings both high in summer. They don't cause each other — summer causes both.
  • Correlation: A and B MOVE TOGETHER systematically (positive or negative). When A increases, B increases (or decreases). Measured by r, ρ, mutual information. Still no directionality — could be A→B, B→A, or C→both.
  • Causation: Changing A DIRECTLY changes B. If you intervene on A (holding everything else constant), B moves. This is the ONLY relationship that supports "do X to achieve Y" decisions.

Why it matters: Correlation supports PREDICTION (if I observe A, I expect B). Causation supports INTERVENTION (if I change A, B will change). Most business decisions are interventions, not predictions. Using correlation to guide intervention = wrong action, wasted money, possible harm.

2. Co-occurrence (Things Happen Together)

Co-occurrence ≠ Related
  • Two rare events in the same patient ≠ connection. With 1000 patients and 50 conditions, SOME will co-occur by chance.
  • Expected co-occurrence = P(A) × P(B) × n. If observed ≈ expected → independent (just chance).
  • Only if observed >> expected (Lift > 2-3×) is there a real association worth investigating.
  • Even then: co-occurrence ≠ causation. It just means "not independent" — still need to find WHY.

Real-world trap — Market basket analysis: "People who buy diapers also buy beer." Co-occurrence = real (both happen in same cart). Causation? NO. Confound: both bought by young parents doing one big shopping trip. Putting beer next to diapers won't increase sales of either.

Real-world trap — Cybersecurity: "This user logged in AND a data exfiltration happened within 1 hour." Co-occurrence = real. Causation? Maybe — or maybe 50 people logged in that hour and only one of them (not this user) exfiltrated. Co-occurrence with rare events looks damning but may be chance.

3. Correlation (Things Move Together)

Correlation Is Real But Doesn't Tell You WHY
  • Ice cream sales ↔ drownings (r=0.85). Perfectly real correlation. Neither causes the other. Summer temperature drives both.
  • Countries with more Nobel prizes consume more chocolate (r=0.79). Both = proxies for wealth/education. Eating chocolate won't make you smarter.
  • Shoe size correlates with math ability in children (r=0.6). Because: both increase with AGE. Bigger feet don't help with algebra.
  • For prediction: Correlation is SUFFICIENT. If shoe size predicts math scores → useful for prediction (even though no causation).
  • For intervention: Correlation is INSUFFICIENT. Giving kids bigger shoes won't improve their math (because the correlation is via age, not direct).

Real-world trap — A/B testing: "Users who use Feature X have 3× higher retention." Correlation = real. Causation? Maybe X causes retention. OR: engaged users (who would retain anyway) are more likely to discover X. Forcing all users to use X may NOT improve retention — it just correlates with the type of user who retains.

Spurious Correlations (Real r, No Relationship)
  • US spending on science → correlates with suicides by hanging (r=0.99 over 10 years). Pure coincidence on trending time series.
  • Nicolas Cage movies → swimming pool drownings (r=0.87). Both just happen to fluctuate similarly over time.
  • The trap: With enough variables, SOME will correlate by chance. 1000 variables → ~50,000 pairwise correlations → ~2,500 at p<0.05 by pure chance.
  • Time-series especially vulnerable: two things that both trend upward will correlate even if completely unrelated. Must detrend before testing correlation.

Rule: Correlation without a PLAUSIBLE MECHANISM is evidence of nothing. The mechanism must exist independent of the data — not invented after seeing the correlation.

4. Causation (One MAKES the Other Happen)

Causation Requires: Intervention → Outcome Change
  • The test: If I CHANGE A (holding everything else constant), does B change? If yes → A causes B.
  • Gold standard: Randomized Controlled Trial (RCT). Random assignment ensures NO confounders.
  • In tech: A/B test IS an RCT. Random users get treatment → measure outcome → difference = causal effect.
  • Without randomization: EVERY observed relationship is potentially confounded. You can't prove causation from observational data alone (only make it more/less plausible).

Real-world example — Drug efficacy: Correlation: "People who take this drug have better outcomes." But: doctors give the drug to LESS SICK patients (indication bias). The drug doesn't help — it just goes to people who were going to recover anyway. Only an RCT (random assignment regardless of severity) reveals true effect.

Real-world example — Feature impact: "Users who completed onboarding have 2× retention." Did onboarding CAUSE retention? Or did motivated users (who would retain anyway) bother to complete onboarding? A/B test: randomly FORCE some users through onboarding vs skip it → measure retention difference → that's the causal effect.

5. Confounders — The Hidden Third Variable

C Causes Both A and B → A Appears to Cause B (But Doesn't)
  • Pattern: Observe correlation between A and B. Conclude A→B. Actually: hidden C→A and C→B simultaneously.
  • Ice cream → drowning? Confound = summer heat. Heat → ice cream AND heat → swimming → drowning.
  • "Education → income"? Partially real, but confound: parental wealth → education AND parental wealth → income (via connections, inheritance). Education's TRUE causal effect is smaller than raw correlation suggests.
  • In ML features: Feature X correlates with target. But: unmeasured variable Z causes both X and target. Remove Z (because unmeasured) → X gets credit for Z's effect. Model WORKS for prediction but misleads if you try to intervene on X.

Real-world trap — Hospital quality: "Hospital A has higher mortality than Hospital B." Confound: Hospital A is a trauma center (takes the sickest patients). The QUALITY is higher at A, but the patient population is sicker → worse outcomes despite better care. Without risk-adjustment: the best hospitals look worst.

6. Direction Problems — Which Causes Which?

A→B or B→A? Data Can't Tell You.
  • "Happy employees are productive": Does happiness → productivity? Or productivity → happiness (feeling competent)? Or both? Direction matters for intervention: invest in happiness programs vs invest in removing productivity blockers.
  • "Users who see more ads buy more": Ads → purchase? Or high-intent users (who would buy anyway) browse more pages → see more ads? The DIRECTION determines whether ad spend is working or wasted.
  • "Support tickets correlate with churn": Bad product → tickets → churn? Or: engaged users file tickets (and DON'T churn), while silent users just leave? Direction: tickets might PREVENT churn (by resolving issues) not cause it.

The rule: Correlation is SYMMETRIC (A↔B looks the same as B↔A in data). Causation is DIRECTIONAL. You cannot determine direction from correlation alone — need: temporal ordering (cause precedes effect), intervention (change one, observe other), or domain knowledge (mechanism).

7. How to Distinguish (Experiments & Natural Experiments)

Methods for Establishing Causation
  • RCT / A/B test: Random assignment eliminates confounders. Gold standard. But: not always possible (can't randomize diseases, can't randomize at scale).
  • Instrumental variables: Find something that affects A but ONLY affects B through A. Example: distance to college → education → income. Distance affects income ONLY through education.
  • Regression discontinuity: Sharp threshold creates natural experiment. Students scoring 69 vs 71 on entrance exam: essentially identical but different treatment (admitted vs not). Compare outcomes around the threshold.
  • Difference-in-differences: Compare change in treatment group vs change in control group over time. Before/after difference minus the counterfactual change.
  • Granger causality (weak): Does past A predict future B (beyond B's own history)? Temporal precedence. NOT true causation — just predictive precedence.

Practical hierarchy: RCT > natural experiment > instrumental variables > difference-in-differences > observational with controls > raw correlation. Each step down = less confidence in causal interpretation.

8. Practical Decision Guide: When Does It Matter?

Prediction vs Intervention: Different Requirements
  • For PREDICTION only: Correlation is sufficient. If shoe size predicts math ability → useful for prediction. Don't need to know WHY. Model can use confounded features without harm.
  • For INTERVENTION/ACTION: Causation is required. "Should we do X to achieve Y?" requires knowing X→Y, not just X↔Y. Acting on correlation = potentially wasting money on something that doesn't actually work.
  • For EXPLANATION: Causation required. "Why did retention drop?" needs causal model. Correlation shows WHAT moved together but not WHAT TO FIX.

The expensive mistake: "Feature X correlates with retention → let's make everyone use Feature X." But if the correlation is non-causal (engaged users find X, not X makes users engaged): forcing X on everyone = bad UX + no retention improvement + wasted engineering. This mistake costs companies millions/year.

When you NEED causation but can't run an RCT: Use multiple lines of evidence. Does the relationship hold across subgroups? Is there a dose-response (more A → proportionally more B)? Is there temporal ordering (A precedes B)? Is the mechanism plausible? Do natural experiments agree? No single method proves causation from observational data — but convergent evidence from multiple methods makes it highly probable.

The one-sentence summary: Co-occurrence = "both present." Correlation = "move together." Causation = "one drives the other." For prediction: correlation works. For decisions about WHAT TO DO: only causation is safe. Everything else is a guess with confidence — and confident guesses are the most dangerous kind.
