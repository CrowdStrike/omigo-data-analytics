BM25

BM25 ranks search results with three plain rules — rare words count more, repeated words help less and less, and long documents don't get to win just by being long

Searching a Recipe Box for "Lemon Tart"

core idearare wordsword weight
  • The site — a recipe website holds 1,000 recipes and someone types "lemon tart" into its search box
  • Common is cheap — "the" appears in 990 recipes, so matching it says nothing; its weight is 0.01
  • Rare is loud — "lemon" is in 100 recipes (weight 2.30) but "tart" is in only 20 (weight 3.89)
  • The rule — a word's weight grows as the share of documents containing it shrinks
  • Rarest of all — "meringue" sits in just 5 recipes, earning weight 5.20, the loudest vote here
  • The score — BM25 adds up, per query word, its rarity weight times a tamed repeat count

A recipe matching only "tart" (3.89) outranks one matching only "lemon" (2.30) — the rarer word is the better clue to what the searcher wants.

Key point: BM25's first ingredient is rarity: matching a word found in 20 recipes tells you far more than matching one found in 990.

Scoring Three Recipes by Hand

worked examplehand-checkable
  • Three candidates — recipe A "Classic Lemon Tart" (120 words), B "Lemon Everything Cake" (240 words), C a short 60-word note
  • The counts — A says lemon 4× and tart 3×; B says lemon 10× and tart 0×; C says each word once
  • Tame the repeats — each tf becomes tf×2.2 / (tf + 1.2×(0.25 + 0.75×length ratio)), so repeats flatten fast
  • Recipe A — lemon part 3.9 plus tart part 6.1 gives 10.0; average length, both words present
  • Recipe C — one mention of each still earns 2.9 + 4.9 = 7.8, boosted by being half the average length
  • Recipe B — ten lemons and no tart in a double-length page score only 4.2; last place

B shouts "lemon" ten times yet loses to C's single "lemon tart" — 4.2 vs 7.8 — because B misses the rare word and pays a length penalty.

Key point: Final order A (10.0) > C (7.8) > B (4.2): covering both query words in a short document beats hammering one common word in a long one.

The Old Formula That Still Wins

where it's usedstrong baselineno training
  • Everywhere — BM25 from the 1990s is still the default ranker inside most search and log-query engines
  • The upset — on searches full of new jargon, plain BM25 often beats neural rankers trained elsewhere
  • Exact words — a searcher typing an error code or drug name wants that exact string, and BM25 lives for that
  • No training — it needs only word counts from the collection itself: no labels, no GPUs, no drift
  • Cheap and clear — every score decomposes by word, so a ranking can be explained in one sentence
  • Team player — modern systems keep BM25 for the first cut, then let a neural model re-rank its top hits

A support portal swaps in a neural searcher; queries like "error 0x80070057" get worse until BM25 is restored for exact-term matching.

Key point: Neural search shines on topics it was trained on; on unfamiliar vocabulary it can fall below the 1990s formula — which is why BM25 remains the baseline to beat.

Saying It Ten Times Doesn't Make It Ten Times Better

common mistakesaturation
  • The mistake — assuming ten mentions of "lemon" count ten times as much as one mention
  • Diminishing repeats — the 1st mention adds 1.00 to the tamed count, the 10th adds only 0.02 more
  • The ceiling — with k1 = 1.2 the tamed count can never pass 2.2, no matter how many repeats
  • Why it's there — a page saying "lemon" 50 times is about lemons, not 50 times more about lemons
  • Spam armor — keyword-stuffed pages gain almost nothing, which is why the cap survived 30 years
  • Old TF-IDF — classic TF-IDF grows without a ceiling; BM25 adds a hard cap plus length normalization

Going from 1 to 4 mentions lifts the tamed count 1.00 to 1.69, but going from 10 to 15 only moves it 1.96 to 2.04 — the curve has gone flat.

Common mistake: Reading BM25 as "count the keyword matches". Repeats saturate toward a hard cap of k1 + 1 = 2.2, so the second mention is worth far more than the tenth — presence matters, piling on doesn't.
