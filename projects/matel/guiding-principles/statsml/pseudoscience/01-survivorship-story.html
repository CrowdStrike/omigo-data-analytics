Survivorship Storytelling

Success stories without failure comparison create unfalsifiable narratives

"I Did X and Succeeded, Therefore X Works"
  • The pattern: Successful person attributes success to specific behavior. Nobody studies the 1000 people who did the SAME behavior and failed.
  • Fitness influencer: "I got ripped by eating 6 meals a day and doing my special workout." Reality: they're 22, genetically gifted (high testosterone, favorable muscle insertions, fast metabolism), and would look good doing almost ANYTHING. The "secret" is genetics they can't sell you.
  • Startup founder: "We succeeded because we pivoted 3 times and never gave up." 10,000 startups ALSO pivoted 3 times and never gave up — they're dead. Survivorship filters the story to ONLY show where persistence worked, never where it was delusional.
  • Investor: "My strategy works — 15 years of beating the market!" Survivorship bias: you never hear from the investors with the SAME strategy who went bankrupt. The ones who survive attribute success to skill; the ones who fail are silent.

Why it's pseudoscience: Observing ONE outcome and inferring a rule. Science requires: observation of successes AND failures of the same strategy, controlling for confounds (genetics, luck, timing). Without the failure comparison, any story is unfalsifiable.

The data version: "Our model uses Feature X and has high accuracy." But: you've only tested on datasets where X is available. Datasets where X failed... you don't use those. Your "feature importance" is survivorship-biased toward features that happened to work on your specific test set.
Old Buildings — "They Don't Build Them Like They Used To"
  • The claim: "Look at this 200-year-old cathedral — craftsmanship was superior back then." People genuinely believe past construction was universally better.
  • What's invisible: Thousands of buildings from the same era that collapsed, rotted, burned, or were demolished because they were poorly built. You can't visit them — they're gone.
  • The filter: Only the BEST-built structures survive 200 years. You're comparing today's average (good and bad, all still standing) against the past's top 1% (the only ones left).
  • The same bias in code: "Legacy code was better engineered." No — the well-engineered legacy code is what's still running. The poorly-engineered code crashed, was rewritten, or was abandoned. You never see it.

The general rule: Any time you compare present-all vs past-remaining, you're comparing an unfiltered set against a filtered one. The past will always look better because time IS the filter.

Music & Art — "The Golden Age Was Better"
  • The claim: "Music in the 60s/70s was so much better than today's garbage." Every generation says this about the generation before.
  • What's invisible: The 60s produced tens of thousands of terrible songs — they were simply forgotten. What survives in playlists, radio, and cultural memory is the curated top 0.1%.
  • The comparison: You're hearing today's ENTIRE output (good + bad + mediocre, all on Spotify) vs the past's greatest hits (50 years of curation removed everything except the best).
  • The mechanism: Time + forgetting = automatic quality filter. The past's average was no better — you just can't access the average anymore. Only the peaks survived.

In data science: "The classic papers were so much better." No — you only read the classic papers that survived citation filtering. Thousands of terrible papers from the same era are buried in forgotten journals, never cited, never digitized.

Mutual Funds — "Our Fund Beat the Market for 20 Years"
  • The claim: A fund advertises 20 years of market-beating returns. Looks like genuine skill.
  • What's invisible: The fund company started 50 funds 20 years ago. By chance alone, some would beat the market over any period. The ones that didn't were quietly merged or closed — their track records erased.
  • The math: Start 50 funds, each with 50% chance of beating market any given year. After 20 years, ~1 fund will have beaten the market every single year by pure coin-flip luck. That fund gets advertised. The other 49 are gone.
  • The mechanism: Fund companies kill underperformers (merging them into winners). Survivors inherit inflated track records. New investors pile in — then the fund reverts to average, because the "skill" was statistical artifact, not alpha.

The data version: Train 50 models with different random seeds. The best one on your test set is NOT the best model — it's the one that got lucky on THIS test set. Publishing only the best run's results is mutual fund survivorship bias in ML.

War — "Armor the Returning Planes"
  • The story: WWII. Military studies bullet holes on returning bombers. Holes cluster on wings and fuselage. Engineers propose: armor those areas.
  • Abraham Wald's insight: You're only seeing planes that SURVIVED. The holes show where a plane can be hit and still fly home. The places with NO holes — engines, cockpit — are where planes were hit and DIDN'T return.
  • The inversion: Armor the places with NO bullet holes. Those are the fatal zones. The surviving planes are evidence of what's survivable, not what's dangerous.
  • Why this is the canonical example: It perfectly illustrates the core mechanism — analyzing only what came back and ignoring what's missing from your dataset.

In A/B testing: "Users who saw the new feature and stayed have high satisfaction scores!" But: users who saw it and LEFT aren't in your satisfaction survey. Your sample is survivor-biased. The feature may be driving away the users who would have given low scores — making the remaining scores look artificially high.

Medicine — "My Grandfather Smoked and Lived to 95"
  • The claim: Anecdotal evidence against medical statistics. "Smoking can't be that bad — my grandfather did it for 70 years and was fine."
  • What's invisible: The grandfathers (and grandmothers) who smoked and died at 55, 60, 65 of lung cancer, COPD, heart disease. They're not here to be counterexamples. They died. That's the point.
  • The math: Smoking increases mortality risk by ~2-3×. It doesn't guarantee death at 60 — it shifts the distribution. Some outliers survive. THOSE outliers are the ones alive to tell the story. The dead majority is the invisible data.
  • The mechanism: You can only hear anecdotes from people who survived. Dead people don't give testimony. So all available anecdotes are survivorship-biased toward "it wasn't that bad."

In data science: "We deployed this model 2 years ago and it still works fine!" — said by teams whose models didn't catastrophically fail. The teams whose models DID fail silently aren't around to warn you. They were fired, the project was cancelled, or the company went under.

Success Mantras — "My 5 Rules for Success"
  • The pattern: Successful person distills their journey into 5 simple rules. "Wake up at 4am. Read 1 book/week. Say no to everything. Take cold showers. Journal daily." Audience copies the rules expecting the same outcome.
  • What's invisible: Millions of people following the EXACT same rules who are still broke, still stuck, still struggling. The rules didn't cause the success — they were co-present with it. The success made the rules publishable.
  • The reverse engineering problem: The successful person examines their own history AFTER winning and identifies habits. But they can't know which habits mattered vs which were irrelevant. Cold showers didn't make them a billionaire — being in the right market at the right time with the right product did. But "be lucky" isn't a monetizable mantra.
  • The industry: Self-help is a $13B/year business built entirely on survivorship storytelling. Every book is "here's what I did" without "here's the 10,000 who did the same and failed." It's not advice — it's autobiography mistaken for science.
  • The unfalsifiability: If you follow the 5 rules and fail, the response is always: "You didn't follow them hard enough" or "You need to be more consistent." The mantra can never be wrong — only the practitioner can.

The test that would disprove it: Take 10,000 random people. Half follow the "5 rules" exactly. Half don't. Measure outcomes after 5 years. If the rules have no effect beyond placebo → survivorship storytelling, not a success system. Nobody runs this test because nobody selling a mantra wants it falsified.
