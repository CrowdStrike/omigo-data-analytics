Build a Solution Without Understanding What Problem Segments You're Actually Solving

Build a Solution Without Understanding What Problem Segments You're Actually Solving
  • AI without problem decomposition: "We'll use AI to solve fraud!" Which fraud? Card-not-present (40% of cases)? Account takeover (30%)? Friendly fraud (20%)? Bot attacks (10%)? You train a model. It picks up the easiest 40% (card-not-present, clearest signal). You report "60% detection rate!" — but you never MAPPED the problem space. When bot attacks increase from 10% to 35% of fraud (distribution shift), your detection collapses to 30%. You didn't know you were only solving one sub-problem.
  • Tech-first, problem-second: "We have a recommendation engine!" What does it recommend? For whom? In what context? It works because TODAY's users are tech-savvy early adopters browsing from desktop. Six months later: mobile-first users from a new market. Different behavior, different needs. The "AI" breaks because you never understood which user segment it actually served — you just saw a green metric and shipped.
  • The symptom: Metric looks great at launch → slowly degrades over months → team is confused ("nothing changed!"). But something DID change: the DISTRIBUTION of problems shifted. As customers ADOPT the product, new customers change the underlying data. An enterprise customer can be 100× the volume of a small one — one new enterprise onboarding shifts your entire distribution overnight. The system was always only solving PART of the problem — you just never quantified which part.
  • Customer adoption shifts distribution: You build for early adopters (tech-savvy, forgiving, small). Product-market fit achieved! Growth kicks in. Now enterprise customers arrive — different scale, different data patterns, different failure modes. One enterprise customer generates more data than your first 500 small customers combined. Your model was trained on small-customer distribution. It breaks silently under enterprise patterns because you never segmented who you built for.
  • Recommendation systems: "Our model drives 15% of revenue!" True — for power users who browse 50+ items/session. But the user base is shifting to mobile (3-5 items viewed). The model has zero signal for short sessions. As mobile grows from 30% to 70% of traffic, your "15% of revenue" drops to 5% and nobody understands why. Because nobody segmented WHERE the value came from.
  • The correct approach: BEFORE building: decompose the problem into segments. What % is each segment? Which segments does your approach theoretically handle? Which are out of scope? What's the distribution TODAY vs where it's trending? What happens when one large customer changes the mix? Then you KNOW: "we solve segments A and B (55% of current volume), not C and D. If C grows, or if one enterprise customer dominates, we need a different approach." That's engineering. Building without this is hoping.

Why it's pseudoscience: Building without quantifying the problem space is running an experiment with no hypothesis. You can't LEARN from success or failure because you don't know WHAT you solved. When it breaks, you can't diagnose WHERE it broke. It's not engineering — it's throwing technology at an unexamined problem and celebrating when the metric happens to be green. The "AI" is a black box operating on a problem you never decomposed, solving an unknown subset, vulnerable to any distribution shift you can't predict because you never mapped the territory.

The mechanism: Quantifying the problem is HARD and unglamorous. It requires data analysis, segmentation, admitting scope limits. Saying "we use AI to detect fraud" is simple, impressive, fundable. Saying "we detect 2 of 5 fraud types comprising 40% of current volume" is honest but less inspiring. The incentive is to skip the hard part (understanding) and jump to the exciting part (building). The bill comes due 6 months later when the distribution shifts.
