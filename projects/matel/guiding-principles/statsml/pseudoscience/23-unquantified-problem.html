Unquantified Problem Solving

Building a Solution Without Understanding What Problem Segments You're Actually Solving

The Pattern
  • AI without problem decomposition: "We'll use AI to solve fraud!" Which fraud? Card-not-present (40%)? Account takeover (30%)? Friendly fraud (20%)? Bot attacks (10%)? The model picks up the easiest segment. You report "60% detection rate!" but never mapped the problem space.
  • Tech-first, problem-second: "We have a recommendation engine!" For whom? In what context? It works because today's users are desktop power users. Six months later: mobile-first users from a new market. Different behavior, different needs. The system breaks because you never quantified which segment it served.
  • Customer adoption shifts distribution: You build for early adopters (tech-savvy, small). Growth kicks in. Enterprise customers arrive — one generates more data than your first 500 combined. Your model was trained on small-customer distribution. It breaks silently under enterprise patterns.
The Mechanism: Distribution Shift Invisible Without a Map
  • The symptom: metric looks great at launch → slowly degrades over months → team is confused ("nothing changed!"). But the DISTRIBUTION of problems shifted.
  • Recommendation systems: "Our model drives 15% of revenue!" True — for power users (50+ items/session). Mobile grows from 30% to 70% of traffic (3-5 items viewed). Model has zero signal for short sessions. Revenue attribution drops to 5%.
  • One large customer changes the mix: An enterprise onboarding shifts your entire distribution overnight. A system that was solving 70% of the problem is now solving 25%.
The Correct Approach
  • BEFORE building: decompose the problem into segments. What % is each segment? Which does your approach theoretically handle? Which are out of scope?
  • Track distribution: what's the distribution TODAY vs where it's trending? What happens when one large customer changes the mix?
  • State scope: "We solve segments A and B (55% of current volume), not C and D. If C grows, we need a different approach." That's engineering. Building without this is hoping.

Why it's pseudoscience: Building without quantifying the problem space is running an experiment with no hypothesis. You can't learn from success or failure because you don't know what you solved. When it breaks, you can't diagnose where. It's throwing technology at an unexamined problem and celebrating when the metric happens to be green.

The Incentive
  • Quantifying is hard and unglamorous: requires data analysis, segmentation, admitting scope limits.
  • "We use AI to detect fraud" is simple, impressive, fundable.
  • "We detect 2 of 5 fraud types comprising 40% of current volume" is honest but less inspiring.
  • The incentive is to skip understanding and jump to building. The bill comes due 6 months later when the distribution shifts.
