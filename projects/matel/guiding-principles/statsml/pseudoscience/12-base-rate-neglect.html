Ignoring How Rare/Common Something Is Before Interpreting a Test
  • Medical screening: "Test is 99% accurate! Patient tested positive!" But: if the disease affects 1 in 10,000 people, even a 99% accurate test gives ~99% FALSE positives. 99% of positives are healthy people. The "99% accurate" feels definitive; the base rate makes it nearly meaningless.
  • Fraud detection: "Our model catches 95% of fraud!" But: fraud is 0.1% of transactions. At 5% false positive rate on 99.9% legitimate transactions → 50× more false alarms than real catches. The "95% detection rate" headline hides the flood of false positives that makes it unusable.
  • Hiring: "Our AI screening tool identifies top performers with 80% accuracy!" But: only 5% of applicants are top performers. At 20% false positive rate on 95% non-top → you reject most of the top performers and promote many average ones. The base rate dominates the result.
  • Security alerts: "This threat detector has only 1% false positive rate!" With 10 million events/day → 100,000 false alerts/day. The "1% FP rate" sounds great; the base rate of non-threats makes it a noise cannon.

Why it's pseudoscience: Reporting test accuracy without base rate is MEANINGLESS. P(disease|positive test) depends on P(disease) — Bayes' theorem, not optional. Ignoring it leads to wildly wrong conclusions every time. A "99% accurate" test on a rare event is almost always wrong when it fires.
