Survivorship Bias: The Failures Left No Forwarding Address

Every trait the winners share is also a trait the losers had — you just never met the losers

The coffee shops that are still open

core ideahidden filterinvisible failures
  • The cohort — two hundred coffee shops opened in one city in the same year.
  • What is left — five years on, fifty-three still trade and the rest have shut.
  • The shared trait — most surviving shops open their doors before seven.
  • The tempting recipe — open early and you will last, since survivors do it.
  • The missing group — the shops that shut opened early at the same rate.
  • Why they vanish — a closed shop has no website and nobody left to ask.
  • The truth here — opening early was set independently of who survived.
  • The lesson — a survivor habit is news only if the failures lacked it.

Of two hundred shops, fifty-three still trade and thirty-seven of those open early — and one hundred three of the one hundred forty-seven that shut did as well.

Key point: A trait counts as evidence only when you can also check its rate in the group that disappeared.

Illustrative Example — cohort generated from a fixed seed.

Fund tables that only list the funds still open

core ideavisible averagewho exits
  • The performance table — one page listing every fund a firm runs and its return.
  • The quiet edit — funds that were closed or merged away drop off the page.
  • Who exits — the funds that get closed are the ones that were doing badly.
  • The visible average — the funds still listed average close to nine a year.
  • The honest average — counting every fund launched, the average is under eight.
  • The size of the flattery — the page reads about a point a year too kindly.
  • The same shape elsewhere — a survey mailed only to customers who stayed.
  • The tell — the number of rows is smaller than the number that entered.

Vendor A's brochure lists twenty-two funds; eighteen more were launched alongside them and were closed before the brochure went to print.

Key point: When a list holds only what is still here, its average describes the exit rule rather than the performance.

Illustrative Example — forty funds generated from a fixed seed, closure chance falling as return rises.

The vanished funds put nearly a point a year on the record

documented casehonest rateoverstatement
  • The database — commercial fund databases carry the funds that still exist.
  • The measured effect — dropping dead funds lifts the average nearly a point.
  • The study — Elton, Gruber and Blake examined this directly in 1996.
  • Independent agreement — Malkiel reported the same size a year earlier.
  • Why it is that large — a fund is closed precisely because it had lagged.
  • What compounding does — over twenty years the flattered rate pulls far ahead.
  • Practical effect — a savings plan on the survivor rate quietly overshoots.
  • The fix — use a database that keeps dead funds and their final returns.

Ten thousand compounding for twenty years at the survivor rate ends about ten thousand ahead of the same stake at the rate the whole cohort actually earned.

Key point: Elton, Gruber & Blake (1996) and Malkiel (1995) both place the survivor-only overstatement near nine tenths of a percentage point a year.

Elton, Gruber & Blake (1996), Journal of Business; Malkiel (1995), Journal of Finance. Growth figures are plain arithmetic on those two rates.

When the disappearance is blind to the outcome

boundaryharmless attritionthe fix
  • The condition — the bias needs the leaving to be linked to what you measure.
  • Harmless case — sixty entrants, half dropped by a flip that ignores score.
  • What happens — the survivors' average lands a fraction from the full one.
  • Harmful case — the same sixty, but everyone below the middle is dropped.
  • What happens — the survivors' average jumps by more than fourteen points.
  • Fix one — fix the denominator at entry rather than at reporting time.
  • Fix two — keep a permanent record of everything that ever entered.
  • Fix three — print the cohort's starting count beside every average.

Both panels start from the same sixty entrants averaging 47.2; blind dropout leaves 47.4, while score-linked dropout leaves 61.5.

Key point: Attrition only distorts an average when it correlates with the outcome — so always ask what decided who left.

Illustrative Example — sixty entry scores generated from a fixed seed.
