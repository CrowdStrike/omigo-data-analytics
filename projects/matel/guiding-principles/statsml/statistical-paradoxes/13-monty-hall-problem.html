Monty Hall Problem

New information changes the odds — even when it feels like it shouldn't.

You're on a game show. Three doors. Behind one: a car. Behind the other two: goats. You pick Door 1. The host (who KNOWS what's behind each door) opens Door 3 — a goat. He asks: "Want to switch to Door 2?"

Most people say "doesn't matter — it's 50/50 now." They're wrong. You should ALWAYS switch. Switching wins 2/3 of the time.

Why your brain gets this wrong: When you picked Door 1, you had a 1/3 chance of being right. That means there was a 2/3 chance the car was behind Doors 2 or 3 combined. When the host opens Door 3 (which he can ALWAYS do — he knows where the goat is), that 2/3 doesn't disappear. It collapses entirely onto Door 2. The host's action gave you information. Your door is still 1/3. The other door absorbed the probability.

The key insight people miss: The host isn't opening a random door. He's forced to show you a goat. That constraint is information. If you'd picked the car (1/3 chance), he can open either goat door. If you'd picked a goat (2/3 chance), he MUST open the other goat door — leaving the car behind the remaining one. Switching captures that 2/3.

Make it obvious — 100 doors: Pick 1 door out of 100. Host opens 98 other doors, all goats. One remains. Still think it's 50/50? Your door is still 1/100. The remaining door holds all the accumulated 99/100 probability. Of COURSE you switch. The 3-door version is the same logic — just harder to feel.

The boundary — where the effect disappears: Suppose Door 3 swings open by ACCIDENT and happens to show a goat. Now it really is 50/50, and switching gains nothing. (A random opener reveals the car 1/3 of the time; condition on the lucky goat-reveals and stay vs switch each win 1/2.) The 2/3 advantage exists ONLY because the host knowingly steered around the car AND around your door. Drop either constraint — a random opener, or a knowing host who was free to open your door — and the math collapses to 1/2 vs 1/2.

Real-world pattern — ask WHO eliminated the option, and what they knew:

  • Evidence-based elimination (most common): Tests rule out cancers A and B; debugging rules out the database and the API layer. That is evidence about the eliminated options — the survivors absorb the freed probability in proportion to their priors. Suspicion concentrates, but the survivors' relative ranking does not flip.
  • Blind elimination: A job finalist withdraws; a variant dies in a failed deploy. Nobody consulted the answer, so the survivors keep their original relative odds — the committee's #1 is still #1. No option gets a mysterious boost.
  • Informed, constrained elimination (true Monty Hall): Someone who knows the answer removes an option but is not allowed to touch your pick — a rival who knows which proposal will win is asked to cut one, and politics forbid cutting yours. The option they spared absorbs the eliminated mass; your pick learns nothing.
Lesson: Elimination reshapes the survivors' odds, but HOW depends on the eliminator. Chance or evidence about the eliminated option → survivors just renormalize (a random goat-reveal makes Monty Hall exactly 50/50). Informed AND constrained → the mass flows unevenly to the options the eliminator was forbidden to remove. Ask who eliminated, what they knew, and what they weren't allowed to touch.
