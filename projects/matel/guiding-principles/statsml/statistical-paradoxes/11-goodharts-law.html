Goodhart's Law

When a measure becomes a target, it stops being a good measure.

Your team picks "time on site" as the key engagement metric. Engineers optimize for it. Six months later, time on site is up 40%. Victory?

Look closer: the team added extra confirmation screens, made navigation confusing, and buried the logout button. Users are spending more time because they're lost — not because they're engaged. Customer satisfaction dropped, but nobody tracked that.

Everywhere: Schools teach to the test (scores rise, learning doesn't). Hospitals reduce "wait times" by not admitting hard cases. Call centers optimize "calls per hour" by hanging up on complex problems. The metric improves. The thing the metric was supposed to measure gets worse.

Lesson: Always pair your target metric with a counter-metric that catches gaming. If "time on site" is the goal, track satisfaction alongside it. If one goes up and the other goes down, you optimized the number, not the outcome.
