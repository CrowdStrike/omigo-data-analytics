Regression to the Mean

Extreme measurements are partly noise — the next measurement will be closer to average.

You run 100 ad campaigns for a week to find the best ones. You pick the top 10 (15%+ click rate) and double their budget. Next week, all 10 drop to 8-11%. You blame the budget increase, the audience saturation, the algorithm.

But the real reason: to measure 15% in week 1, a campaign needed true performance PLUS favorable noise (right people online that week, lucky timing). Week 2 has the same true performance but different noise. It regresses toward the real average.

If you'd also tracked the bottom 10, you'd see them improve — same effect in reverse.

Everyday version: A manager berates an employee after a rare, terrible mistake. Next week the employee does better. The manager concludes punishment works. But the mistake was a statistical anomaly — performance would have bounced back regardless. The manager just taught themselves a false lesson.

The sequel reality: A studio's hit movie was skill + luck (perfect casting, cultural moment, timing). The sequel tries to duplicate that fluke. It drops back toward the studio's average — not because it's bad, but because it's being compared to a lucky masterpiece. Lightning rarely strikes twice.

Lesson: Never select on one measurement and expect the next to match. Require multiple measurements before declaring something extreme, or use a holdout group.
