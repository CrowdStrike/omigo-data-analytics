k-Means Clustering

You say "give me 3 groups" — the algorithm finds where 3 group centers sit and hands every customer to its nearest center

200 Customers, No Labels, Three Piles

core idearunning example
  • The data — 200 customers, each just two numbers: monthly spend and visits a month
  • No answer column — nobody labeled the segments; we want the groups to emerge
  • You choose k — we ask for k = 3 groups; the algorithm decides who goes where
  • A center per group — each group is summarized by one point, the average member
  • Membership rule — every customer simply belongs to whichever center is nearest

Like sorting a drawer of receipts into 3 piles when nobody told you what the piles are.

k-Means: pick k, then find k center points so that everyone sits close to their nearest center. The "mean" in the name is the group average that each center becomes.

One Full Round by Hand, Six Customers

worked examplearithmetic
  • Six customers — A(1,1), B(2,1), C(1,2) and D(8,8), E(9,8), F(8,9); ask for k = 2
  • Start — drop the two centers on customers B(2,1) and E(9,8); any start works
  • Assign — A and C are nearest to B's center; D and F are nearest to E's center
  • Move — each center jumps to its members' average: (1.33, 1.33) and (8.33, 8.33)
  • Repeat — reassign with the moved centers: nobody switches sides, so it stops
  • Converged — two rounds and done; that little loop is the whole algorithm

New center x = (1 + 2 + 1) ÷ 3 = 1.33 — just the average of A, B, C.

The loop: assign everyone to the nearest center, move each center to the average of its members, repeat until nothing moves. Nothing else is happening at any scale.

What the Three Segments Buy You

where it's usedpayoff
  • 200 rows become 3 stories — each segment is described by its center alone
  • Bargain regulars (85) — around $25 and 9 visits: small basket, constant presence
  • Steady mids (70) — around $75 and 5 visits: the quiet core of the business
  • Big-ticket occasionals (45) — around $140 and 2 visits: rare but heavy buyers
  • Same trick elsewhere — compressing image colors, grouping error logs, user personas

Marketing cannot write 200 different emails, but it can happily write 3.

The algorithm never names the groups: it returns coordinates, not meanings. "Bargain regulars" is your reading of center ($25, 9) — that interpretation step is on you.

You Chose k — the Algorithm Never Complains

common mistakecaution
  • Ask for 4, get 4 — k-means returns exactly k groups even if the data has 3, or none
  • No error message — a wrong k does not fail; it just slices real groups arbitrarily
  • Elbow check — plot total within-group distance for k = 1, 2, 3, ...; look for the bend
  • Here the bend is at 3 — 980 → 410 → 160, then barely improves (135, 118, ...)
  • Random starts matter — a bad start can wedge; rerun a few times (k-means++ default)

Run k = 3 on customers with no real groups at all and you still get 3 tidy "segments".

The confusion: "it returned 3 clusters" is not evidence that 3 clusters exist. The elbow plot — not the output — is where the data gets a say in k.
