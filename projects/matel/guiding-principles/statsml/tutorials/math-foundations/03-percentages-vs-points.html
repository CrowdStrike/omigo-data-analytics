Percentages vs Percentage Points

Going from 2% to 3% is one percentage point but a 50% jump — the same change has two honest names, and headlines pick the bigger-sounding one

The Checkout Change With Two Names

core idearelative vs absolute
  • The store — 2 of every 100 visitors buy; after a checkout redesign, 3 of every 100 buy
  • Points view — the rate moved 2% → 3%: a difference of 1 percentage point (3 − 2)
  • Percent view — buyers grew from 2 to 3 per hundred: a 50% increase (1 ÷ 2)
  • Both true — "up 1 point" and "up 50%" describe the exact same redesign
  • The tell — "points" subtracts the rates; "percent" divides the change by the old rate

Marketing announced "conversions up 50%!" while finance logged "+1pt" — same numbers, different drama.

Key point: A percentage point is the absolute gap between two rates; a percent change is that gap divided by where you started. Always ask which one a claim is using.

Redoing the Math With 1,000 Visitors

worked examplerate changes
  • Before — 1,000 visitors, 2% buy: 0.02 × 1,000 = 20 buyers
  • After — 1,000 visitors, 3% buy: 0.03 × 1,000 = 30 buyers
  • Point change — (30 − 20) extra buyers ÷ 1,000 visitors = +0.01 = +1 point
  • Percent change — (30 − 20) extra buyers ÷ 20 old buyers = +0.50 = +50%
  • Same 10 people — both calculations count the same 10 new buyers, against different bases

Ten extra buyers are 1% of all visitors but 50% of the old buyer count — the base is the whole story.

Key point: Points divide by everyone (1,000 visitors); percent divides by the old yes-group (20 buyers). Small yes-groups make percent changes look enormous.

Why "Risk Doubled" Can Mean Almost Nothing

where it's usedreporting
  • The headline — "fraud rate DOUBLES after new payment option" sounds like a crisis
  • The data — fraud went from 1 to 2 orders per 10,000: +100% relative, +0.01 points absolute
  • The picture — on an axis showing all 10,000 orders, both bars are practically invisible
  • Fair reporting — give both: "doubled, from 0.01% to 0.02% — 2 in 10,000 orders"
  • Same trick — drug side effects, crime stats, and A/B test wins all get inflated this way

"Doubled risk" was one extra fraudulent order per 10,000 — the fix cost more than the fraud.

Key point: Relative changes on tiny base rates produce huge percentages from trivial effects. Never accept a percent change without the absolute rates behind it.

Down 50%, Up 50%, Not Back to Start

common mistakerule of thumb
  • The slump — daily sales fall 50%: 100 coffees → 50 coffees
  • The rebound — sales then rise 50%: 50 coffees → 75 coffees, not 100
  • Why — the +50% is measured from the smaller base of 50, so it only adds back 25
  • Undo rule — reversing a −50% needs +100%; reversing −20% needs +25%
  • Points differ — percentage points do undo cleanly: −1pt then +1pt is back to start

The shop celebrated "we regained the 50% we lost" while still selling 25 fewer coffees a day.

Common mistake: Treating percent changes as addable. They multiply: −50% then +50% is ×0.5 × 1.5 = ×0.75. Chain them by multiplying, or switch to points.
