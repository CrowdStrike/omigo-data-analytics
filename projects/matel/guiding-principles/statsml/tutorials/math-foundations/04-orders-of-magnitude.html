Orders of Magnitude

Thinking in powers of ten — a five-second exponent check that catches numbers a thousand times off before they reach a dashboard

The Coffee Shop Dashboard That Read $100,000

core ideapowers of ten
  • The shop — sells about 200 cups a day at $5 each: roughly $1,000/day, which is 10³
  • The dashboard — a new revenue report shows $100,000/day for the same shop: 10⁵
  • The check — compare exponents, not digits: 5 vs 3 means the number is ~100× too big
  • The bug — prices were stored in cents; $5.00 arrived as 500 and never got divided
  • One order — a ×10 step; the cents bug is two whole steps, visible before any digging

Nobody needed the exact figure — "a small shop cannot make 10⁵ dollars a day" ended the debate in seconds.

Key point: An order of magnitude is one power of ten. Compare exponents first: if the exponent is wrong, no amount of precision in the digits can save the number.

Estimating a City's Daily Coffee, Step by Step

worked exampleestimation
  • Question — how many cups does a city of 1,000,000 people drink per day?
  • Step 1 — population: 1,000,000 = 10⁶
  • Step 2 — say half drink coffee: 10⁶ × 0.5 = 500,000 drinkers
  • Step 3 — say 1.5 cups each: 500,000 × 1.5 = 750,000 ≈ 10⁶ cups/day
  • Rounding is fine — each guess can be 2× off and the exponent barely moves

Whether the truth is 400,000 or 1,500,000 cups, "about a million, not ten thousand or a hundred million" is the useful answer.

Key point: Chain rough factors and track only the exponent. Errors in the factors partly cancel, so the final power of ten is far more reliable than any single guess.

Exponent Checks on a Data Pipeline

where it's usedsanity checksfailure mode
  • Row counts — a join expected near 10⁶ rows but produced 10⁹: a 1,000× fan-out
  • Latency — a service expected at ~10² ms logs 10⁵: seconds recorded as milliseconds
  • Revenue — expected 10⁴, got 1.3 × 10⁴: same exponent, plausibly fine
  • File size — expected 10⁷ bytes, got 9 × 10⁶: same ballpark, no alarm
  • The habit — write the expected power of ten before running anything; compare after

The join bug shipped a 10⁹-row table downstream; a one-line "expect ~10⁶ rows" check would have stopped it.

Key point: Most catastrophic data bugs — unit mix-ups, join fan-outs, double counting — move a number by whole powers of ten. Exponent checks catch exactly that class.

Ballparks: When 4× Is a Detail and 100× Is a Different Question

common mistakerule of thumb
  • Same ballpark — 2 and 8 differ by ×4 but share the 1–10 band: less than one order apart
  • Different worlds — 2 and 200 are two orders apart; no rounding argument bridges that
  • The mistake — debating the third decimal of a forecast whose exponent is unverified
  • Order first — settle the power of ten, then refine digits only if the decision needs it
  • Ten, not two — "an order of magnitude more" means ×10; twice as big does not qualify

A team argued whether a campaign would bring 4,200 or 4,600 signups — the real number came in at 40.

Common mistake: Polishing digits while the exponent is unchecked. Being 4× off is often survivable; being 100× off means the model of the problem is wrong.
