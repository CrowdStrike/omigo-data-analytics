Flight Tracking (ADS-B)

Every plane screams its location into the sky. Anyone with a $20 radio can listen.

What is it?

ADS-B stands for Automatic Dependent Surveillance-Broadcast. Every commercial plane has a transmitter. It broadcasts position, altitude, speed, and identity on 1090 MHz. The signal is unencrypted. No authentication. No opt-out.

A cheap USB dongle (RTL-SDR, about $20) plugged into a laptop picks up these signals. Websites like Flightradar24 and ADS-B Exchange crowdsource thousands of these receivers worldwide. The result: real-time tracking of nearly every aircraft on Earth.

The system was designed for safety. It was never designed for privacy. Now anyone can watch.
What does it collect?
  • ICAO address — unique ID for each aircraft, like a license plate
  • Latitude and longitude — exact position, updated every second
  • Altitude — barometric and GPS altitude
  • Ground speed and heading — how fast, which direction
  • Callsign — flight number (e.g., UAL417)
  • Vertical rate — climbing or descending
  • Squawk code — emergency codes visible to everyone
All of this broadcasts continuously. No request needed. No consent given. The plane just talks.
Why do they collect it?

What they tell you: ADS-B replaces old radar. Radar is expensive, has blind spots, and can't cover oceans. ADS-B gives air traffic control better accuracy. Planes see each other. Collisions get avoided. Search and rescue finds crashed planes faster.

What they don't tell you: The open broadcast means hobbyists, journalists, and activists track private jets. @ElonJet tracked Musk's movements in real time using public ADS-B data. Planespotters exposed CIA rendition flights by matching tail numbers to shell companies. Governments tried to suppress this. They failed. The signal is physics. You cannot un-broadcast radio waves.

The FAA offers a "privacy" program (LADD) that hides data from FAA feeds. But it cannot stop anyone with their own receiver from listening directly.
How much can they see?

A single ground receiver covers roughly 200-300 miles in every direction. At cruising altitude, planes are line-of-sight to receivers hundreds of miles away.

Crowdsourced networks (ADS-B Exchange has 30,000+ feeders) cover most of the populated world. Satellites now pick up ADS-B over oceans. There are almost no gaps left.

Historical data is stored forever. You can look up where any plane was on any date. Pattern analysis reveals: where billionaires vacation, which military aircraft flew where, how often a CEO visits a competitor's headquarters.

You can hide your phone. You can use a VPN. But if you fly private, your movements are public record broadcast at the speed of light.
