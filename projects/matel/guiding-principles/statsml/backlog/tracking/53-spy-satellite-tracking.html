Spy Satellite Orbit Tracking

Amateurs with backyard telescopes and basic math track the orbits of classified spy satellites the government says don't exist.

What is it?

Governments launch classified satellites to photograph military targets from space. The US National Reconnaissance Office (NRO) operates dozens of these. Their orbits are kept secret. They don't appear in any public catalog.

But satellites reflect sunlight. If you know where to look and when, you can see them with a small telescope or even binoculars. A global network of amateur watchers does exactly this.

Ted Molczan, a Canadian hobbyist, has tracked classified satellites since the 1980s. He and others maintain independent catalogs of objects the government refuses to acknowledge.
What do they collect?
  • Time of pass: Exact moment the satellite crosses overhead, down to fractions of a second
  • Brightness: How bright the reflection is tells you the satellite's size and shape
  • Altitude: Calculated from the pass timing and angle. Most spy satellites orbit at 250-500 km
  • Inclination: The tilt of the orbit tells you what latitudes it flies over
  • Orbital period: How long one trip around Earth takes. About 90 minutes for low orbit
  • Position predictions: Once you know the orbit, you can predict every future pass
Multiple observers across different cities see the same satellite at slightly different angles. Combining their reports gives a precise orbit within hours of launch.
Why do they track them?

What governments tell you: These satellites are classified for national security. Publishing their orbits would help adversaries hide from them. You don't need to know.

What they don't tell you: When a spy satellite suddenly changes orbit, it moves to watch something specific. If it shifts to pass over Iran, you know tensions are rising. If it moves over Ukraine, something military is happening. The orbit change is the signal.

Amateur trackers noticed US spy satellites repositioning over Iraq weeks before the 2003 invasion became public. The satellites told the story before the newspapers did.

Orbit maneuvers burn fuel. Fuel is limited. Every move is a deliberate decision by intelligence agencies. Tracking those moves reveals priorities they never intended to share.
How much can they see?

The math is straightforward. You need a stopwatch, a star chart, and a telescope. Time when the satellite passes known stars. The stars give you exact angles. The timing gives you speed. Speed plus angle gives you the full orbit.

With just three good observations, you can calculate an orbit accurate to within a few hundred meters. That is enough to predict every future pass for weeks.

The community shares observations online. One person in Texas sees a pass. Another in Australia confirms it 45 minutes later. The orbit locks in.

The US government has never successfully kept a satellite orbit secret from determined amateur watchers. Not once. The math always wins.
