Geological & Seismic Sensors

The planet is wired with listening devices. They hear everything that shakes, cracks, or explodes.

What is it?

Sensors buried in the ground, bolted to dams, dropped on the ocean floor, and drilled into boreholes. They measure vibrations. Every explosion, earthquake, landslide, and submarine engine sends waves through rock and water. These sensors catch them all.

Dam sensors detect cracks forming before they become disasters. Fault-line sensors track tectonic plates grinding against each other. Ocean-floor hydrophones listen to whale songs and submarine propellers alike.

SOSUS (Sound Surveillance System) was a Cold War network of underwater microphones built to track Soviet submarines. It now tracks whale migrations and undersea earthquakes.
What does it collect?
  • Ground motion — vibrations in three directions (up-down, north-south, east-west)
  • Acoustic waves — sound traveling through water and air (infrasound below human hearing)
  • Strain and tilt — how much rock or concrete bends under pressure
  • Radionuclides — radioactive particles in the air that confirm nuclear explosions
  • Water pressure changes — tsunami waves detected thousands of miles from shore
  • Arrival times — when waves hit different stations, revealing the source location
A seismometer records P-waves (fast, compressional) and S-waves (slow, shearing). The time gap between them tells you how far away the event happened.
Why do they collect it?

What they tell you: Earthquake early warning. Tsunami alerts. Dam safety monitoring. Volcano eruption prediction. Scientific research on Earth's interior structure.

What they don't tell you: The same network detects unauthorized nuclear tests anywhere on Earth. Hydrophone arrays built for whale research track submarine movements. CTBTO's 337 stations can pinpoint a one-kiloton explosion underground, underwater, or in the atmosphere within hours. Every nation's secret weapons tests are heard by everyone else.

Seismometers distinguish earthquakes from nuclear tests by waveform shape. Earthquakes start with a slow build. Explosions hit hard and fast with a sharp spike.
How much can they see?

CTBTO (Comprehensive Nuclear Test Ban Treaty Organization) runs 337 monitoring stations across the globe. They use four technologies: seismic, hydroacoustic, infrasound, and radionuclide detection.

Three stations can triangulate any event on Earth. The network detects explosions as small as one kiloton. It caught every North Korean nuclear test within minutes.

Hydrophone arrays hear across entire ocean basins. A whale call in the Antarctic reaches sensors near Alaska. A submarine in the Pacific cannot hide from the combined network.

The entire planet is under continuous acoustic surveillance. No explosion above one kiloton goes undetected. The data is shared among 180+ member nations.
