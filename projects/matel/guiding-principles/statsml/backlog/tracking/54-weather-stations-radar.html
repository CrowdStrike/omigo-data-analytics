Weather Stations & Radar

Ground stations measure your local sky. Doppler radar sees storms hundreds of miles away. Together they watch more than just the weather.

What is it?

A layered network of sensors that tracks what the atmosphere is doing. Ground stations sit on the surface and measure conditions right where they are. Doppler radar shoots microwave pulses into the sky and listens for echoes bouncing off raindrops, hail, and even insects.

The US runs about 160 NEXRAD Doppler radars. They spin continuously, scanning the sky in a full circle every few minutes. Personal weather stations from companies like Davis and WeatherFlow add millions more data points from backyards and rooftops.
What does it collect?
  • Temperature — air temp at ground level, updated every few seconds
  • Humidity — how much moisture is in the air
  • Wind speed and direction — gusts, sustained speed, shifts
  • Barometric pressure — rising or falling tells you what is coming
  • Precipitation — rain rate, snow accumulation, hail size
  • Radar reflectivity — how dense the rain or debris is at each altitude
  • Doppler velocity — whether rain is moving toward or away from the radar (reveals rotation in tornadoes)
  • Lightning strikes — location, polarity, strength of each bolt
Why do they collect it?

What they tell you: Severe weather warnings. Tornado alerts. Flood predictions. Flight safety. Farmers need frost warnings. Cities need snowplow timing. Insurance companies need hail maps.

What they don't tell you: Lightning detection networks like Vaisala can spot missile launches. Rockets produce a unique electromagnetic signature that looks nothing like natural lightning. The same sensors that warn you about thunderstorms can detect ballistic missile launches within seconds.

Doppler radar picks up bird migrations and insect swarms. That data goes to ecologists but also to military planners. Weather data feeds agriculture futures trading — hedge funds buy early access to forecasts to trade corn and wheat before farmers even check the sky.

The same lightning sensor that tells your phone a storm is 10 miles away can detect an ICBM launch on the other side of the planet.
How much can they see?

A single NEXRAD radar covers about 250 miles in every direction. The full US network has no gaps — every square mile is under at least one radar. Most areas are covered by two or three overlapping radars.

Lightning networks are global. Vaisala detects every stroke anywhere on Earth with location accuracy within a few hundred meters. Personal weather stations now number in the hundreds of thousands in the US alone, creating a mesh denser than any government network.

Radar updates every 4-6 minutes. Lightning data arrives in under a second. Ground stations report every 1-5 seconds. There is no gap in time or space.
