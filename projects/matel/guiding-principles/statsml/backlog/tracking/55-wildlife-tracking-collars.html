Wildlife Tracking Collars

GPS collars on endangered animals generate conservation data that also exposes poaching routes and illegal land clearing.

What is it?

Scientists attach GPS collars, tags, or fin-mounted transmitters to wolves, elephants, and sharks. The devices ping satellites every few minutes. Each ping records the animal's location. The data goes into databases like Movebank, where researchers worldwide can study animal movement.

The collars run on solar or battery power. They last months to years depending on the species. Some fall off on a timer. Others stay on for the animal's lifetime.
What does it collect?
  • GPS coordinates — latitude, longitude every 15 minutes to 4 hours
  • Movement speed — how fast the animal travels between pings
  • Heart rate — stress levels, activity state (resting, running, feeding)
  • Body temperature — health status, environmental exposure
  • Altitude/depth — elevation for land animals, dive depth for sharks
  • Proximity alerts — when collared animals meet each other or approach human zones
  • Accelerometer data — walking, swimming, fighting, mating behavior
Why do they collect it?

What they tell you: Track migration routes. Study habitat use. Monitor population health. Understand predator-prey relationships. Inform conservation policy.

What they don't tell you: When a collared elephant suddenly stops moving, rangers get an alert. That often means poaching. The GPS trail leads straight to the kill site. Over time, the data reveals poaching corridors — paths that hunters use repeatedly. Animals that suddenly avoid an area they used to cross? That reveals illegal deforestation before satellite images catch it. Shark tags show encounters with fishing vessels in protected zones. Wolf pack GPS data gets used in livestock conflict lawsuits.

Conservation data becomes law enforcement data. The same GPS trail that maps migration also maps crime.
How much can they see?

Every step, every rest, every interaction. They know where the animal sleeps, drinks, feeds, and breeds. They know which animals are social and which are loners. They can predict where an animal will be next Tuesday.

Combined across species, the data shows entire ecosystem maps. Migration corridors. Water sources. Seasonal patterns. Conflict zones between wildlife and humans.

One collared wolf reveals the pack. One tagged shark reveals the fishing fleet. One elephant reveals the poaching network.
