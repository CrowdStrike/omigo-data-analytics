20. License Plate Readers (Flock/ALPR)

Small cameras on residential streets that log every car passing by — no video, just data.

What is it?

Small solar-powered cameras mounted on poles in neighborhoods, parking lots, and side streets. Companies like Flock Safety sell them to HOAs, police departments, and businesses. They don't record video. They capture a snapshot of each passing vehicle, extract the plate and vehicle details, then discard the image. What remains is pure searchable data.

"No video retained" sounds privacy-friendly. But the extracted data — plate, time, place, vehicle description — is far more useful for surveillance than video ever was.
What does it collect?
  • License plate number
  • Timestamp (date and time to the second)
  • GPS location of the reader
  • Vehicle type (sedan, SUV, truck, motorcycle)
  • Vehicle color
  • Make and model (from shape recognition)
  • Direction of travel
  • Bumper stickers, roof racks, damage (distinguishing features)
Why do they collect it?

What they tell you: Neighborhood safety. Catch package thieves. Find stolen cars. Help police solve crimes faster.

What they don't tell you: Police can search weeks of history across entire cities. They run "be on the lookout" alerts for any plate. HOAs can see who visits which house and when. The data feeds into fusion centers that combine it with other surveillance. No warrant needed — it is purchased, not seized.
How much can they see?

A single Flock camera reads about 1,500 plates per day. A city with 200 cameras logs 300,000 plate sightings daily. Over 30 days, that is 9 million data points. Police can type any plate and see everywhere it has been. They can also search by description — "red pickup truck seen on Oak Street between 2-4 AM" — even without a plate number.

Your neighbor's doorbell camera watches their porch. An ALPR watches the entire street, 24/7, and remembers for weeks.
