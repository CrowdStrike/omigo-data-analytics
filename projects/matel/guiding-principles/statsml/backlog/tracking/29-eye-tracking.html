Eye Tracking

Cameras follow exactly where your eyes look — which ad caught your attention, which product you stared at, what you skipped over.

What is it?

A camera watches your eyes and calculates exactly where you're looking on a screen or in a room. Meta Quest VR headsets do this. Some laptops and monitors have it built in. Research labs use it to test ads. Retail stores are experimenting with shelf-level eye tracking. It knows what grabbed your attention and what you ignored.
What does it collect?
  • Exact point of gaze (where you're looking, pixel-level)
  • How long you stare at each thing (dwell time)
  • Pupil dilation (reveals excitement or interest)
  • Reading patterns (did you actually read the terms?)
  • What you skipped or scrolled past
  • Eye movement speed (saccades reveal cognitive load)
Why do they collect it?

What they tell you: Better VR experiences. Accessibility features. Helping disabled people use computers with their eyes.

What they don't tell you: Advertisers pay to know which billboard you actually looked at. VR companies map your interests by what you stare at in virtual worlds. Pupil dilation reveals emotional reactions they can exploit for pricing.
How much can they see?

They see your attention as a physical reality — not what you clicked, but what you actually looked at. They know you glanced at a competitor's product. They know you re-read the price three times (price-sensitive). They know if you looked at someone's body. In VR, this data is constant and inescapable.

Intrusiveness: Very High (in VR), Medium (desktop/retail). Maps your inner attention. Reveals thoughts you never expressed.
