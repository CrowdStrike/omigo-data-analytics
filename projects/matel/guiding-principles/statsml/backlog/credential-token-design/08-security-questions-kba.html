Security Questions & KBA

The knowledge factor that outsourced its secret to your biography — and what happened when biographies became searchable.

Premise: the design rests on answers being private, stable, and memorable — all three failed in the field.

1. The static Q&A design

A knowledge factor captured at enrollment and replayed at recovery.

  • Enrollment: the user picks canonical questions and records answers
  • Recovery: matching answers stand in for the password
  • Assumed private: only you know the answers
  • Assumed stable: the same answer holds years later
  • Assumed memorable: no need to write answers down
  • Low entropy: a few common answers cover most users
  • Tiny space: the answer space is a fraction of a password's
  • Researchable: the questions ask facts people publish
  • Public secret: the answer often sits on a public profile
  • Shared: the same life facts unlock every site
  • Unrotatable: a leaked answer can never be changed
  • Fixed history: your biography does not rotate

2. Failure analysis

  • Reused password: the same answer works on every site that asks
  • No reset: no reset button exists for a mother's maiden name
  • Social-media era: the canonical questions became lookup-able
  • The canon: first pet, mother's maiden name, first school
  • Published lives: people publish the facts the questions ask
  • Attacker's job: shifted from guessing to reading
  • Honest answers: truth concentrates in a tiny answer space
  • Strictly worse: honesty loses to answering randomly
  • Random answers: restore entropy but guarantee forgetting
  • Self-defeating: recovery fails at the moment it exists for
Dilemma: honest answers are guessable and researchable, random answers are forgettable — no setting of the knob makes static Q&A work well.

3. Dynamic KBA (credit-file style)

Dynamic KBA generates its questions on the fly instead of storing them.

  • On-the-fly: questions are built from third-party records
  • Record sources: past addresses, loan figures, vehicles
  • Nothing enrolled: no stored answers leak from the service
  • Stronger bar: the attacker must hold a copy of your records
  • Higher cost: the job shifts from research to data acquisition
  • Broker errors: honest users fail on wrong or stale records
  • No telling apart: a confused owner looks like an attacker
  • Locale-limited: needs locales with deep consumer data files
  • Hard borders: coverage stops where the data files stop
  • Breach decay: every broker or credit-file leak weakens it
  • Knowledge shift: "only you know" becomes "the attacker knows"
  • Permanent: breached records never rotate back
Outsourced secret: every breach of the third party's database decays a strength the user cannot restore — the unrotatable-secret failure, one level removed.

4. Regional prevalence and decline

  • Once mandatory: major webmail and banking providers required them
  • Standard path: for years the default recovery route
  • Peak scale: among the most widely deployed credentials ever
  • Still lingering: some banking and government flows keep them
  • Regional: some regions retain static Q&A or credit-file KBA
  • One layer: now one gate among several, rarely the sole gate
  • The replacement: a code sent to a channel you already control
  • Possession proof: control of a device or mailbox
  • Why it won: stronger and easier than proving a memory
  • Modern guidance: knowledge-based recovery is discouraged outright
  • New systems: mostly never build it at all
  • Legacy tail: the remaining installations are holdovers
  • Two-decade retreat: the retirement has run for twenty years
Illustrative Example: Alice enrolled honest answers years ago and has not thought about them since; today the same facts appear across her public profiles, so her "secret" expired without her doing anything at all.
