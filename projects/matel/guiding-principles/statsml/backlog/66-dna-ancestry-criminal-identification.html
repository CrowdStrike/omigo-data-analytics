DNA Ancestry → Criminal Identification CASE STUDY

Investigative genetic genealogy (IGG) — uploading crime-scene DNA to consumer ancestry databases, finding distant relatives, and narrowing to a suspect through family-tree reconstruction.

The Method

A suspect never submitted their own DNA. But a third cousin did — to a consumer ancestry service. That partial match, combined with public genealogy records, narrows millions of candidates to one person.
  • Crime-scene DNA is sequenced and uploaded to a public genealogy database (e.g., GEDmatch, FamilyTreeDNA)
  • The system returns partial matches — not the suspect, but distant relatives (3rd–4th cousins share ~0.8% DNA)
  • Investigators build a reverse family tree from each matched relative back several generations
  • Trees converge on a small set of living descendants in the right age/geography/sex
  • Suspect's identity is confirmed with a direct DNA sample (discarded cup, door handle, trash)

Why This Is a Data Science Problem

AspectWhat Makes It Interesting
Entity resolution at scaleMatching partial genetic markers across millions of profiles — probabilistic, not deterministic
Graph traversalBuilding family trees is graph search — nodes are people, edges are parent-child relationships
Shrinking candidate setEach matched relative adds a constraint — geography, age, sex — exponentially reducing candidates
Consent propagationOne person's decision to submit DNA exposes hundreds of relatives who never consented
Coverage thresholdResearchers estimate that once ~2% of a population is in a database, nearly everyone is identifiable through relatives

The Consent Paradox

  • The suspect never submitted DNA — a relative did, years earlier, for ancestry curiosity
  • DNA is shared biology — you cannot opt out of what your relatives share
  • 3rd cousins: most people don't even know who they are, yet they create identifiability
  • The database doesn't need YOU — it needs enough of your extended family

Discussion Points

  • Population coverage math: How many submissions does it take for a database to cover 90% of a population via familial links?
  • False positive risk: Partial DNA matches have error rates — what happens when the family tree points to the wrong branch?
  • Cold case success rate: IGG has solved cases decades old — what's the selection bias in reported successes vs. dead ends?
  • Policy tension: Consumer databases changing terms of service after the technique became public — GEDmatch now requires opt-in for law enforcement
  • Asymmetric privacy: Your genetic privacy depends on decisions made by relatives you may never meet
  • Precedent expansion: Initially reserved for violent crimes (murder, rape) — where does the boundary settle?

The Statistical Angle

This is probabilistic identification, not deterministic. Each familial match provides a likelihood ratio, not a certainty. The family tree narrows candidates, but the final confirmation always requires a direct sample. The question is: at what point does the probabilistic narrowing become a de facto identification — and who oversees the intermediate steps?
