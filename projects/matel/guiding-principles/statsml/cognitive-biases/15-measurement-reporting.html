Measurement & Reporting Biases

How the act of measuring, recording, and publishing data systematically distorts what we think we know.

1. Recall Bias

People who experienced an outcome remember exposures differently than those who did not. The outcome motivates a more thorough search of memory.

  • Mothers of babies with birth defects recall more medication use during pregnancy
  • Patients with cancer recall more chemical exposure
  • Pharmacy records show no actual difference in usage
Impact: Creates spurious associations in case-control studies. Objective records (pharmacy logs, medical charts) are the antidote.

Example: Cases recall 45% medication use; controls recall 20%. Pharmacy records show both groups at 25%.

2. Observer / Measurement Bias

The act of measuring changes what is being measured. An unblinded observer unconsciously shifts their assessment toward the expected result.

  • Doctor in treatment group looks harder for improvement
  • Interviewer nods more with hypothesized answers
  • Subjective endpoints are most vulnerable
Impact: Double-blinding eliminates this. Without it, effect sizes inflate by 15-25% on average in clinical trials.

Example: Unblinded pain assessment shows 40% improvement; blinded assessment shows 22%.

3. Early Termination Bias

Stopping a trial when interim results look good inflates the observed effect size by 30-50%. Random noise is highest at small n; stopping at a peak locks in the noise.

  • Trial at n=200: observed effect +40%
  • Full enrollment (n=1000): true effect +12%
  • The earlier you stop, the more inflated the estimate
Impact: Pre-registered stopping rules with alpha-spending functions (O'Brien-Fleming) control this. Ad-hoc stopping is a red flag.

Illustration (hypothetical): consider a trial stopped at n=200 showing +40% benefit; run to full enrollment (n=1000), the effect settles at +12%.

4. Publication / Reporting Bias

Negative or null results rarely get published. If 20 teams run the same experiment, 19 find nothing (file drawer), and 1 finds p < 0.05 by chance—only that one publishes.

  • The literature shows 95% positive results
  • Reality: only 5% of attempts succeeded
  • Meta-analyses inherit the distortion
Impact: Pre-registration (ClinicalTrials.gov, OSF) and registered reports combat this. Funnel plots detect it in meta-analysis.

Example: 20 teams test a supplement. 1 finds p=0.03 (by chance at alpha=0.05). Only that paper is published.

5. Information Bias (Misclassification)

When labels are noisy, model performance has a hard ceiling below 100%. If 20% of "diabetic" patients don't actually have diabetes, no model can exceed 80% accuracy on those cases.

  • Non-differential: random noise in both directions
  • Differential: noise correlated with exposure/outcome
  • Label noise of X% creates ceiling at (100-X)%
Impact: Audit a sample of labels before modeling. If label noise is 20%, your "90% accurate" model is actually performing above the theoretical ceiling—meaning it's overfitting to noise.

Example: 1000 patients labeled "diabetic". 200 are misclassified. Model ceiling = 80% on this cohort.
