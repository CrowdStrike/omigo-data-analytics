Overconfidence in Small Samples

Premature conclusions from early results before the signal stabilizes

The Trap

Treating early results like the final answer. Your brain locks onto the first few data points and refuses to wait for enough evidence to actually know.

  • A/B test running 2 hours. 47 treatment, 39 control — +22%
  • PM: "Can we call it?"
  • CI is [-15%, +60%] — includes "treatment is harmful" as a real possibility
  • The number looks large, so the urge to stop early is strong

Example: New employee resolves a critical bug in week 1 — strong first impression. By week 8: missed 3 deadlines, caused 2 incidents. But the n=1 first observation anchored the narrative permanently.

Defense: Set sample size requirement BEFORE you start and don't peek. Early data is a movie trailer — exciting, misleading, and not the actual film.

Where It Strikes

Small-sample overconfidence isn't limited to statistics. It invades every domain where humans encounter sequential data and feel pressure to act.

  • The pattern: early signal looks decisive, action is taken, reality catches up later
  • The cost: reverted decisions, wasted resources, eroded trust in data
  • The cause: variance dominates at small n, but our intuition treats every observation as equally informative
Notice: In every case below, the early conclusion felt certain. Confidence was high precisely because there wasn't enough data to reveal the noise.

The Funnel

Early data is high-variance data. The confidence interval at small n covers almost the entire plausible range — it tells you almost nothing.

  • n=5: CI width ~80% — could be anything
  • n=10: CI width ~56% — still enormous
  • n=25: CI width ~36% — vaguely directional
  • n=50: CI width ~25% — starting to narrow
  • n=100: CI width ~18% — actionable range
  • n=500: CI width ~8% — precise enough for decisions

The CI shrinks proportional to 1/sqrt(n). You need 4x the data to halve the uncertainty. There is no shortcut.

Rule of thumb: If your CI includes both "this works" and "this is harmful," you have no result — you have a wish.

Pipeline Defense

Protecting against small-sample overconfidence requires structural controls — not willpower. Encode the rules into the pipeline so peeking is impossible.

  • Minimum sample gates: Block analysis output until n reaches pre-computed requirement
  • Sequential testing: Use alpha-spending functions (O'Brien-Fleming, Pocock) that penalize early looks
  • Pre-committed stopping rules: Define interim analysis points before data collection starts
  • Mandatory CI reporting: Never show a point estimate without its interval — force the width into every report

Sequential boundaries let you stop early IF the effect is massive — but the threshold at early looks is so extreme that only a 4-5 sigma result passes. This is the tax for peeking.

Key insight: The boundaries are widest at the start (hardest to cross) and narrow over time. This is the opposite of human intuition, which is most confident at the start.
