Anchoring Bias

First numbers become phantom baselines from irrelevant contexts

The Trap

The first number you see becomes your reference point — everything after is judged relative to it, even when it's irrelevant or from a different population.

  • Team reads "average churn is 5%" from an enterprise SaaS study
  • Their B2C freemium product has 7% — alarm
  • But 10-15% monthly churn is normal for their category
  • They spend 6 months "fixing" churn that was already best-in-class

Example: First paper reports 0.95 AUC (with data leakage). Your clean 0.88 feels terrible. The leaked number poisoned your expectations — your 0.88 might be state-of-the-art.

Defense: Ask "where did my reference number come from? Same population, same conditions, same methodology?" If not — discard it and compute your own baseline from first principles.

In Data Science

Anchoring manifests in ML workflows as invisible constraints on exploration:

  • Default hyperparameters barely explored — sklearn's defaults become the ceiling because "good enough" stops the search
  • First paper's architecture copied as-is — ResNet-50 used for 128x128 grayscale when a 4-layer CNN suffices
  • Anchoring on training loss magnitude — "loss should be below 0.1" becomes gospel regardless of task complexity
  • Initial EDA observation becomes the permanent mental model — first scatter plot shows linear trend, team never checks for non-linearity

Example: Grid search around lr=0.001 with range [0.0005, 0.005]. Actual optimum at lr=0.03 is never found because the search radius was anchored to the default.

Defense: Start searches on log-scale spanning orders of magnitude. Random search beats grid search partly because it escapes the anchor's gravitational pull.

Pipeline Defense

Multi-candidate validation breaks the anchor by forcing breadth:

  • Broad grid search: Cover the full plausible range, not just ±10% around the default
  • Data-driven thresholds: Let the data tell you optimal splits, not convention
  • Multiple starting points: Run optimization from 5+ random initializations, not just the "standard" one
  • Adversarial baselines: Always include a naive baseline to calibrate what "good" actually means

Example: Team anchored on 0.5 classification threshold. Cost-sensitive analysis reveals 0.32 threshold reduces total business cost by 40% — but no one checked because 0.5 "felt right."

Principle: A pipeline that only tests one neighborhood of parameter space is not validating — it's confirming. Sweep wide, then narrow.
