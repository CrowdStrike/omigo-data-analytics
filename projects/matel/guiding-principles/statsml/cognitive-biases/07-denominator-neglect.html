Denominator Neglect

Reacting to percentages while ignoring the tiny sample behind them — confusing precision of expression with precision of knowledge.

The Trap

Getting drawn to the percentage while ignoring the tiny sample behind it. A large number from a small bucket is noise masquerading as signal.

  • Product A: 5.0 stars (3 reviews)
  • Product B: 4.2 stars (1,847 reviews)
  • Three ratings are trivially easy to game or coincidence
  • Product B's 4.2 has been validated by nearly 2,000 independent observations

Example: "Dr. Smith has 98% success rate!" (50 procedures). Dr. Jones: 94% (5,000 procedures). Dr. Smith's 98% could easily be 90% with a few more patients. Dr. Jones's 94% is real.

Defense: When given a percentage, first question: "out of how many?" If they can't answer, the percentage is meaningless.

Why Small Denominators Lie

The width of a confidence interval for a proportion scales as 1/√n. This means:

  • n=10: ±30 percentage points of uncertainty
  • n=100: ±10 percentage points
  • n=1000: ±3 percentage points
  • n=10000: ±1 percentage point

At n=10, a "70% success rate" is statistically indistinguishable from 40% or 93%. The number looks precise — it has digits — but the knowledge behind it is wildly imprecise.

The illusion: "70%" feels like a measurement. At n=10, it's a guess.

Three Flavors

1. Classic denominator neglect: Ignoring sample size entirely. "80% accuracy" with no context.

2. Base rate neglect: Ignoring how common the event is. A 99%-accurate test for a 1-in-10,000 disease still produces mostly false positives.

3. Relative risk framing: "Doubles your risk!" — from 1 in a million to 2 in a million. The denominator (baseline rate) is hidden by the multiplier.

All three share the same root: attention is captured by the numerator (the dramatic number) while the denominator (the context that gives it meaning) is invisible.

Pipeline Defense

How a statistical pipeline prevents denominator neglect:

  • Minimum-n gates: Refuse to compute metrics below a sample floor
  • Mandatory CI reporting: Never report a point estimate without its interval
  • Effect size + n together: Always pair "what happened" with "how sure are we"
  • Shrinkage/pooling: Small-sample estimates get pulled toward the population mean (Bayesian or empirical Bayes)
  • Flagging: Visual or textual warnings when n is below a reliability threshold
Principle: A percentage without a sample size is not information — it's decoration. The pipeline refuses to produce decoration.
