Sampling & Selection Biases

How non-representative samples distort conclusions and create phantom effects

1. Sampling Bias

The sample systematically excludes or underrepresents segments of the population, making conclusions invalid for the full group.

Classic case: 1936 Literary Digest phone survey predicted Landon over Roosevelt. Phones were owned by the wealthy — the sample missed the majority who voted FDR.
Core problem: The mechanism of selection correlates with the outcome variable.
  • Online surveys miss offline populations
  • Convenience sampling over-indexes accessible groups
  • Large N does not fix biased selection (10M biased samples are still wrong)

2. Non-Response Bias

When non-respondents differ systematically from respondents, the observed data skews toward whoever chose to participate.

Example: Customer satisfaction survey gets 40% response rate. Respondents report 4.2/5 satisfaction. The 60% who didn't respond are estimated at 3.0/5 — they left because they were unhappy.
Core problem: Willingness to respond correlates with the variable being measured.
  • Happy customers respond to NPS; unhappy ones churn silently
  • Healthy patients return for follow-up; sick ones drop out
  • Higher response rate does not guarantee less bias

3. Healthy Worker / Volunteer Bias

People who volunteer for studies or remain active in systems are inherently different from the general population — they're healthier, more motivated, or more engaged.

Example: Fitness app study: 15% of users tracked consistently for 6 months. 85% quit within weeks. The "findings" only describe the motivated minority.
Core problem: The act of participation is itself a filter on the outcome.
  • Workplace studies show workers are healthier than general pop — because sick people aren't employed
  • Clinical trial volunteers are more health-conscious
  • Active users are not representative of all users

4. Attrition Bias

When participants who drop out of a study are systematically different from those who remain, the surviving sample no longer represents the original population.

Example: Online course starts with 1000 students. 60% drop out. Completers average 85% score — but this reflects selection, not teaching quality.
Core problem: Dropout correlates with the outcome, inflating apparent effect sizes.
  • Drug trials lose patients with side effects — survivors look healthier
  • Longitudinal studies lose mobile/poor participants
  • A/B tests with differential attrition produce phantom winners
