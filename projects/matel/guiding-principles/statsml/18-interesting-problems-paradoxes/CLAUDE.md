# Interesting Problems & Paradoxes

Classic puzzles, real-world analogues, and problems that reveal non-intuitive truths — Birthday paradox, Monty Hall, Nash equilibrium, etc.

## Page Length: 3–4 Sections, Hard Cap

Every detail page in this folder gets **at most 3–4 numbered sections**, one canvas each.

- **Three is the target.** A puzzle that needs five sections has not been compressed yet.
- **The last section is the boundary** — when the surprise stops applying, or what the thing is
  often confused with. This is mandatory and counts toward the cap.
- **Density comes from math boxes, not sections.** A section takes 2–3 `.math-box` blocks, so
  three related facts belong in one section rather than three.
- **Merge rather than append.** Two sections running the same computation, or two that would
  share a chart, are one section.
- **Do not pad to the cap.** Two sections plus a boundary is fine.
- **Symptom of a violation:** a section whose only job is to restate an earlier one with
  different numbers, or a role-labelled filler section ("Where It Strikes", "The Defense").

Reason: these pages are read in one sitting to get one idea. Past four sections the reader has
stopped tracking which surprise the page was about, and the extra sections are almost always
re-derivations of the first one.

## Text Volume Inside a Section

The cap on sections is not an invitation to make each one longer.

- **One `.math-box` per section.** Needing a second means the section is carrying two ideas —
  split it out or cut the weaker one.
- **Five bullets.** Each is a bold colored label plus a phrase that fits on one line.
- **No lead paragraph.** The math box is the opening; prose above it is redundant.
- **The table inside the math box does the work.** Three or four rows, not seven.
- **Cut the figure that only supports another figure.** If a number is not the point of its own
  sentence, it belongs in the chart or nowhere.

`21-somebody-has-to-win-and-usually-nobody-does` is the reference for this shape.
