Folk Wisdom Under a Microscope

Everyday statements examined for the gaps between what they claim and what they can support.

The purpose: not to criticise popular sayings, and not to collect contradictions as a curiosity. Most of these carry something real — a few are simply correct. The exercise is to take a statement everyone accepts and ask what it would take to actually establish it: where the science is thin, which biases produced the evidence, which words are carrying more than one meaning, and where a single number has been asked to stand in for something with many dimensions. The habit transfers. The same questions that dissolve a proverb are the ones that should be asked of a dashboard, a study, or a confident claim in a meeting. The goal is to find the structural grounding in everyday statements — not to conclude there is none.
★ SPECIAL — HOW TO ASSESS ANY CLAIM

1. Contradictory Proverb Pairs

Pairs that advise the exact opposite with equal confidence — "look before you leap" vs "he who hesitates is lost," "great minds think alike" vs "fools seldom differ," "knowledge is power" vs "ignorance is bliss." The contradiction is not a flaw in either half: each pair brackets a hidden conditioning variable that neither proverb names, and the disagreement is precisely what locates it. Closes with an interrogation you can run against any confident statement — define, falsify, find the comparison group, name the regime, check direction, check the metric, check observability, fix the horizon, test independence, locate yourself, discount the packaging.

Mental model: a rule that fires unconditionally cannot be wrong, so it cannot be useful. The pair carries more information than either half alone.
CAUSATION

2. "No pain no gain"

Effort and discomfort often accompany growth, so the brain pattern-matches pain to progress. But pain is a correlate at best and an injury signal at worst — it is not the mechanism.

Mental model: pain accompanies growth; it does not cause it.
SURVIVORSHIP

3. "Behind every successful man there is a woman"

A pattern observed only among successful men, with no comparison group of unsuccessful men. Every term — "successful", "behind", "a woman" — is left undefined so nothing can refute it.

Mental model: a pattern found only among successes is not a cause.
UNFALSIFIABLE

4. "Everything happens for a reason"

Fits every possible outcome, so it forecasts none. Assigning purpose to random events reduces anxiety, which is why the claim survives despite having no predictive content.

Mental model: a claim that fits every outcome predicts none.
SURVIVORSHIP

5. "What doesn't kill you makes you stronger"

The people it did kill, or permanently damaged, are not available to contradict it. Adversity produces a distribution of outcomes; the saying reports only the right tail.

Mental model: the ones it did break are not around to disagree.
SELECTION

6. "Winners never quit"

Circular by design: anyone who quits is reclassified as not-a-winner. Strategic withdrawal — the thing that preserves capital for a better bet — is coded as moral failure.

Mental model: defined so that quitting disqualifies you retroactively.
CAUSATION

7. "Follow your passion"

Causal direction is probably reversed: mastery tends to produce passion rather than the other way round. Also ignores whether anyone will pay for the passion in question.

Mental model: the arrow may point from success to passion.
UNIVERSAL QUANTIFIER

8. "The customer is always right"

"Always" is a universal quantifier that is straightforwardly false, and treating it as policy inverts authority — the least-informed party overrides domain expertise.

Mental model: "always" is doing all the work, and it is false.
FALSE AUTHORITY

9. "Data doesn't lie"

The presence of data and a chart is not evidence that either is right. Inherited from the big-data wave of the 2000s, when data-driven decisions became the standard and the reasoning quietly inverted into: if there is data, it must be true.

Mental model: data is a perspective — it can be buggy, biased, or selectively chosen, and a polished chart hides all three equally well.
BASE RATE

10. "Failure is not an option"

Declaring an outcome unacceptable does not change its probability — it only suppresses reporting of early warning signs, which raises the real failure rate.

Mental model: declaring failure impossible does not lower its probability.
SELECTION

11. "Good things come to those who wait"

Selects on the dependent variable and never prices the opportunity cost of waiting. Patience that happened to be rewarded gets remembered; patience that wasted years does not.

Mental model: waiting has a cost that the saying never counts.
UNIVERSAL QUANTIFIER

12. "Time heals all wounds"

True on average, false for many individuals. Chronic grief and untreated trauma are the counterexamples the saying quietly excludes to keep its universal quantifier.

Mental model: averages heal; individuals sometimes do not.
FALSE DICHOTOMY

13. "Actions speak louder than words"

A false dichotomy — words are a form of action, and both can be performed cheaply for an audience. Whether behaviour beats stated intent depends entirely on context.

Mental model: words are actions, and both can be staged.
SURVIVORSHIP

14. "Try and try until you succeed"

Persistence without a feedback loop is repetition, not learning. Encourages sunk-cost escalation by treating "stop and reassess" as the one forbidden move.

Mental model: persistence without feedback is just repetition.
JUST-WORLD

15. "You reap what you sow"

A just-world assumption dressed as an observation. Outcomes are shaped by starting conditions, timing, and luck, none of which the sowing metaphor has room for.

Mental model: assumes the world settles accounts. It does not.
CHERRY-PICKING

16. "Slow and steady wins the race"

Deliberately truncates a fable and ignores every domain where speed wins. Competing proverbs — "strike while the iron is hot" — advise the exact opposite with equal confidence.

Mental model: the proverb was chosen, not derived.
THRESHOLD

17. "Money can't buy happiness"

Ignores the threshold below which money reliably does buy relief from misery, and conflates "cannot buy all happiness" with "cannot buy any."

Mental model: "cannot buy all" is not "cannot buy any."
TRUNCATED QUOTE

18. "Jack of all trades, master of none"

The full proverb ends "...but oftentimes better than master of one." The truncation reverses the original meaning and then hides behind it.

Mental model: the original proverb praised generalists.
MISLEADING METRIC

19. "Girls grow faster than boys"

"Growth" is not one quantity — height, weight, bone maturity, and muscle mass each have a different sex ordering. Fixing on height still equivocates between earlier onset, higher rate, and greater total.

Mental model: one scalar over a many-aspect process hides the weights.
CIRCULAR BENCHMARK

20. "Humans are the most intelligent animals"

The species being ranked first authored the criteria, chose the axes it dominates, and scored the results. Any counterexample gets absorbed by redefining intelligence.

Mental model: the contestant wrote the scoring rubric.
SURVIVORSHIP

21. "Hard work always pays off"

Survivorship plus an undefined "hard" makes the claim true by construction: if you failed, you did not work hard enough. The millions who worked equally hard and failed are never interviewed.

Mental model: you only interview the winners.
OBSERVABILITY BIAS

22. "Attack is the best form of defence"

Defence succeeds by producing non-events that leave no record, while attack generates countable artefacts. Also confuses per-round payoff with cumulative ruin: a strategy can win 90% of encounters and still face ~88% elimination over twenty rounds.

Mental model: when one arm of a trade-off is countable and the other is an absence, metrics favour the countable arm regardless of value.
MISSING NOT AT RANDOM

23. "Silence is golden"

Speaking produces a traceable cost; staying quiet produces a non-event attributable to nobody. Speaking can be strongly positive in expectation yet reliably negative in observed experience — so agents learning from feedback converge on silence, and the norm deletes the evidence that would refute it.

Mental model: a policy that suppresses the observations which would evaluate it can never be corrected by experience.
FEEDBACK CONTAMINATION

24. "First impressions matter"

Unlike the others, this claim is empirically true — which is what makes it instructive. The impression influences the treatment that determines the outcome, so measured accuracy records the intervention rather than the forecast.

Mental model: influence and accuracy are different quantities. A signal can dominate decisions while barely beating a coin flip.
MISLEADING METRIC

25. "Family is the most important thing"

"Most important" asserts rank without margin. Family at 30% beats friends at 25% and is still outweighed by everything else combined. The winner also depends on bucketing — pooling all relatives into one category while counting friends individually decides the ranking before any weighing happens.

Mental model: argmax is not magnitude, and the categories are chosen by whoever wants the answer.
What recurs: a few failure modes account for most of what goes wrong above. Terms loose enough to fit any outcome. Evidence drawn only from the cases that survived to be observed. One scalar standing in for something with many dimensions. A rank quoted as though it were a magnitude. A description repeated until it functions as an instruction. No stated regime, so the claim never says where it stops applying. None of this makes the sayings worthless — a claim that survives with its conditions written down is genuinely useful. It makes them untested, which is a different thing, and the difference is only visible once you run the check.
