Prediction Markets Pitfalls

Why prediction market prices are noisier and less informative than they appear

1. Thin Liquidity = Noisy Prices
A market says "60% probability" but only has $500 in the order book. A single $200 bet moves it to 72%. The "probability" is unreliable with low volume — it's not a wisdom-of-crowds estimate but rather the opinion of whoever bet last. You need liquidity-weighted confidence: a 60% on $5M volume is meaningful; a 60% on $500 volume is noise. Most prediction markets have thin books, making their "probabilities" highly susceptible to manipulation and random fluctuation.
2. Binary vs Continuous Outcome Mismatch
Markets resolve YES/NO but the underlying outcome is often continuous. "Will inflation exceed 3%?" — the market price reflects the probability of a binary threshold crossing, not the expected inflation level. A market at 50% for "inflation > 3%" tells you nothing about whether expected inflation is 2.9% or 1%. Enormous information is lost in the binarization. Similarly, you cannot reconstruct the full distribution from binary markets without strong assumptions. The resolution criteria compress continuous reality into a coin flip.
3. Market Manipulation by Small Capital
In a $50K total volume market, $5K can move the price by 10 percentage points. A sophisticated actor with information (or a desire to mislead) can buy early, move the price, attract followers who see the price move as a "signal," and exit at a profit. Or worse: manipulate the market specifically to mislead observers who are using the market price as an information source. When prediction markets are used for decision-making, the incentive to manipulate them for external gain (not market profit) becomes enormous.
4. Information Asymmetry Timing
An insider knows the outcome. They bet, the price moves. The public notices the price movement, interprets it as a "signal," and bets in the same direction. The insider exits at a profit. The "probability" you see at any given moment already reflects insider action that you cannot distinguish from noise. Was that move from 40% to 55% a genuine shift in public information, or one person with insider knowledge? You can't tell from the price alone. By the time you're reading the market, the information has already been extracted.
5. Event Resolution Ambiguity
"Will X happen by December 2024?" — but what exactly counts as X? Market makers define resolution criteria that may not match your model's prediction target. Edge cases generate disputes, frozen markets, and ambiguous outcomes. "Will there be a recession?" depends on whose definition. "Will the candidate win?" seems clear until there's a recount, legal challenge, or contested result. Your model predicts one thing; the market resolves on something subtly different. This resolution risk is priced into the market but invisible to external models.
6. Kalshi: Regulatory Contract Limits
Kalshi (the regulated US prediction market) requires CFTC approval for each contract type. Some markets get shut down mid-contract. Politically sensitive events may be barred entirely. Your model might identify edge on a topic that cannot be legally traded. The universe of available contracts is artificially restricted by regulation, not by information availability. Contract limits mean the market's coverage is incomplete — you're seeing a censored view of predictable events, biased toward whatever the CFTC approves. Selection bias in what's tradeable.
7. Polymarket: Blockchain Settlement Delays
Bets settle on-chain. When gas prices spike during volatile events (exactly when you want to trade), you cannot exit positions quickly. The "market price" during high-gas periods does not equal true probability because trading is too expensive — the market can't update efficiently. A $50 bet might cost $30 in gas during network congestion. This creates stale prices exactly when information is moving fastest. The blockchain's settlement layer introduces friction that distorts price discovery during the most important moments.
8. Correlation Across Markets
If "Republicans win Senate" = YES, then "Republicans win House" probability should also move up. These markets are correlated but priced independently. In theory, arbitrage exists: buy correlated outcomes that are underpriced relative to each other. In practice, execution across multiple markets simultaneously is difficult, capital is locked until resolution, and the correlation structure itself is uncertain. Your model might correctly identify mispricing, but the inability to efficiently arbitrage across multiple markets means the mispricing persists — and you can't profit from being right.
