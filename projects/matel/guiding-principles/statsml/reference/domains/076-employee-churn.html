Domain Pitfalls: Employee Churn

Six critical modeling pitfalls in employee attrition prediction

1. Self-fulfilling prophecy
Model predicts "will leave" → manager assigns less important projects → employee feels undervalued → leaves. The prediction CAUSED the outcome. Deployed churn models must be invisible or they create churn.
2. Exit interview bias
Departing employee: "great company, just found a better opportunity" = polite fiction. Real reason: toxic manager, no growth, underpaid. Exit interviews capture PR-safe reasons, not real reasons. Data from exits is systematically dishonest.
3. Counterfactual unknown
Employee got retention offer ($10K raise) and stayed. Would they have stayed WITHOUT the offer? Maybe — they weren't actually leaving, just interviewed. You spent $10K on someone who wasn't churning. Can't measure what would have happened.
4. "Flight risk" label harms retention
Tagged as flight risk → excluded from promotion pipeline → "they're leaving anyway, don't invest." Employee notices reduced opportunities → actually starts looking. The label creates the condition it describes.
5. Internal transfer ≠ churn
Employee moves from Eng to Product = "left engineering" but didn't leave company. Depending on how you define churn scope: internal mobility looks like loss. Must distinguish exit from internal movement.
6. Boomerang employees
Left 2 years ago, wants to come back. Your model said they were gone forever. 15–20% of "churned" employees return within 3 years. "Churn" is less permanent than models assume. Need to model return probability too.
