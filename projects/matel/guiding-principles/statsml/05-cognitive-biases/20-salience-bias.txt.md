# Salience Bias: Nothing Else on the List Was Ever Counted

A fault somebody watched break gets fixed first, and because nothing else on the list was ever counted, queue position ends up set by who happened to be in the room.

## 1. Witnessed Faults Eat the Week, and They Are Not the Big Ones

Tags: core idea | capacity follows sight | same days either way

- **The ones somebody watched break** — a checkout warning, a dead link, a receipt dated yesterday
- **The ones nobody happened to see** — a half-saved form, a search finding nothing, a bad export
- **The week's fixing capacity** — four days, exactly what the three witnessed faults cost
- **Spend it on what was seen** — 600 of the 4,000 hit each month stop being hit, 15% of the harm
- **Spend the same four days by reach** — 3,400 stop instead, 85% of the harm, 5.7 times as many
- **What being watched tells you** — that the fault is real, and nothing about how far it reaches
- **What it does to you anyway** — it moves that fault to the front of a queue never sorted by reach
- **The worst fault on the list** — the half-saved form, 1,800 a month, 45% of the harm, unwatched

**Key point:** Nobody chose the smaller outcome. The three faults that happened to break in front of somebody cost exactly the four days the week had, and the same four days spent by reach would have spared 3,400 people instead of 600.

*Illustrative Example — six constructed faults with made-up monthly reach and fix costs; the totals, both shares, the ratio and the best affordable set are all computed at render time.*

## 2. Ten Defensible Weeks in a Row

Tags: it compounds | ten weeks | queue-jumping

- **The rule in force** — one fix a week, given to whichever fault somebody just watched break
- **Ten quiet weeks** — a different small fault surfaces in view each week, 10 to 59 people apiece
- **The one at the back** — a counted fault hitting 420 a week, more than all ten small ones together
- **Every week it loses** — the fresh sighting jumps the queue, because you cannot unsee a fault
- **No single week is wrong** — each choice is defensible on the morning it is made, and made alone
- **What the ten weeks cost** — 6,044 encounters, where the same ten fixes by reach cost 2,117
- **Why nothing corrects it** — nothing got unfixed; the big one simply never reached the front
- **Where the cost really lives** — 3,927 extra encounters, 3,780 of them from the 420 fault waiting

**Key point:** No single week's decision was wrong on its own. The 420-a-week fault lost every week to a fresher one, and ten defensible choices in a row cost 6,044 encounters where the very same ten fixes in reach order cost 2,117 — nearly three times as many, counting one person hit once as one encounter.

*Illustrative Example — ten seeded weekly fault sizes plus one constructed large fault; both cumulative curves, their difference and the ratio are computed at render time.*

## 3. When Acting on What You Saw Is Right, and What Actually Fixes This

Tags: the boundary | confirmed beats suspected | count the rest

- **The honest case for acting** — a fault you watched break is confirmed, and needs no hunch
- **The one you only inferred** — bigger if it is there, but nobody counted it and it may not be
- **The sure thing** — 240 people a month, certain, and the half-day you have covers it outright
- **The maybe** — 1,200 a month if the hunch holds, five times as many, and nobody if it does not
- **Where the two break even** — a 1-in-5 chance of being right, since 240 divided by 1,200 is 20%
- **Below that line** — chasing the hunch helps fewer than the 240 you would have spared for certain
- **The defect stated plainly** — not that you fixed what you saw, but that nothing else was counted
- **What an hour of counting buys** — a count replaces the chance, so the 1-in-5 line stops mattering

**Key point:** Acting on what you witnessed is not the mistake — a confirmed 240 carries no risk of being imaginary, and below a 1-in-5 chance that certainty helps more people than a 1,200-person maybe. The mistake is that nothing else on the list was ever measured, so you never learn which side of the 20% line you are on. An hour of counting converts the whole problem.

*Illustrative Example — two constructed faults, one counted and one only suspected; the break-even chance is computed at render time as the confirmed reach divided by the suspected reach.*
