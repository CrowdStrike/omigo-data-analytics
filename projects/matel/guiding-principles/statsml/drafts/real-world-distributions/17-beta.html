Beta Distribution — Real-World Examples

The natural distribution for proportions, probabilities, and rates bounded between 0 and 1. Extremely flexible: uniform, U-shaped, J-shaped, bell-shaped — all from two parameters α and β.

1. Baseball Batting Averages

α=80, β=220

Career batting averages cluster around .260-.280. The Beta distribution is the conjugate prior for the binomial, making it the natural model for "hits per at-bat."

  • Shrinkage toward league average: a rookie going 4-for-10 is not really a .400 hitter — the Beta prior pulls the estimate back toward .267
  • Why .400 seasons are almost impossible now: the narrowing of talent distribution (better competition, better training) compresses the variance — the Beta gets tighter over decades
  • Large α+β = high concentration = low variance, reflecting thousands of career at-bats

2. Website Conversion Rates

α=3, β=50

Fraction of visitors who buy, across many sites. Most sites convert 2-8%, with a right-skewed tail because few sites achieve excellence.

  • Bayesian A/B testing uses Beta as prior: α=successes (purchases), β=failures (visits without purchase)
  • Posterior after observing data: Beta(α+conversions, β+non-conversions)
  • Right-skewed because α << β — most probability mass near zero
  • Industry benchmarks: e-commerce ~2-3%, SaaS landing pages ~5-7%, email opt-in ~10-15%

3. Proportion of Defective Items in a Batch

α=2, β=30

QC inspection results across batches. Usually low defect rate, with occasional bad batches creating a right tail.

  • Acceptance sampling: if the Beta posterior gives P(defect rate > threshold) > 5%, reject the batch
  • Process capability indices (Cpk) relate to how far the Beta mean is from specification limits
  • Overdispersion: batch-to-batch variation in defect rate is itself Beta-distributed (Beta-Binomial model)
  • Six Sigma targets: 3.4 defects per million, corresponding to extremely tight Beta(α, very large β)

4. Student Test Score Proportions

α=5, β=2

Fraction of questions answered correctly on a well-designed exam. Left-skewed because most students pass — α > β shifts the distribution right.

  • Item Response Theory (IRT): the probability of a correct answer is modeled as a function of ability, and the score distribution across students follows a Beta
  • Ceiling effects for easy exams: when α >> β, the distribution piles up near 1.0
  • Floor effects for hard exams: when α << β, pile-up near 0
  • A well-designed exam targets α ≈ β for maximum discrimination between students

5. Voter Turnout by Precinct

α=4, β=4

Fraction of registered voters who vote, across precincts. When α ≈ β, the Beta is symmetric and bell-shaped — bounded on [0,1].

  • Symmetric bell shape because α = β, centered at 0.5
  • Varies by election type: local elections (low turnout, effectively smaller α+β), presidential (higher, shifted right)
  • Urban vs rural: different Beta parameters reflect structural differences in accessibility, motivation
  • Unlike a Normal, this cannot produce impossible values (<0 or >1) — a key advantage for proportions

6. Allele Frequency in Population Genetics

α=0.5, β=0.5 (U-shaped)

Frequency of a genetic variant across populations. A mixture: 300 samples from Beta(1,1) (neutral/uniform) and 700 from Beta(0.3,0.3) (U-shaped under selection).

  • Wright-Fisher model: genetic drift pushes allele frequencies toward 0 or 1 (fixation or loss)
  • U-shape = balancing selection maintaining polymorphism (e.g., sickle cell trait in malaria regions)
  • Beta(1,1) = Uniform: under pure neutrality with no drift, all frequencies equally likely
  • The mixture captures real genomic data: most loci are near-fixed, some are balanced
