Weibull Distribution — Real-World Examples

The reliability engineer's workhorse. Shape parameter k controls failure behavior: k<1 = infant mortality (decreasing hazard), k=1 = exponential (constant hazard), k>1 = wear-out (increasing hazard). Scale parameter λ sets the characteristic life.

1. Wind Speeds at a Site

k=2, λ=7 m/s

10-minute average wind speeds measured at hub height for wind energy resource assessment.

  • k≈2 (Rayleigh distribution) is nearly universal for wind speeds across sites worldwide
  • λ varies by site geography and elevation (typical range 3–12 m/s)
  • Turbine power output ∝ v³, so the upper tail of the distribution matters enormously for energy yield
  • Annual Energy Production (AEP) depends on both shape and scale — two sites with the same mean wind but different k yield different energy
  • Wind resource engineers fit Weibull parameters from anemometer data to predict 20-year energy production

2. Steel Fatigue Life

k=3, λ=500,000 cycles

Cycles to failure for a structural steel beam under repeated loading at a fixed stress amplitude.

  • k>1 indicates wear-out failure mode — the hazard rate increases with age as fatigue cracks propagate
  • Cracks grow deterministically after nucleation; scatter comes from initial flaw size distribution
  • S-N curves (stress vs. number of cycles) characterize material fatigue behavior
  • For structural design, the minimum life (lower tail) matters far more than the average life
  • Engineers use B10 life (10th percentile) as the design criterion, not the mean

3. Electronic Component Infant Mortality

k=0.5, λ=1000 hours

Early-life failures in semiconductor devices during burn-in testing.

  • k<1 produces a decreasing failure rate — units that survive early hours become increasingly reliable
  • Burn-in screening subjects all units to elevated stress (temperature, voltage) to precipitate latent defects
  • This represents the left side of the bathtub curve (infant mortality region)
  • Survivors pass into the useful-life period with near-constant (exponential) hazard
  • Manufacturing defects (voids, contamination, oxide pinholes) cause the early failures

4. Marathon Finish Times

k=4, λ=260 min

Time to complete a marathon for all finishers in a large city race.

  • k≈3–5 produces slight right skew: fast runners cluster tightly, slower runners spread out
  • Bounded below by human physiological limits (no one finishes in 60 minutes)
  • Race cutoff time (typically 6–7 hours) creates right-censoring in the data
  • The distribution captures the asymmetry better than a Normal — skewness is inherent, not a nuisance
  • Pacing strategy, training volume, and weather all shift λ while k reflects field heterogeneity

5. Flood Return Periods

k=1.5, λ=20 years

Years between major floods exceeding a discharge threshold at a river gauge station.

  • k=1.5 indicates slight "wear-in" — each flood does not fully reset hydrological conditions
  • The "100-year flood" corresponds to the 95th percentile of the inter-arrival time distribution
  • Climate change is shifting both k and λ: increasing urbanization reduces λ (more frequent floods), while changing precipitation patterns alter k
  • Flood insurance and infrastructure design rely heavily on return period estimation
  • Non-stationarity violates the i.i.d. assumption — parameters must be re-estimated each decade

6. Ceramic Fracture Strength

k=8, λ=400 MPa

Breaking strength of ceramic rods tested in four-point bending.

  • High k (≈8) produces a tight, nearly symmetric distribution — ceramics are brittle and fail suddenly with little deformation
  • Weakest-link theory: failure initiates at the single worst flaw in the stressed volume
  • Larger specimens have lower mean strength (size effect) because more flaws are sampled
  • Ceramics use Weibull rather than Normal for strength because the underlying physics is extreme-value, not additive
  • The Weibull modulus (k) is a material property — higher k means more reliable (less variable) strength
