12. Linear Regression (OLS)

Fits a line minimizing squared residuals — the most assumption-heavy algorithm in common use
  • Assumes linearity: Relationship between each feature and the outcome MUST be linear. A quadratic or threshold relationship is invisible — the model draws a flat line through a curve.
  • Assumes normal residuals: Errors must be normally distributed for p-values and confidence intervals to be valid. Non-normal residuals → can't trust significance of any coefficient.
  • Assumes homoscedasticity: Variance of residuals must be constant across all predicted values. "Fan-shaped" residual plots mean standard errors are wrong — typically too small, producing false significance.
  • Assumes no multicollinearity: Features must not be strongly correlated. Correlated features → coefficients become unstable (flip signs, explode in magnitude), standard errors inflate.
  • Assumes independence of errors: No autocorrelation. Violated in time series, spatial data, or clustered data. Leads to underestimated standard errors.
  • Assumes no influential outliers: One extreme point can drag the entire regression line. Cook's distance > 1 means a single observation is controlling the fit.
  • Violation: Predicting house price from square footage — relationship is linear below 3000 sqft but flat above (luxury pricing is location-driven). OLS draws one line through both regimes, underestimating small homes and overestimating large ones.
  • Real example: Ames housing: OLS reports "lot area" coefficient = $2.50/sqft (p<0.001). But residual plot shows massive fan-shape — cheap homes have tight residuals, expensive homes have huge variance. The $2.50 estimate is "significant" but its CI is 4× too narrow.
  • Alternatives: Non-linear → polynomial regression, GAM, or range-based features. Heteroscedastic → weighted least squares or robust regression. Non-normal residuals → GLM with appropriate link. Multicollinear → ridge regression, LASSO, or drop redundant features.
