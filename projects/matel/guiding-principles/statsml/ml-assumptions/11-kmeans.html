11. K-Means Clustering

Partitions data into K spherical clusters — assumes equal-size, equal-variance blobs
  • Assumes spherical clusters: K-means minimizes distance to centroid — this only works when clusters are roughly round. Elongated, crescent-shaped, or irregular clusters get split wrong.
  • Assumes equal variance: All clusters are expected to have similar spread. A tight cluster near a spread-out one → K-means gives the big cluster too much territory, steals points from the tight one.
  • Assumes equal size: Biased toward equal-membership clusters. A rare subgroup (n=20) next to a large cluster (n=5000) gets absorbed entirely.
  • Requires feature scaling: Distance-based — unscaled features dominate. Same problem as KNN/SVM.
  • Must specify K in advance: How many clusters? There's no correct answer from the algorithm itself. Elbow method and silhouette scores are heuristics, not proofs.
  • Sensitive to initialization: Different random seeds → different results. K-means++ helps but doesn't guarantee global optimum.
  • Violation: Two concentric rings → K-means draws a line through the middle, splitting both rings in half. Two clusters of different sizes → K-means equalizes membership, misassigning boundary points.
  • Real example: Customer segmentation with income ($0–$1M) and age (18–90). Without scaling, K-means clusters entirely by income (ignoring age). With scaling, it finds "young high-earners" vs "old low-earners" — but if the real structure is "3 lifestyle segments" of unequal size, K-means forces equal splits.
  • Alternatives: Non-spherical → DBSCAN, spectral clustering. Unequal sizes → Gaussian Mixture Models (GMM). Hierarchical structure → agglomerative clustering. Unknown K → DBSCAN (auto-detects), or BIC-based model selection with GMM.
