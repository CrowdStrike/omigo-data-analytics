Statistical Paradoxes

Counterintuitive results where hidden structure — confounds, filters, base rates, sampling methods — breaks your intuition. Each paradox exposes a different failure mode in how humans process data.

Why these matter: Every one of these paradoxes has killed real projects, reversed business decisions, or led teams to draw exactly the wrong conclusion from good data. They're not theoretical curiosities — they're recurring patterns of how structure in data contradicts human intuition.
AGGREGATION

1. Simpson's Paradox

A trend in every subgroup can reverse when you combine them. Treatment A wins on mobile, wins on desktop, yet loses overall — because the audiences were distributed differently.
PROBABILITY

2. Base Rate Fallacy

Ignoring how rare something is makes accurate tests look useless. Your fraud model is 99% accurate, yet 91% of its alerts are false — because fraud itself is 0.1% prevalent.
SAMPLING

3. Berkson's Paradox

Filtering your data can create correlations that don't exist. When you only hire people strong in at least one skill, it forces an apparent trade-off between unrelated traits.
MEASUREMENT

4. Regression to the Mean

Extreme measurements are partly noise — the next measurement will be closer to average. Your top-10 campaigns all dropped not because you changed something, but because week 1 was luck + skill.
AGGREGATION

5. Ecological Fallacy

Group averages don't describe individuals. "High spend" segment has 78% retention — but one-time big buyers churn at 35% while daily small buyers stay at 95%. Same segment, opposite behaviors.
MEASUREMENT

6. Multiple Comparisons

Test enough things and you'll find "winners" by pure chance. 50 tests at p<0.05 gives you 2.5 expected false positives even when nothing is real. Those 3 "winners" prove nothing.
SAMPLING

7. Survivorship Bias

You only see what survived — not what failed. S&P 500 returns look great because declining stocks are quietly removed. You're measuring a highlight reel, not the original cohort.
AGGREGATION

8. Will Rogers Phenomenon

Moving borderline cases between groups can improve both averages. Reclassify bottom-tier "medium" users as "low" — both segments' averages rise, with zero behavior change.
MEASUREMENT

9. Lindley's Paradox

With enough data, meaningless differences become "statistically significant." p<0.0001 on button color with 10M users — the lift is 0.002%, worth $4/month. Significant but worthless.
SAMPLING

10. Inspection Paradox

You're more likely to observe things that last longer. Average completed session is 3 minutes, but "users active now" averages 14 minutes — because you oversample long sessions.
INCENTIVES

11. Goodhart's Law

When a measure becomes a target, it stops being a good measure. Optimize for "time on site" → team adds friction, hides logout. Metric up 40%, satisfaction down 57%.
PROBABILITY

12. Birthday Paradox

Collisions happen far sooner than your intuition says. Need only 23 people for 50% chance of shared birthday. Systems fail at "safe" scales because pairs grow as n², not n.
PROBABILITY

13. Monty Hall Problem

New information changes the odds — even when it feels like it shouldn't. Host opens a goat door. Your door is still 1/3, but switching wins 2/3 because the host's knowledge is free information.
PROBABILITY

14. Lottery Paradox

The system guarantees a winner. It does NOT guarantee YOU will win. With 1M players daily, someone wins every day — yet you playing for 30 years has 99% chance of never winning. Population certainty ≠ individual certainty.
The common thread: Every paradox is a case where hidden structure — a confound, a filter, a prevalence, a sample method — breaks your intuition. The defense: (1) segment before you aggregate, (2) compare to base rates, (3) use control groups, (4) correct for multiple tests, (5) always ask "what's not in this dataset?"
