E-Commerce & Marketplace — How Your Data Actually Looks

7 data shapes you will see in any online store's numbers — and why they matter

DROPS OFF A CLIFF

Where People Click in Search Results (Geometric Decay)

The first result gets ~30% of clicks, the second gets half that, and by the fifth spot almost nobody is clicking. Users build trust in the platform's ranking over time — so they develop a strong habit of clicking position #1. The platform earns this by consistently placing relevant items on top, which trains a steep positional bias. Think of it like a hallway with doors: at each door, about half the remaining people stop walking.

  • At each position, about half the remaining eyeballs give up and leave
  • Positional trust compounds over time — users learn to rely on the ranking
  • This same cliff shape shows up on every search engine, marketplace, and ranked list
  • If you're A/B testing search results, the position effect drowns out the content effect
SMOOTH HILL WITH SPIKES

Shopping Cart Totals — Free Shipping Changes Behavior (Log-Normal + Spikes)

Most cart totals form a smooth hill around $35, like you'd expect. But then there are sharp spikes at exactly $25, $35, $49, and $75 — because those are the free-shipping thresholds. People literally add "filler items" to hit the magic number. You can reverse-engineer a company's shipping policy just by looking at the spike locations.

  • Those sharp spikes? They're people gaming the free-shipping cutoffs
  • The smooth hill in the middle is natural spending — people buying what they actually want
  • The spikes mess up models that assume spending changes smoothly
  • When the company changes its free-shipping threshold, the spikes move — instantly visible in the data
TWO HUMPS, GAP IN THE MIDDLE

How Long Until Someone Buys — Impulse vs. Researchers (Bimodal)

There are two totally different kinds of buyers hiding in this data. Group A buys within hours (impulse buyers who saw an ad and clicked "buy now"). Group B takes about two weeks (researchers who compare options and come back after retargeting emails). Days 3-10? Almost nobody buys then. The "average" of 7 days describes literally no one.

  • The dead zone (days 3-10) is where nobody converts — the "average" time lands right here and describes no real customer
  • Two completely different buyer types: same-day impulse shoppers and two-week comparison researchers
  • If your marketing doesn't know which group it's talking to, it will fail at reaching both
  • Two humps = two different decision-making processes happening in your data
LOVE IT OR HATE IT

Star Ratings — Why You See So Many 5s and 1s (J-Curve)

Most products have tons of 5-star reviews, a bunch of 1-star reviews, and almost nothing in between. That's not because products are either amazing or terrible — it's because only delighted or angry people bother to write reviews. The 3-star "it was fine" crowd stays silent. You're seeing motivation to review, not product quality.

  • "It was fine" people don't write reviews — silence is the most common opinion
  • This shape tells you who is motivated enough to leave feedback, not how good the product is
  • The "average rating" of 3.7 stars describes nobody — it's a blend of lovers and haters
  • When reviews are required (like after delivery on some platforms), the desert in the middle fills in and the shape looks completely different
MOST PAY NOTHING, FEW PAY A LOT

Revenue Per User — The "Two Universes" Problem (Zero-Inflated with a Long Tail)

70% of your users spend exactly $0. Of the 30% who do spend, a tiny 1% of them generate 40% of all your revenue. "Average revenue per user" is meaningless here — it's like averaging the salary of homeless people with billionaires. You have two completely separate populations that need separate treatment.

  • 70% at exactly $0 — you're literally averaging two different universes together
  • Among people who do spend: a tiny group of whales generates almost half the money
  • Standard "compare the averages" tests give you nonsense on data shaped like this
  • You need to ask two separate questions: "Who converts?" and then "Among converters, who spends big?"
DIFFERENT CATEGORIES, DIFFERENT WORLDS

Return Rates — Why "Overall 15%" Is Misleading (Category Split)

Clothing gets returned 20-35% of the time (because sizing is a guessing game online). Electronics get returned 3-8% of the time (usually dead-on-arrival or wrong spec). If you mash them together into "our overall return rate is 15%," that number describes neither category. Worse: a 15% return rate in electronics is a five-alarm fire, while 15% in clothing is actually better than normal.

  • Clothing: clusters around 25% returns (mostly sizing and fit issues)
  • Electronics: clusters around 4% returns (mostly defects or wrong model ordered)
  • The blended "15%" is a mirage — no actual product category lives there
  • After the holidays, both categories spike about 5% higher (gift returns)
  • Spotting a policy change: watch the whole shape shift, not just the average number
QUICK DROP, THEN A SURPRISE BUMP

How Many Pages Before Buying — Two Types of Shoppers (Funnel with Spike)

Most people either buy on the first page they see (they clicked an ad and knew what they wanted) or give up quickly. But there's a surprising bump around pages 8-12 — those are the comparison shoppers who looked at everything before deciding. They're fewer in number but spend more per order. "Average pages viewed" hides both groups.

  • Impulse buyers: 40% buy on page 1 or 2 — they came from an ad or recommendation and already knew what they wanted
  • The big drop-off in the middle: at each page, about 30% of remaining people give up
  • Comparison shoppers: that surprise bump at pages 8-12 — they viewed many options but spend more when they finally buy
  • Mobile users give up faster than desktop users (small screen fatigue is real)
  • Making page 1 better vs. keeping page 8+ visitors engaged are completely different design problems
