Social Media & Content — Distribution Patterns

MOST GET NOTHING, A FEW BLOW UP (WITH A CAP)

Post Virality — Why Your Post Probably Won't Go Viral

Think of it like a lottery: almost every post gets ignored (0-2 shares), a handful get lucky and spread, but there's a ceiling that nobody can break through. That ceiling isn't natural — the platform deliberately slows down content that's spreading too fast. If you can figure out where that ceiling is, you've basically reverse-engineered the platform's safety valve.

  • The vast majority of posts get fewer than 2 shares — basically invisible
  • A tiny fraction takes off, and those few hog almost all the attention
  • There's a hard invisible ceiling where the platform hits the brakes on spread
  • Finding that ceiling tells you where the platform's content moderation kicks in
TWO EXTREMES, NO MIDDLE GROUND

Time Between Posts — You're Either Binging or Gone

People use social media in exactly two ways: rapid-fire scrolling and posting (every few minutes), or total absence (days or weeks of silence). There's basically nobody in the "I casually check in for 10 minutes once a day" camp. That missing middle is the fingerprint of addictive design — the app is engineered to pull you into a binge, and if it fails, you disappear entirely.

  • One cluster of users posts every 1-5 minutes — that's a binge session in action
  • The other cluster goes quiet for days or weeks at a time
  • Almost nobody uses social media "in moderation" — that middle ground is a ghost town
  • The gap between binge and dormant modes is the energy it takes to pull someone back in
THE RICH GET RICHER

Follower Counts — A Winner-Take-All Game

99% of users have fewer than 1,000 followers, while a tiny elite sits at millions. It's like wealth inequality but for attention: once you have followers, the algorithm shows you to more people, so you get even more followers. The steepness of this curve tells you exactly how aggressively the platform funnels all the eyeballs toward the top.

  • 99% of users have fewer than 1,000 followers — most people are shouting into the void
  • People who already have followers get recommended more, so they grow faster (snowball effect)
  • How steep the drop-off is reveals how much the algorithm crushes mid-level creators
  • A steeper drop = the platform concentrates attention on fewer people at the top
THREE CAMPS: LOVE IT, HATE IT, OR SILENT

Comment Sentiment — Only Strong Feelings Get Typed Out

Comments cluster into three groups: very angry, very enthusiastic, and a big blob of "meh" (people who saw it but didn't feel strongly enough to write anything). If you feel mildly positive about a post, you just scroll past — you don't comment. Only strong emotions make people reach for the keyboard. Platforms know this and optimize for outrage and hype because those are the only things that generate comments.

  • Angry comments and glowing praise dominate — mild opinions stay quiet
  • The big "neutral" mass represents lurkers who never bother writing anything
  • If you only mildly agree or disagree, you just keep scrolling — no motivation to type
  • Platforms that maximize engagement are really maximizing polarization — it's a feature, not a bug
EACH PLATFORM AGES CONTENT DIFFERENTLY

Content Half-Life — How Fast Your Post Dies Depends on Where You Posted

On a real-time feed platform, your post is basically dead after 18 minutes. On a video platform with search, it can get views for weeks or months. On a short-video platform, your content either dies in hours OR randomly gets resurrected by the algorithm days later. The shape of the decay curve is a direct X-ray of how the platform's recommendation engine works — feed-based means fast death, search-based means long life.

  • Real-time feed platforms: content dies in minutes (it's all about the scroll)
  • Search-heavy video platforms: content lives for weeks because people keep finding it
  • Algorithm-driven short-video platforms: either dead fast, or randomly brought back to life days later
  • The decay shape IS the architecture — you can literally see how the recommendation system works
CONVERSATIONS DIE FAST

Reply Chain Depth — Most Threads Are Dead After Two Messages

Online conversations fizzle out shockingly fast. Each reply has about a 60-70% chance of being the very last one in the thread. Most threads go: someone says something, one person reacts, done. The rare threads that keep going past 10+ replies? They're almost always arguments or super-niche passion topics. Platforms that chase "engagement" are really chasing arguments — because those are the only conversations that refuse to die.

  • 45% of threads: one reply and done (someone reacted, conversation over)
  • 30% of threads: a quick 2-3 reply exchange and both people move on
  • 15% of threads: an actual back-and-forth discussion (4-7 replies)
  • 10% of threads: 8+ replies — almost always heated debates or passionate niche fans
  • Each reply only has about a 35% chance of getting another reply after it
  • Platforms benefit from arguments because they're the only things that generate long threads
MAGIC MINIMUM TO GET NOTICED

Posting Frequency vs Growth — There's a Sweet Spot

Posting more doesn't equal growing more — it's not a straight line. Below about 3 posts per week, you're invisible (the algorithm simply won't bother showing you to people). Once you cross that threshold, growth starts happening. But here's the twist: post MORE than about 2 times a day and growth actually flattens or drops — your audience gets tired of you and unfollows. The sweet spot exists, but even within it, what you post matters way more than how often.

  • 0-2 posts/week: basically invisible — below the minimum the algorithm cares about
  • 3-5 posts/week: you've crossed the visibility threshold, growth starts (roughly 2-5% per week)
  • 1-2 posts/day: the sweet spot on most platforms (about 5-10% weekly growth)
  • 3+ posts/day: too much — people start unfollowing because you're flooding their feed
  • Even at the perfect posting rate, results vary wildly — content quality is king once you clear the minimum
  • The exact threshold differs by platform — short-video apps demand more frequency than professional networks
