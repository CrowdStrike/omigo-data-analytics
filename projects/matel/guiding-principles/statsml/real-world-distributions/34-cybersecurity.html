Cybersecurity — Distribution Patterns

Distributions that expose the statistical structure of attacks, vulnerabilities, detection gaps, and why averages mislead in security

CAMPAIGN CLUSTERING

Attack Inter-Arrival Times (Poisson with Bursts)

Attack arrivals are NOT uniform Poisson. They cluster into campaigns — a threat actor probes a target intensely for days, goes silent, then returns. The inter-arrival time distribution is a mixture: exponential during quiet periods, near-zero during active campaigns. A simple "alerts per hour" threshold misses this structure entirely.

  • Quiet periods: exponential with mean ~4 hours between probes
  • Campaign bursts: hundreds of attempts in minutes
  • Campaign duration: log-normal (median 3 days, tail 30+)
  • Alert fatigue: averaging over bursts = always above threshold
  • Better signal: changepoint detection, not rate thresholding
DETECTION LAG

Dwell Time — Days from Breach to Detection

Dwell time (how long an attacker is inside before detection) follows a log-normal distribution with a heavy right tail. The median is ~21 days, but the distribution extends to 200+ days for advanced persistent threats. Organizations that report "average time to detect" conflate the fast detections (automated alerts) with the catastrophic slow ones (APTs hiding in AD).

  • Median: ~21 days (Mandiant M-Trends data)
  • Mean: ~70 days (pulled by the tail)
  • Fast mode: 1-3 days (automated detection, commodity malware)
  • Long tail: 200-400 days (state actors, supply chain)
  • Damage correlates with dwell time exponentially, not linearly
BIMODAL SEVERITY

CVSS Scores — Bimodal with Sparse Middle

CVSS vulnerability scores don't form a nice bell curve. They cluster bimodally: a mass of low-severity findings (3-4 range, information disclosures) and a mass of critical ones (8-10 range). The middle range (5-7) is relatively sparse. This means "average CVSS" is meaningless — a portfolio with mean 6.0 could be all mediums, or half trivial and half catastrophic.

  • Mode 1: CVSS 3-4 (info disclosure, missing headers)
  • Mode 2: CVSS 8-10 (RCE, auth bypass, SQLi)
  • Sparse middle: 5-7 range (real exploitability is binary)
  • Gaming: vendors tune scores to avoid "critical" classification
  • CVSS is ordinal, not interval — arithmetic on it is invalid
WEIBULL SURVIVAL

Time to Patch — Weibull with Never-Patch Tail

The time from vulnerability disclosure to patch deployment follows a Weibull survival curve with a twist: a fraction of systems NEVER patch. The hazard rate increases initially (organizations respond) then flattens — the remaining unpatched systems are effectively permanent. "Mean time to patch" is undefined because the tail doesn't converge.

  • 20% patch within 7 days (automated pipelines)
  • 50% within 30 days (regular patch cycles)
  • 80% within 90 days (quarterly windows)
  • 15-20% never patch (EOL systems, forgotten servers)
  • Attackers know this: exploit development targets the never-patch segment
FAT-TAILED COST

Breach Cost — Zero-Inflated Pareto Distribution

Most security incidents cost little (contained phishing, blocked intrusions). But the cost distribution has a Pareto tail — a small fraction of breaches cause catastrophic losses. The IBM Cost of a Data Breach report's "$4.45M average" is misleading: most breaches cost under $500K, but mega-breaches ($100M+) pull the mean far from typical experience.

  • 50% of incidents: < $100K (contained, no data loss)
  • 30% of incidents: $100K-$1M (limited exposure)
  • 15% of incidents: $1M-$10M (regulatory + remediation)
  • 5% of incidents: $10M-$500M+ (mega-breaches)
  • Pareto index α ≈ 1.2 — mean exists but variance doesn't
CONDITIONAL RATE

Phishing Click Rate — Time-of-Day Conditioned Beta

Phishing click rates aren't a fixed percentage. They follow a beta distribution whose parameters shift by time of day and day of week. Monday morning (post-weekend inbox overload) and Friday afternoon (reduced vigilance) have significantly higher α, shifting the distribution right. Training effectiveness also has a Weibull decay — it wears off within 3-6 months.

  • Baseline click rate: beta(2, 18) → mean 10%, mode ~6%
  • Monday 8-10am: beta(4, 16) → mean 20%, mode ~17%
  • Post-training (week 1): beta(1, 30) → mean 3%
  • Post-training (month 6): back to beta(2, 18) — it decays
  • Authority spoofing: shifts α by 2-3x regardless of time
POLICY-SHAPED

Password Entropy — Multimodal at Policy Boundaries

Password entropy doesn't follow a smooth distribution. It clusters at policy minimums — if you require 8 characters, you get a spike at exactly 8. If you require uppercase + number, you get "Password1" patterns. The distribution is multimodal: a mass at minimum compliance, a second mass at passphrase users, and a thin tail of truly random passwords. Policy shapes the distribution more than education does.

  • Mode 1: minimum compliance (8 chars, "Summer2024!")
  • Mode 2: passphrase users (~25-35 bits, "correct-horse-battery")
  • Mode 3: password managers (~80+ bits, truly random)
  • Gap: 40-60 bit range is sparse (too complex to memorize, not using manager)
  • Reuse rate: ~65% — entropy per-site is effectively zero for most users
