Advertising & AdTech — Distribution Patterns

ALMOST ALL VALUES CRAMMED NEAR ZERO

Click-Through Rate — Most Ads Get Ignored (Beta shape)

Think of a hundred ads shown to people. Almost all of them get clicked less than half a percent of the time. The typical ad sits around 0.2% — meaning 998 out of 1000 people scroll right past it. The rare ad that breaks 2% has found something that actually grabs attention. Here's the kicker: going from 0.2% to 0.3% sounds like nothing, but that's a 50% improvement in clicks.

  • The typical ad gets clicked twice per thousand views — most humans just ignore ads
  • The rare breakout ad (>2%) found a hook that defeats the "scroll past" reflex
  • Tiny-sounding improvements (0.2% to 0.3%) are actually massive in percentage terms
  • Values are trapped between 0% and 100%, with almost everything piled up at the low end
LOPSIDED HILL THAT SLIDES LEFT AND RIGHT

Cost-Per-Click — The Price Moves With Who's Bidding (Log-normal shape)

Imagine an auction where the price of a click forms a hill tilted to the right. On weekdays, business-focused advertisers flood in and push the whole hill rightward (more expensive). On weekends, those advertisers back off and the hill slides left (cheaper). You can literally see who is in the auction just by looking at where the price hill sits on a given day.

  • The shape is a hill with a long tail stretching toward expensive clicks
  • Weekdays: business advertisers bid aggressively, prices go up across the board
  • Weekends: those bidders vanish, everything gets cheaper
  • Where the hill sits on any given day tells you who is competing in the auction
FAST DROP-OFF WITH SURPRISE BUMPS LATER

Attribution Lag — Payday Cycles Hidden in the Data (Exponential + spikes)

Most people who buy something after clicking an ad do it within the first hour — that's the impulse crowd. After that, interest drops off fast. But then something interesting happens: there are bumps at day 7 and day 30. Why? Paychecks. People click, realize they can't afford it yet, then come back when they get paid. If your tracking window cuts off at 7 days, you'll miss a whole wave of buyers who were just waiting for payday.

  • Big spike within the first hour — these are impulse buyers acting on emotion
  • Interest drops off rapidly after that — people forget or lose motivation
  • Bumps at day 7 and day 30 — people come back when their paycheck hits
  • Cutting your tracking window at 7 days means you miss real customers who just needed to wait for money
STEADY DECLINE THEN A SUDDEN WALL

Ad Frequency — Your Brain Learns to Block It (Geometric + cliff)

Each time someone sees the same ad, they're a little less likely to click it — that's natural. But at a certain point, something sharper happens: the brain just starts filtering it out like background noise. Click rates don't gently fade to zero — they fall off a cliff. For banner ads, that wall hits around the 3rd view. For video, you get about 7 views before people tune out completely.

  • Fewer people engage each additional time they see the ad — gradual decline
  • At a certain repetition count, the brain just blocks it entirely — like tuning out a repetitive noise
  • Banner ads hit the wall at about 3 views; video ads last until about 7 views
  • Looking at average click rates hides this cliff — you need to look at each frequency separately
TONS OF PENNIES, A FEW JACKPOTS

Revenue Per Impression — A Few Slots Pay for Everything (Zero-heavy + extreme tail)

Most ad slots on the internet earn almost nothing — we're talking fractions of a penny per view. They actually cost more to serve than they earn. But then there's a tiny slice of premium spots (think: the top of a popular news site) that earn 1000x more. It's the same pattern as wealth inequality: a small number of winners carry the whole system. Publishers use their handful of goldmine slots to subsidize all the junk ones.

  • The majority of ad slots earn nearly nothing — less than a penny per thousand views
  • A handful of premium slots earn 1000x more than the typical slot
  • Same pattern as wealth inequality — the top 1% generates most of the money
  • Publishers use their best slots to financially carry all the low-earning ones
