Pareto Distribution — Real-World Examples

The 80/20 rule formalized. A minimum threshold xm below which no observations exist, and a power-law tail above it. P(X > x) = (xm/x)α. Smaller α = heavier tail = more inequality.

1. Insurance Claim Sizes

α=1.5, xm=$500

Individual claim amounts above the deductible. With α < 2, the variance is infinite — a single catastrophic claim can exceed all others combined.

  • Infinite variance (α < 2): the sample mean is unstable, jumping wildly as extreme claims arrive
  • A single catastrophic claim (hurricane, major surgery) can exceed the sum of thousands of routine claims
  • Reinsurance thresholds: insurers offload tail risk above a cutoff to reinsurers who specialize in rare extremes
  • Why "average claim size" is misleading — the median is far more stable than the mean for Pareto data

2. Book Sales

α=1.2, xm=100 copies

Copies sold per title per year. The extreme bestseller effect: the top 1% of titles account for roughly 50% of all sales.

  • Long tail: most books sell fewer than 1000 copies, but a handful sell millions
  • α=1.2 is very heavy-tailed — the mean exists but barely (requires α > 1), variance is infinite
  • Why publishers need many titles: the portfolio strategy hedges against the unpredictability of which book hits the tail
  • Winner-take-all dynamics: bestseller lists create positive feedback (visibility begets more sales)

3. Website Daily Visitors

α=1.1, xm=10

Unique visitors per day across all websites. Preferential attachment — popular sites get linked more, creating extreme concentration.

  • Preferential attachment (Barabási-Albert model): new links go to already-popular sites, creating power-law degree distributions
  • Google/Facebook receive orders of magnitude more traffic than the median website
  • α=1.1 is extremely heavy: even the mean is barely finite, the top 0.01% of sites dominate total traffic
  • Advertising market follows this: ad revenue concentrates on the same sites that concentrate attention

4. Mineral Deposit Sizes

α=1.3, xm=1 ton

Recoverable ore in tons per deposit. Geological accumulation processes produce a few supergiant deposits that dwarf all others.

  • A few supergiant deposits (Witwatersrand gold, Escondida copper) contain more ore than thousands of small deposits combined
  • Exploration cost: the economic value is in finding the tail — most exploration expenditure finds nothing exceptional
  • Resource estimation uncertainty: for power-law quantities, confidence intervals on total reserves are extremely wide
  • Geological mechanism: multiplicative accumulation over millions of years produces log-scale variation

5. CEO Compensation

α=1.5, xm=$200k

Total annual compensation for chief executives. Stock options and equity grants create an extreme right tail.

  • Stock options create the extreme right tail: base salary is bounded, but equity upside is theoretically unlimited
  • α has decreased over decades (more inequality): in the 1960s it was closer to 2.5, now around 1.5
  • CEO-to-worker pay ratio: now ~300:1 in the US, up from ~20:1 in the 1960s — a direct consequence of the tail fattening
  • Tournament theory: extreme pay at the top incentivizes competition at all levels below

6. Landslide Volumes

α=1.4, xm=100 m³

Volume of material displaced per landslide event. Self-organized criticality: small slides are frequent, large ones rare but catastrophic.

  • Self-organized criticality (SOC): the slope naturally evolves to a critical state where any perturbation can trigger a slide of any size
  • Small slides are very frequent (hundreds per year in a region), large ones rare but can bury entire villages
  • Slope stability follows threshold dynamics: once shear stress exceeds cohesion, the failure size is scale-free
  • Climate change: increased rainfall intensity increases the frequency parameter but preserves the power-law shape
