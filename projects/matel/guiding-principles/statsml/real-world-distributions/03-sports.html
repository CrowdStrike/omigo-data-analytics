Real-World Distributions — Sports Analytics

BELL CURVE WITH HARD CUTOFFS

Batting Averages — Truncated Beta (What You See Is Filtered, Not Natural)

Think of batting averages like grades in a class where anyone below a C gets expelled. What's left looks like a nice bell shape between .200 and .350 — but it's not showing you natural talent. The bottom was chopped off because teams cut players who hit below .200, and the top is limited by physics (no human can hit .400 consistently). You're looking at a filtered survival group, not raw ability.

  • The floor at .200 isn't talent — it's the "you're fired" line. Anyone below gets sent down.
  • The ceiling around .350 is where human reflexes and physics say "that's the max."
  • If you could see everyone who ever tried, the curve would stretch much wider and lower.
  • This is like measuring heights in the NBA — the distribution tells you about selection, not about people.
LOPSIDED WITH SUDDEN SPIKES

Marathon Finish Times — Skewed + Spikes (Round Numbers Change Behavior)

Most people finish between 4 and 4.5 hours, with a long tail of slower runners trailing off to the right. But here's the wild part: there are sudden spikes of people finishing just under 3:00, 3:30, 4:00, and 4:30. Why? Because thousands of runners push harder in the last mile to beat a round number they set as a goal. The data literally shows human psychology bending physical performance.

  • Suspicious clusters just under each half-hour mark — people sprinting to beat the clock.
  • Just left of the spike = "I gave it everything." Just right = "I gave up on that goal."
  • Nobody speeds up to beat 3:17 or 4:23 — only round, culturally meaningful numbers trigger this behavior.
  • Your arbitrary mental target physically changes how your body performs. The data proves it.
RARE EVENTS WITH STREAKS

Goals Per Match — Overdispersed Count (Momentum Is Real, Not a Myth)

Most soccer matches have 1-2 goals, and 0 or 5+ goal games are rarer — shaped like a staircase going down. If each goal were completely independent (like flipping a coin), this staircase would drop off smoothly. But in reality, the high-scoring tail is fatter than it should be. Why? Because scoring a goal changes the game — one team gets energized, the other gets demoralized. Momentum is baked into the data.

  • The shape looks like "most common = low scores, rarer = high scores" — which makes sense for rare events.
  • The surprise: there are more blowout games (5, 6, 7 goals) than pure randomness would allow.
  • This proves momentum and team morale actually shift scoring odds — goals breed goals.
  • If scoring were purely random, there would be fewer extreme blowouts. The data says otherwise.
LOTS OF ZEROS + A FEW SUPERSTARS

Draft Pick Value — Zero-Heavy with Winner-Take-All (Scouting Is Nearly Random)

About 40% of draft picks contribute almost nothing to their team over a career — essentially zero value. Then among the remaining players, a tiny handful at the very top produce a wildly outsized chunk of total value. If talent scouts were actually good at predicting success, you'd see a smooth decline from good to bad. Instead, there's a cliff from "star" to "bust" that screams: outside the top few picks, scouting barely beats a coin flip.

  • 4 out of 10 draft picks are essentially busts — and teams treat this as bad luck rather than a structural reality.
  • The top 5-10 picks produce more total career value than hundreds of later picks combined.
  • If scouting worked well, there would be a smooth gradient. The cliff proves it doesn't, past the obvious top tier.
  • That massive pile-up at zero isn't a fluke — it's the fundamental limit of predicting who'll succeed in pro sports.
BELL CURVE WITH SURPRISE EXTREMES

Point Spread Error — Fat-Tailed Bell (Blowouts Happen Way More Than Expected)

Betting lines (point spreads) are pretty accurate on average — the errors center right around zero. If you graphed prediction errors, you'd expect a nice symmetric bell. And it mostly is, except the edges are 2-3 times thicker than a normal bell would predict. Those thick edges are blowouts — games where one team mentally checks out and the score runs away. The middle of the prediction is fine; it's the extreme outcomes that are wildly underestimated.

  • On average, the betting market gets it right — errors cluster near zero, nicely balanced.
  • But extreme blowouts (20+ point margins of error) happen 2-3 times more often than the standard bell predicts.
  • This is why certain types of combination bets are profitable for bookmakers — they exploit the fact that bettors underestimate extreme outcomes.
  • Most bettors assume thin, well-behaved edges. The house knows the real edges are thick with surprises.
