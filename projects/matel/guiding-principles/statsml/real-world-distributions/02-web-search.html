Web Search / Ranking — Distribution Patterns

FEW WINNERS, ENDLESS LONG TAIL

What Everyone Searches For — Query Frequency (Zipf's Law)

Think of search queries like pop songs: a tiny handful of hits ("weather", "news") get played millions of times a day, while billions of obscure queries each get searched once or twice — ever. The popular ones are easy to optimize because you have tons of data. The rare ones? You're basically flying blind. And you can't use the same strategy for both.

  • Just 100 queries make up 20% of ALL search traffic — like the same 5 songs dominating every playlist
  • Billions of one-off queries exist where the search engine has almost nothing to learn from
  • You literally need two different systems: one for the "hits" and one for the weird stuff
  • If you plot this on a chart, it looks like a cliff followed by an endless flat desert
STEEP DROP-OFF BY POSITION

Position Creates Trust — Click-Through Rate (Exponential Decay)

Position #1 on a search engine gets ~30% of all clicks, position #2 gets ~15%, and so on — halving each time. Over time, users develop strong trust in their search engine's ranking — so they default to clicking position #1 with high probability. The engine earns this trust by consistently putting good results on top, which trains user behavior into a steep positional bias.

  • Clicks halve with each position (#1=30%, #2=15%, #3=8%...) — a strong positional habit
  • Users trust the engine's ranking, so position becomes a shortcut for relevance
  • This shape IS the entire ad revenue model — higher spot = more expensive
  • Same pattern across every search engine and marketplace — each builds its own trust curve
TWO CAMPS, NO MIDDLE GROUND

People Either Bounce or Stay — Dwell Time (Bimodal)

When someone clicks a search result, one of two things happens: they leave in 2-5 seconds (wrong page, misclick, or instant answer found), OR they stay for 30-120 seconds reading the content. Almost nobody spends 10-15 seconds — it's like a restaurant where people either walk in and immediately leave, or sit down for a full meal. There is no "casual browsing" middle ground. This means "average time on page" is a misleading number — it blends two completely different behaviors into mush.

  • Two types of users: "Nope, wrong page" (2-5 seconds) and "This is exactly what I needed" (30-120 seconds)
  • The 5-15 second zone is basically empty — nobody has "moderate interest"
  • Averaging these two groups together gives you a meaningless number — like averaging the temperature of fire and ice
  • You MUST separate these groups before you can use time-on-page as a quality signal
SAME QUIT RATE EVERY TIME

Each Search is a Coin Flip of "Found It?" — Queries Per Session (Geometric)

About 40% of search sessions end after just one query. Of those who try again, another 40% leave after query #2. And so on. It's like a coin flip after each search: "Did I find what I needed?" The drop-off rate is constant — each query has the same chance of being your last. Here's the paradox for search engines: better search means people find answers faster, which means fewer queries, which means less ad revenue. Oops.

  • The "give-up rate" is the same after every single search — like flipping the same coin each time
  • Steeper drop-off actually means the search engine is BETTER (people find answers faster)
  • Better search = fewer queries = fewer ad impressions = less money (the search engine paradox)
  • This constant rate proves that each query acts independently — your 5th search has the same chance of success as your 1st
TWO BEHAVIORS BLENDED TOGETHER

Quick Retry vs. New Topic — Time Between Searches (Mixture)

Look at the gap between one search and the next. You'll see two distinct patterns mixed together: a sharp spike at 3-8 seconds (that's someone frustrated, quickly rewording the same question), and a long, slow tail stretching to minutes (that's someone who finished one task and moved on to a new topic). The dividing line between "still looking for the same thing" and "moved on" is around 12 seconds — and that boundary is super useful for understanding when search failed.

  • Quick retry in 3-8 seconds = "That didn't work, let me rephrase" (frustration signal)
  • Long gap of 30+ seconds = "Done with that, now I'm looking up something else entirely"
  • The boundary between these two tells you where one search task ends and another begins
  • The percentage of fast retries directly measures how often search is FAILING people
FAST DROP-OFF WITH A RESEARCH BUMP

Most People Click Once, But Researchers Go Deep — Click Depth (Geometric with Tail)

More than half of all search sessions end after a single click — the person typed a brand name, got the website, done. Each additional click is about half as likely as the previous one. BUT there's a stubborn 8% of sessions where users dig 5+ pages deep — these are shoppers comparing options or students doing research. They're a completely different species from the one-click crowd. Optimizing for "average clicks" mashes these two groups together and helps neither.

  • 55% of sessions: one click and done (they knew exactly what they wanted, like typing a URL directly)
  • Each extra click halves in likelihood — most people give up quickly
  • 8% of sessions go 5+ pages deep — these are serious researchers or comparison shoppers
  • Mobile users give up even faster than desktop users (smaller screen = less patience)
  • Deep clickers are worth WAY more money — they're ready to buy or deeply engaged
SEARCH DEAD ENDS

When Search Comes Up Empty — Zero-Result Queries (Failure Spikes)

Sometimes you search for something and get literally zero results — a complete dead end. This doesn't happen evenly across all types of searches. Brand searches ("Nike") almost never fail. But try searching for a person the engine hasn't indexed, or badly misspell something, and you'll hit a wall 30-40% of the time. The pattern is stark: categories either almost never fail OR fail catastrophically. There's no middle ground — which means fixing zero-result rates means focusing on the specific problem categories, not broad improvements.

  • Brand/website searches: less than 1% come up empty (these are well-covered)
  • Product searches: 5-8% fail (gaps in the catalog)
  • People/place searches: 25-40% fail (the engine simply doesn't know about them)
  • Badly misspelled queries: 35%+ come up empty (spell-check can only fix small typos)
  • This failure rate is the clearest measure of "how good can this search engine possibly get?"
