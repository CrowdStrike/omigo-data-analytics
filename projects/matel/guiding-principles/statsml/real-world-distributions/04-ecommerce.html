E-Commerce & Retail — Distribution Patterns

Multi-Modal Purchase Amount ($X.99 Spikes)

Transaction amounts in e-commerce cluster around psychological price points ($9.99, $19.99, $29.99, $49.99) rather than spreading continuously. The result is a multi-modal distribution with sharp spikes superimposed on an exponential decay tail.

  • Psychological pricing creates discrete mass points that violate continuity assumptions
  • Continuous test failure: KS tests, kernel density estimates, and parametric fits all break on the spike structure
  • Multi-modal artifact: each price tier is a separate sub-population — averaging across them is meaningless
  • The Pareto tail from high-value orders further distorts variance-based summaries

Zero-Inflated Session Duration (Bounce Spike)

Web session durations show a massive spike near zero (bounces: users who leave within 5 seconds), a mode around 30-60 seconds for engaged visitors, and a long tail extending to 30+ minutes from users who leave tabs open.

  • Bounce rate creates zero-inflation: 30-70% of sessions are effectively zero-duration, making the distribution bimodal at minimum
  • "Left tab open" artifacts: long sessions often represent abandoned tabs, not engagement — these false long sessions inflate mean and variance
  • Filtering bounces vs. analyzing them requires different distributional assumptions
  • Log-transform does not fix the zero-inflation problem

Discrete + Spikes Items Per Cart

Cart sizes are discrete counts that superficially resemble a Poisson distribution, but with a dominant spike at 1 (single-item purchases), pack-size spikes at 6, 12, and 24, and a heavy tail from bulk buyers.

  • Pack-size spikes: products sold in packs of 6, 12, 24 create discrete mass points that no standard count model captures
  • Mean is misleading: average cart size of ~4 items describes almost nobody — most carts have 1-3 items, a few have 20+
  • Poisson assumption (mean = variance) fails badly due to overdispersion
  • Negative binomial is better but still misses the pack-size structure

Periodic + Exponential Days Between Purchases (Repeat Customers)

Inter-purchase intervals combine an exponential baseline (random shopping) with periodic spikes at 7, 14, and 30 days from subscription cycles and habitual weekly/monthly shopping behavior.

  • Subscription cycles: auto-replenishment at 30-day intervals creates sharp peaks invisible in summary statistics
  • Habit formation: weekly shoppers create a 7-day spike; biweekly creates a 14-day spike
  • Averaging hides periodicity: mean inter-purchase time of ~20 days completely obscures the multi-modal structure
  • Hazard rate is non-monotonic — standard survival models fail

Mixture of Betas Product Return Rate by Category

Return rates across products follow a Beta distribution, but with a clear bimodal structure: most products (electronics, home goods) cluster around 5-15% return rate, while fashion/apparel clusters around 30-40%.

  • Different categories, different behaviors: fashion has fundamentally higher return rates due to fit uncertainty — pooling all categories is invalid
  • A single Beta(a,b) fit misrepresents both sub-populations
  • Benchmarking a product's return rate against the overall mean penalizes fashion and rewards electronics unfairly
  • Category-stratified analysis is mandatory before any return-rate modeling
