Ride-sharing & Delivery — Distribution Patterns

SPIKE AT 1x, THEN STEP-LADDER BUMPS

Surge Multiplier — The Algorithm Picks Price Tiers, Not Supply & Demand

Most rides have no surge (big spike at 1.0×). When surge kicks in, it jumps to specific steps: 1.25×, 1.5×, 2.0× — not a smooth curve. This proves pricing isn't a free market — it's a set of pre-tested price tiers chosen because riders still accept them.

  • Massive spike at 1.0× — most of the time, no surge at all
  • Discrete bumps at 1.25×, 1.5×, 2.0× — the algorithm picks fixed tiers, not a smooth dial
  • If it were real supply-and-demand, you'd see a continuous curve — these steps prove it's designed
  • Each tier was psychologically tested: "at what multiplier do riders still tap Accept?"
LOPSIDED — LATE MUCH MORE THAN EARLY

ETA Error — Apps Deliberately Over-Promise

The app is almost never early, often on time, and frequently late. If the algorithm optimized for accuracy, errors would be symmetric (early and late equally). Instead, it over-promises because showing a shorter wait time gets more people to place orders — even if drivers arrive late.

  • Peak at 0 (on time) — but almost never early
  • Heavy late tail — drivers are often 5-15 minutes behind the estimate
  • The lopsidedness proves the app optimizes for order volume, not accuracy
  • A truly accurate model would be equally wrong in both directions
TWO HUMPS WITH A GAP

Driver Idle Time — You're Either Pre-Matched or Stuck in the Queue

Drivers either get their next ride within 30 seconds (the app matched them during the previous trip) or wait 10-20 minutes in the general queue. Almost nobody waits 1-5 minutes — there's a dead zone in between. The algorithm is binary: either you got lucky with pre-matching, or you didn't.

  • Spike at 0-30 seconds = pre-matched while still finishing the last ride
  • Dead zone at 1-5 minutes = nobody lands here, it's algorithmically impossible
  • Broad hill at 10-20 min = competing in the general queue with everyone else
  • Two separate experiences: instant dispatch vs. waiting — no middle ground
TWO HILLS — TWO DIFFERENT PRODUCTS

Trip Distance — Short Hops and Long Hauls Are Completely Different Businesses

One cluster at 1-3 miles (quick errands, bar hops) and another at 8-15 miles (airport runs, cross-town commutes). These aren't the same product — short trips are impulse decisions where riders don't check the price. Long trips are planned, and riders compare alternatives. The valley between them is where people think "should I just drive myself?"

  • First hill at 1-3 miles = "I don't feel like walking/parking" — impulse, price-insensitive
  • Second hill at 8-15 miles = planned trips where riders compare cost to driving/transit
  • Valley between = the distance where riders switch from "just get me there" to "is this worth it?"
  • One platform serving two fundamentally different customer needs
SPIKES WHERE THE BUTTONS ARE

Tip Amount — People Don't Decide Tips, They Pick Buttons

30% leave no tip. The rest cluster exactly at 15%, 20%, and 25% of the fare — with almost nothing in between. Nobody is doing math. They're tapping whichever pre-set button feels right. The platform controls average tip behavior just by changing which buttons it shows.

  • 30% at $0 = no tip at all
  • Sharp spikes at exactly 15%, 20%, 25% = these are the three buttons on screen
  • Almost nothing between spikes — nobody types a custom amount
  • Change the button defaults and you change tipping behavior overnight
