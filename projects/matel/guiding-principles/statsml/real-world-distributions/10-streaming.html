Streaming & Recommendations — Distribution Patterns

WINNER-TAKE-ALL SHAPE

Play Counts — Most Content Gets Ignored (Power Law)

Think of it like a popularity contest where a tiny handful of hits hog almost all the attention, and millions of songs or episodes sit in a forgotten corner. People assumed the internet would help niche content thrive — turns out, recommendation algorithms do the opposite. They keep funneling everyone toward the same top hits, making the rich richer.

  • The top 1% of content gobbles up about 90% of all plays — the rest fight over scraps
  • Millions of tracks have fewer than 100 plays in their entire lifetime — basically invisible
  • The "hidden gems will find their audience online" idea turned out to be wishful thinking
  • Algorithms concentrate attention even more than old-school record stores ever did
HIGH AT EDGES, DIP IN MIDDLE

Time to Abandon — You Have 2 Minutes to Hook a Viewer (Bathtub Shape)

People don't slowly lose interest — they make a snap judgment in the first 2 minutes: stay or leave. After that initial cliff, the viewers who remain are committed and mostly stick around. Then at the very end of an episode, there's another bump of drop-offs. That opening window is everything — it's why shows are designed to start with a bang, not a slow build.

  • The first 2 minutes are a binary "I'm in" or "I'm out" decision — not a gentle fade
  • Once someone survives the opening, they're basically locked in for the rest
  • There's a spike at the intro timestamp — people without auto-skip bail there
  • 120 seconds is the designed hook window that content creators obsess over
BUNCHED UP WITH A HARD WALL

Genre Diversity — Your Brain Caps Out at ~5 Genres (Left-Skewed)

Even with unlimited options, most people stick to 3-5 genres and basically never break past 8-10. This isn't the algorithm failing to show variety — it's a brain thing. Humans can only actively care about a handful of interest areas at once, like how you can juggle about 5 close friendships but not 50. No amount of "discover new genres!" nudges changes this ceiling.

  • Most users cluster tightly around 3-5 genres — that's the sweet spot for human attention
  • There's a hard wall at 8-10 genres that almost nobody breaks through, no matter how big the catalog
  • This isn't a broken recommendation system — it's a built-in limit of how brains work
  • Every streaming platform sees this same wall, regardless of how hard they push diversity
HALVING PATTERN WITH A SURPRISE TAIL

Binge Length — Cliffhangers Are Measurably Addictive (Geometric + Fat Tail)

If watching episodes were like flipping a coin — "watch another? heads yes, tails no" — you'd expect each episode to have half the viewers of the last. And for the first few episodes, that's exactly what happens. But then something weird shows up: way more people watch 5, 6, 7+ episodes than the coin-flip model predicts. That extra "I can't stop" tail? That's the cliffhanger effect, and you can actually measure how addictive a show is by how fat that tail gets.

  • The basic pattern is like a coin flip: half the people stop after each episode
  • The surprise fat tail (episodes 5+) = people who got hooked and can't stop watching
  • The gap between "expected stops" and "actual stops" literally measures addictiveness
  • Shows without compelling hooks follow the coin-flip decay perfectly — no fat tail at all
SHAPE CREATED BY THE BUTTON DESIGN

Rating Distribution — The Rating System Creates Its Own Shape (UI Artifact)

Here's a fun twist: the pattern of ratings you see has almost nothing to do with content quality — it's created by the rating buttons themselves. Give people 5 stars and they pile onto 1 and 5 (a J-shape). Give them thumbs up/down and 90%+ go positive. Give them 10 points and they cluster at 1, 7, and 10 (three humps). One major streaming platform switched from stars to thumbs specifically because the J-shape made average content invisible.

  • 5-star systems create a J-shape — almost nobody picks 2 or 3, they slam 1 or 5
  • Thumbs up/down systems produce 90%+ positive ratings — negativity requires more effort
  • 10-point scales produce three humps at 1, 7, and 10 — the middle scores feel pointless to users
  • The shape tells you about the UI design, not about how good the content actually is
