Plaid & Open Banking BACKLOG

Bank accounts, balances, transactions with merchant and category enrichment, income, liabilities, and investment holdings — accessed under revocable end-user consent, via aggregation in the US and a regulated standard in the UK and EU.

Last verified: August 2026

Overview

What It Provides
  • Accounts & Balance — account list with type, subtype, mask, and current/available balance. /accounts/balance/get forces a live refresh at the institution; /accounts/get may return cached values.
  • Transactions — posted and pending transactions with amount, date, description, and Plaid's enrichment: normalised merchant name, category, payment channel, and location.
  • Income — employment and payroll-derived income, either from transaction inference or from a direct payroll connection.
  • Liabilities — loan and credit-card detail: APR, minimum payment, next due date, remaining principal.
  • Investments — holdings, securities, and investment transactions (buys, sells, dividends, fees).
  • Identity — account-holder name, address, phone, email as held by the bank.
Authentication & Scopes
  • Item model — one Item is one user's login at one institution. Link produces a public_token; you exchange it for a long-lived access_token scoped to that Item.
  • Products are declared at Link time and the consent the user granted is what constrains you. Requesting a product later can require re-authentication.
  • Server credentials — client_id and secret in the request body, per environment (sandbox / production).
  • Revocable. The user can revoke at Plaid, at the bank's own consent dashboard, or simply change their password. Your access ends without you doing anything wrong.
Consent expiry is a first-class data problem, not an ops problem. Items decay: ITEM_LOGIN_REQUIRED and PENDING_EXPIRATION arrive continuously, and the population that re-authenticates is not a random sample of your users. Any longitudinal metric computed over "users we still have bank data for" is conditioned on survival of consent.
Granularity

Individual transaction, dated to the day. There is no reliable intraday timestamp; datetime and authorized_datetime are populated only by some institutions. Transaction history depth at initial connection varies by institution — often around two years, sometimes far less — and is not something you can request more of.

Key Endpoints
  • POST /link/token/create, POST /item/public_token/exchange
  • POST /accounts/get, POST /accounts/balance/get
  • POST /transactions/sync — cursor-based; the correct endpoint for ongoing ingestion
  • POST /transactions/get — date-window based; older pattern
  • POST /liabilities/get, POST /investments/holdings/get, POST /investments/transactions/get
  • POST /item/remove — deletes the Item and stops billing; do this when a user disconnects
Rate Limits

Plaid rate-limits per Item per endpoint, and the binding constraint is usually a small number of calls per Item in a short window rather than a large global quota. The mechanism to plan around: do not poll. Use /transactions/sync driven by the SYNC_UPDATES_AVAILABLE webhook, which tells you when there is actually something new. Polling every Item on a fixed schedule will hit per-Item limits while wasting most calls on unchanged data.

Webhooks
  • SYNC_UPDATES_AVAILABLE — new or modified transactions for an Item.
  • INITIAL_UPDATE / HISTORICAL_UPDATE — first-pull completion; historical backfill lands after the initial window.
  • ITEM: ERROR, PENDING_EXPIRATION, USER_PERMISSION_REVOKED — consent lifecycle.
  • TRANSACTIONS_REMOVED — a transaction you already stored no longer exists.
Business Scenarios
  • Underwriting and affordability — cashflow-based lending decisions from income and expense patterns.
  • Personal financial management — budgeting and categorised spend.
  • Account verification for ACH — confirm ownership and routing details before debiting.
  • Subscription detection — recurring-charge identification for cancellation tooling.
  • Net-worth aggregation — combining depository, credit, loan, and investment balances.
Key Restrictions & Gotchas
  • Merchant and category are model output, not bank data. The bank sends a raw descriptor string like SQ *BLUE BOTTLE 41 SAN FRANCISC. Plaid's merchant_name, personal_finance_category, and logo_url are the result of parsing and classifying that string. They carry an error rate, that error rate is not uniform across merchants, and it changes when the model is updated.
  • Enrichment is retroactively mutable. A model update can recategorise transactions you ingested months ago. If you snapshot categories and never re-read them, your history and Plaid's history will diverge; if you always take the latest, your historical aggregates will change under you. Both are defensible — but you must pick one and say which.
  • Pending transactions are replaced, not updated. A pending transaction gets a new transaction_id when it posts, linked by pending_transaction_id. Transaction IDs are therefore not stable across the pending-to-posted transition, and naive upserts on transaction ID double-count.
  • A pending transaction can change amount or vanish entirely. Authorisation holds are estimates — a restaurant pre-auth settles higher with tip, a fuel hold settles far lower, and an abandoned authorisation simply disappears without ever posting. Treating pending amounts as spend overstates some categories and invents transactions that never happened.
  • Transactions can be removed. Banks reverse and re-post. Your store must handle deletes, not just inserts.
  • Amount sign conventions differ by account type and are a common source of silently inverted spend figures. Verify against a real statement before trusting any aggregate.
  • No merchant identity across institutions is guaranteed to be stable enough to key on. Use it for display and grouping, not as a primary key.
  • Balance is a point-in-time read, not a series. There is no historical daily-balance endpoint. If you want a balance time series you must sample it yourself, going forward, and you cannot backfill it.
  • Investments do not give you cost basis history reliably, and institution coverage for investment products is materially thinner than for depository accounts.
UK / EU: Open Banking as a Standard

The regulatory model differs from US aggregation in ways that matter analytically:

  • Mandated APIs. Under PSD2 and the UK Open Banking standard, banks must expose account information (AIS) and payment initiation (PIS) interfaces. Coverage is a legal obligation, not a scraping outcome, so field availability is more consistent.
  • Licensed access. You must be an authorised or registered AISP/PISP, or operate as an agent of one. This is a regulatory gate, not a signup form.
  • Consent has a statutory clock. Consent must be re-confirmed periodically — historically every 90 days, with later SCA rules relaxing re-authentication for pure AIS in some jurisdictions. Either way, consent is time-bounded by regulation, so attrition is structural.
  • No enrichment in the standard. The regulated APIs deliver the bank's own transaction data. Merchant cleaning and categorisation are added by the aggregator on top — so the model-output caveat applies identically, just from a different vendor.
The single most consequential error in this domain is writing WHERE category = 'Restaurants' and reading the result as a fact about the user. It is a fact about a classifier's output on a truncated bank descriptor. Any conclusion drawn from it inherits the classifier's error, and if that error correlates with anything you care about — merchant size, region, card network — the bias is not noise, it is confounding.
