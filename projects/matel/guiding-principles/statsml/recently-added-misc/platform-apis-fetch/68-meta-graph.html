Meta Graph & Instagram BACKLOG

One versioned HTTP graph over Facebook Pages and Instagram Business accounts. Reporting on assets you control works well; the research-facing surface has contracted repeatedly, and each contraction is a changepoint in any time series that crosses it.

Last verified: August 2026

Overview

What It Provides

Everything is one graph: nodes (a Page, a media object, a comment), edges between them, and fields on each node. A request names a node, an optional edge, and a field list; nested field expansion lets one call return a subtree, and batch requests bundle several calls into one HTTP round trip. Paths are versioned (/vXX.0/...) and each version has a published sunset date, after which calls against it fail.

  • Facebook Page surface — Page metadata, published posts, comments and replies, reactions, and Page Insights: impressions, reach, engaged users, follower counts, video views, and demographic breakdowns of followers by age, gender, country and city.
  • Instagram surface — for Business and Creator accounts linked to a Facebook Page: media objects, comments and replies, mentions and tags, stories, hashtag search, and Insights covering reach, impressions, saves, profile views, follows, video views, plus audience demographics.
  • Webhooks — change notifications for feed, comments and mentions, so ingestion is push rather than poll.

Authentication & Scopes

  • Token types — user tokens (from Facebook Login), Page access tokens (issued to a Page admin), app tokens, and system-user tokens from Business Manager for server jobs that must not expire when an employee leaves.
  • Page-scoped user IDs (PSIDs) — the same human has a different ID in different apps. Identity is deliberately not portable across apps, so you cannot join your Meta data to another platform's on an ID.
  • Instagram requires a Business or Creator account. A personal profile is not readable through this surface at all.

Access Control Is The Real Bottleneck

App Review, not rate limiting, is what stops most projects. Non-trivial permissions require App Review with a written use case and a screencast; Business Verification requires legal entity documents; some data additionally requires a data-use checkup or annual re-attestation. Until you hold advanced access, permissions work only for users who hold a role on your app — which is why local prototypes succeed and production launches fail.

Granularity — And One Asymmetry

Content metrics are per-object; audience data is aggregate only. You can get reach and saves for a specific media object. You get follower demographics as bucketed counts — by age band, by gender, by country, by city. You never get a list of your followers with attributes attached. There is no follower enumeration with demographics, at any access tier. Any analysis needing individual-level audience covariates is not possible here, and no amount of review approval changes that.
  • Insights arrive as name / period / values triples on a separate edge, in windows Meta chose (day, week, 28-day, lifetime) — not arbitrary ranges. The event stream underneath is not recoverable.
  • Demographic breakdowns are suppressed below a minimum audience size, returning empty arrays rather than small counts. Small accounts and small segments therefore go missing-not-at-random, and pooled averages skew toward large accounts.

Negative Findings — Things People Assume Work

  • No personal-profile friend data. The v2.x deprecations removed general friend-list access. You can see only friends who also use your app; taggable_friends and similar survivors are narrow and gated. There is no social-graph extraction.
  • Instagram Basic Display API is deprecated. The route for personal (non-business) Instagram accounts to expose their own media to a third-party app has closed. Consumer-account media access is effectively gone; Business and Creator accounts via the Instagram Graph API, or Instagram API with Instagram Login, are what remain.
  • No arbitrary public-content search. Public post search was removed long ago. Instagram hashtag search exists but is scoped and capped — a limited number of distinct hashtags can be queried per user within a rolling window — and it is not a firehose.
  • CrowdTangle is shut down. The tool a large body of published research relied on was retired in August 2024, so it no longer exists as a route to public Facebook and Instagram post monitoring. Meta directs former users to the Meta Content Library and its API — but that is not a like-for-like swap. Eligibility is restricted to approved researchers at qualifying academic and non-profit institutions, applied for through an external research administrator, and analysis happens inside a provisioned research environment rather than by pulling data into your own warehouse. Commercial brand monitoring, which CrowdTangle was informally used for, has no successor at all. Treat any Facebook or Instagram public-post pipeline described before 2024 as non-reproducible today.

Rate Limits

Described by mechanism, because the numbers are dynamic and revised between versions:

  • Platform rate limits are computed per app over a rolling window, and the allowance scales with your app's user base — a new app has a small budget regardless of willingness to pay.
  • Business use case limits are separate budgets per business or asset and per use case (Pages, Instagram, Ads), reported back on every response in usage headers as percentages of call count, CPU time and total time.
  • Per-edge caps apply to some edges, hashtag search most notably, with their own windows and object-count rules; page size also affects cost.
  • The durable engineering answer is to treat the usage-percentage headers as the source of truth and back off on percentage, not on an HTTP error alone.

Business Scenarios

  • Owned-channel reporting for Pages and IG accounts you or your client administers — the sanctioned case, and it works well
  • Social listening restricted to your own assets: mentions, tags, comments on your content
  • Creator and influencer performance measurement, with the creator's consent and token
  • Ad-adjacent organic benchmarking alongside Marketing API spend and delivery data
  • Community management: comment and mention triage via webhooks, replies through the API

Key Restrictions & Gotchas

  • Versions sunset on a clock. An integration is a maintenance commitment. Field and metric names change across versions.
  • Metric definitions are not stable. Meta has revised reach and impressions methodology within the API's life, and consolidated engagement metrics under new names. A level shift with no behaviour change is a real and common outcome.
  • The visible corpus is survivorship-biased. Deleted posts, moderator-removed comments, policy takedowns and accounts gone private are absent. Removal correlates with exactly the toxic, spam or crisis content most analyses are about, so prevalence estimated from what remains is biased downward.
  • Hidden and filtered comments may not appear on the comment edge, so "share of negative comments" is measured after the platform has already cleaned the sample.
  • No retroactive archive. Insights windows are bounded. If you did not collect it, it is gone.
  • Deprecated permission traps. A long-lived token can carry a scope that no longer grants anything, failing silently as empty results.

The Analytical Centrepiece: Access Policy Creates Discontinuities

Social platform APIs contracted sharply, and that contraction breaks longitudinal research. The failure mode is specific. If your time series spans an access-policy change — a permission moved behind App Review, an endpoint sunset, a metric definition revised, a research tool withdrawn — then the series contains a discontinuity that is an artifact of data availability, not of the underlying phenomenon. The measured population changed; the measured quantity changed; the sampling frame changed. Users did not.

Do not attribute such a break to real-world change. The practical mitigation is bookkeeping, not statistics: record the API version, the permission set, and the metric definition alongside every extract. Then treat version and policy boundaries as known changepoints declared in advance — segment or model around them — rather than letting a changepoint detector rediscover them and hand you a "finding" about user behaviour. A changepoint at a date you can look up in a changelog is not a result.
