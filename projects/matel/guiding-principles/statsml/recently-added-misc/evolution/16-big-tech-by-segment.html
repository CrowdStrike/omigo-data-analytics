Big Tech by Technology Segment

Grouped by what the product actually is, not by who owns it — several companies appear in more than one segment, which is the point.

Dates are approximate and mark mainstream adoption rather than first release. Bars in red ended as mainstream practice; orange survives in specific niches only.

The Eras

EraWhat changed
1994 – 2001
Portals: one front door to the webgone
Yahoo, AOL, Excite, Lycos, Netscape, MSN
The assumption was that the web needed a curated entrance, so the winners would be whoever owned the home page. Directories were compiled by hand and the metric was page views. Search was one feature on a crowded portal rather than the product itself.
1995 – present
E-commerce: catalogue, then marketplace, then logistics
Amazon, eBay, Alibaba, Flipkart, Shopify, Temu
Selling online moved through three distinct businesses. First an online catalogue, then a marketplace where third parties carried the inventory risk, then a logistics and fulfilment operation where delivery speed was the actual product. Each shift changed what the company was, not just what it sold.
1996 – present
Email: from paid accounts to identity infrastructure
Hotmail, Yahoo Mail, Gmail, Outlook, Superhuman
Free webmail broke email's tie to an internet provider, so an address became portable and permanent. Storage capacity was the headline competitive metric until it stopped mattering. The lasting consequence was structural rather than technical: an email address became the login and recovery mechanism for everything else, which is a far stronger position than a mail product.
1998 – present
Search: answering the query instead of listing the web
Google, Baidu, Yandex, Bing, DuckDuckGo
Ranking by what other pages linked to beat human curation as scale grew, and the intent expressed in a query turned out to be far more valuable to advertisers than undirected browsing. Search became the segment that funded expansion into every other segment, which is exactly why grouping by company hides more than it shows.
2003 – present
Social networking: the graph as the product
Friendster, MySpace, Orkut, Facebook, LinkedIn, VK
The stored list of who knows whom became the asset. Content arrived because of a relationship rather than a search, which made the feed the most valuable screen in software. Early leaders lost on reliability and on failing to control the feed's ranking rather than on features.
2005 – present
Video: upload, then subscribe, then recommend
YouTube, Netflix, Hulu, Twitch, Disney+
Bandwidth made user-uploaded video viable, then streaming replaced the disc, then licensing costs pushed the platforms into producing their own content. Live streaming split off as its own segment with a different economic model built on direct viewer payment.
2005 – present
Maps and the physical world
Google Maps, Waze, Apple Maps, Uber, Didi, DoorDash
Digitising streets, then adding live traffic, then routing people and goods in real time. Mapping turned out to be the substrate for an entire category of businesses that dispatch physical things, which is why the map itself is rarely the revenue source.
2006 – present
Cloud and enterprise platforms
AWS, Azure, Google Cloud, Salesforce, Snowflake, Databricks
Selling infrastructure and business software by consumption rather than by licence. This segment quietly became the profit engine funding consumer products elsewhere in the same companies, and it is where the competitive positions are most durable because switching costs are highest.
2010 – present
Mobile-native sharing: camera first
Instagram, Snapchat, WhatsApp, Pinterest, Telegram
Products designed for a phone rather than adapted to one. The camera became the primary input and ephemerality became a feature rather than a limitation. Messaging in particular concentrated to near-utility status in many countries, where one application effectively became the phone network.
2016 – present
Recommendation over connection
TikTok, Reels, Shorts, Kuaishou
The feed stopped being assembled from your connections and became whatever a model predicted you would watch. This decoupled reach from follower count, which meant a new account could reach millions immediately — the first serious break in the social graph's advantage since it was established.
2019 – present
Model providers as a segment
OpenAI, Anthropic, Google DeepMind, Meta AI, Mistral, Hugging Face
Capability sold as a metered interface rather than as software you install. The segment is unusual in that its main input is compute and data rather than headcount, and the same handful of chip and cloud suppliers sit underneath every competitor in it.
1960s – 1980s
Hardware vendors, software as an accessorygone
IBM, DEC, Fujitsu, Burroughs
The dominant companies sold machines, and software came bundled to make the hardware worth buying. IBM's position rested on the mainframe and the service contract around it. Nobody's main business was selling software by itself.
1980s – 1990s
The operating system and the office suite
Microsoft, Intel, Apple, Lotus, Novell, Adobe
Software separated from hardware and became the more profitable half. Controlling the operating system meant controlling which applications existed, and the office file format became the real lock-in — documents outlived the software that made them, so compatibility mattered more than features.

What Disappeared

Ended as mainstream practice:
  • Hand-compiled web directories — Yahoo's original business, made unworkable by the web's growth rate
  • The portal as the web's front door — the assumption that users wanted a curated entrance rather than a query box
  • Dial-up providers as gatekeepers — AOL bundled access, content and identity; broadband unbundled all three
  • Standalone instant messaging clients — ICQ, AIM and MSN Messenger, each with tens of millions of users
  • Paid email accounts tied to your provider — changing internet provider once meant changing address
  • Storage size as a marketing claim — mailbox capacity was a headline competitive number until it became irrelevant
  • Buying software in a box — the retail software aisle, and the version-upgrade cycle that went with it
Alive, but only in niches:
  • Blogging platforms and RSS readers — displaced from the mainstream by feeds, still the backbone of independent publishing and podcasting
  • Forums and message boards — still dominant for specialist communities where a searchable archive matters more than a feed
  • Regional platform leaders — VK, Line, Kakao and Naver hold positions in single markets that global platforms never took
  • Desktop email clients — still standard in enterprises, largely abandoned by consumers
