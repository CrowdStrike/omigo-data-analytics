Evolution of Neural Networks & Deep Learning

Three waves of the same idea, separated by two winters — and what changed each time to make it work.

Dates are approximate and mark mainstream adoption rather than first release. Bars in red ended as mainstream practice; orange survives in specific niches only.

The Eras

EraWhat changed
1969 – 1986
The first wintergone
Funding and interest withdrawn
Work continued in a few places, but neural networks were widely considered a closed question. The gap matters for understanding the field: the ideas were not missing, the training method and the compute were.
1986 – late 1990s
Backpropagation
Multi-layer perceptrons, sigmoid activations
Efficiently propagating error backwards through layers made multi-layer training practical, and LeNet showed convolutional networks reading handwritten digits at scale. Then progress stalled: deeper networks suffered vanishing gradients, data was scarce, and SVMs offered better results with stronger theory. Neural networks went out of fashion a second time.
2006 – 2012
Pretraining and the return of depthgone
Deep belief nets, layer-wise pretraining, autoencoders
Training deep networks one layer at a time made depth tractable and revived the field's credibility. The technique itself was superseded within a few years by better initialisation, ReLU activations and dropout — but it re-established that depth was the direction.
2012 onward
Convolutional networks and the ImageNet moment
AlexNet, VGG, ResNet, GPU training
A large convolutional network trained on GPUs cut the ImageNet error rate dramatically and settled the argument. Three things had converged that were absent in earlier waves: labelled data at web scale, GPU compute, and practical tricks for deep training. Residual connections then made networks of hundreds of layers trainable.
2014 – 2020
Sequences and gated memory
RNN, LSTM, GRU, seq2seq, attention as an add-on
Recurrent networks with gating carried state across time steps, driving machine translation and speech recognition. They were inherently sequential and therefore hard to parallelise, and long-range dependencies remained difficult — both problems the transformer solved directly.
2014 onward
Learning to generate
GANs, VAEs, then diffusion
Adversarial training pitted a generator against a discriminator and produced the first convincing synthetic images, though training was notoriously unstable. Diffusion models — learning to reverse a gradual noising process — later proved more stable and controllable, and became the basis of current image generation.
2017 onward
Attention replaces recurrence
Transformers, self-attention, positional encoding
Dispensing with recurrence entirely in favour of attention allowed every position to attend to every other in parallel. This made training on very large corpora feasible and turned out to be architecture-agnostic across modalities: the same design now handles text, images, audio and protein sequences.
2018 onward
Pretraining, then scale as strategy
BERT, self-supervision, scaling laws
Learning from unlabelled data by predicting masked or next tokens removed the labelling bottleneck. Empirical scaling relationships between parameters, data and compute then turned research into an engineering programme: predictable gains from more of everything, which is what made the current generation of models an investment decision rather than a discovery.
1950s – 1960s
The perceptrongone
Single layer, learned weights
A simple learning rule adjusted weights on inputs to classify patterns, and it genuinely learned from examples. Its limitation was proven rather than suspected: a single layer cannot represent XOR. Published in 1969, that result — combined with no method for training multiple layers — largely halted the field.

What Disappeared

Ended as mainstream practice:
  • Single-layer perceptrons — provably unable to represent simple non-linear functions
  • Sigmoid and tanh in deep hidden layers — replaced by ReLU and variants, which do not saturate the gradient
  • Layer-wise unsupervised pretraining — the technique that revived deep learning, obsolete within about five years
  • Recurrent networks for long sequences — LSTMs were state of the art for translation, almost entirely displaced by transformers
  • GANs for high-fidelity image synthesis — largely superseded by diffusion for stability and controllability
  • Task-specific architectures — a bespoke model per problem, replaced by pretraining one model and adapting it
Alive, but only in niches:
  • Convolutional networks — still preferred where efficiency matters, as in on-device vision and medical imaging
  • LSTMs — still reasonable for short sequences and low-resource settings where transformer overhead is unjustified
  • Reinforcement learning — never mainstream for business problems, central to game playing, robotics and model alignment
