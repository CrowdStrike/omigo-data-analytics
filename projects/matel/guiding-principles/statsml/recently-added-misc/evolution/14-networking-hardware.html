Evolution of Networking & Data Movement

How fast we can move data, and why moving it is now harder than computing on it.

Dates are approximate and mark when each phase became common rather than when it first appeared. Bars in red ended as everyday practice; an arrow at the right edge means the phase is still ongoing. Speeds are quoted in bits per second — megabits (Mbps) and gigabits (Gbps).

A yardstick for the whole page: take one movie-sized file, a few gigabytes. On 1990s dial-up it would need over a week of unbroken connection. On an early office network, a day. On a gigabit link today, under a minute. That is roughly a millionfold change across a single working lifetime — and it is why things like streaming, video calls and cloud backup did not need inventing so much as waiting for.

The Big Jumps

PhaseWhat changed
1960s – 1990s
Dial-up: measured in kilobitsgone
Modems over telephone lines
The computer made a phone call to get online, and the connection ran at kilobits — thousands of bits per second. A single photograph took minutes to arrive and you watched it appear in strips. Moving a movie-sized file would have taken more than a week of continuous connection, so it simply was not a thing anyone did. The line was busy while you were online, and you paid by the minute.
1980s – 1990s
One shared wire for the whole officegone
Coaxial Ethernet at 10 Mbps
Offices got their own network, and every machine shared a single cable running at 10 megabits. Because it was shared, the speed was split between everyone using it — and if two machines spoke at once, both messages were spoilt and had to be resent. Adding more computers made the network slower for everybody, which is the opposite of how people expect capacity to work.
Mid 1990s
The switch: everyone gets their own lane
Switched Ethernet, 100 Mbps then 1 Gbps
This was the jump that mattered most. Instead of one shared cable, the switch gave every machine its own dedicated lane, so adding computers no longer slowed anyone down — and collisions disappeared entirely. Speeds then climbed from 10 megabits to 100, then to a gigabit: roughly a hundredfold in about a decade. That movie-sized file went from a week to under a minute.
Mid 1990s onward
Glass instead of copper between cities
Fibre optic cable, many colours of light at once
Long distances moved from electrical signals in copper to pulses of light in glass strands thinner than a hair. The clever part came next: a single strand can carry many separate channels simultaneously by using different colours of light, multiplying its capacity many times over without laying new cable. So much long-distance capacity was built that the price of moving data between continents collapsed — which is the reason streaming video and cloud storage are affordable rather than luxuries.
Around 2000
Homes get always-on connections
Broadband, then fibre to the home
Broadband replaced dialling in with a connection that was simply always there. Going online stopped being something you started and finished and became the default state of the house. Speeds went from kilobits to megabits, and later to a gigabit on fibre — roughly ten thousand times the dial-up era. One quirk persists: home connections are deliberately lopsided, far faster at receiving than sending, on the old assumption that people mostly consume rather than publish. Video calls and cloud backups have made that assumption look increasingly dated.
2000s onward
Cutting the cable, then the whole building
Wi-Fi, and mobile from 2G to 5G
Wi-Fi removed the cable from the device, and each mobile generation added roughly ten times the speed of the last — from barely enough for text on 2G, to photographs on 3G, to video on 4G. For a great many people worldwide the phone became not just the main computer but the only internet connection they ever had, which changed what products had to be designed for.
2007 onward
Inside the data centre, the traffic turns sideways
10, 40 and 100 Gbps between servers
Once applications were split across many machines, most traffic stopped going in and out of the building and started going between servers inside it. Networks were rebuilt so any two machines could talk at full speed, at 10 then 40 then 100 gigabits. This is what made modern cloud architecture possible: splitting a system across hundreds of machines only works if talking between them is nearly free.
2009 onward
Storage stops being the slow part
Flash and SSDs replace spinning disks
Hard disks had moving parts, so reading data meant physically waiting for a head to travel and a platter to spin — thousands of times slower than the machine could think. Flash memory has no moving parts and removed that wait almost entirely. The effect was to move the bottleneck somewhere else: with storage fast, the connections carrying data around the machine became the slow link.
2015 onward
Moving data now costs more than computing on it
400 and 800 Gbps links, dedicated chip-to-chip fabrics
Training large AI models spreads the work over thousands of chips that must constantly compare notes with each other. The surprising result is that these chips often sit idle waiting for data to arrive rather than running out of calculating power. So the link between them — now 400 to 800 gigabits, with special direct chip-to-chip connections — has become as important as the chips themselves. After decades of computing being the expensive part and moving data being cheap, that relationship has flipped.
