Evolution of the Internet & Communication

From dial-up bulletin boards to always-connected feeds — how people found each other and published to each other.

Dates are approximate and mark mainstream adoption rather than first release. Bars in red ended as mainstream practice; orange survives in specific niches only.

The Eras

EraWhat changed
1971 onward
Email
SMTP, mailing lists
Store-and-forward messaging predates the web and has outlasted every intended replacement. It is federated, requires no shared platform, and remains the default identity for logging into other services — which is a large part of why it persists despite being widely disliked.
1993 – 2000s
Publishing, then finding
Static sites, portals, then search engines
The early web was documents linked by hand, navigated through curated directories that stopped scaling as page counts grew. Link-analysis search replaced browsing a hierarchy with querying an index, which changed the fundamental act from navigating to asking, and made discoverability the central concern for anyone publishing.
1999 – 2000s
Anyone can publish
Blogs, forums, RSS, wikis
Publishing tools removed the need to write HTML, and RSS let readers subscribe to sources directly without an intermediary deciding order. This was the period when the reading list was assembled by the reader rather than selected by a platform.
2004 onward
Organised around people
Facebook, LinkedIn, Twitter
The social graph became the organising structure: content arrived because of who you were connected to rather than which topic you had sought out. Real identity became the norm, and the reverse-chronological feed established the pattern of an endless stream rather than discrete pages.
2008 onward
The phone becomes the primary device
App stores, mobile web, push notifications
Smartphones plus app stores shifted access from a machine one sat down at to a device carried continuously. Notifications inverted the initiative — applications began contacting users rather than waiting to be opened — and always-on connectivity made continuous rather than sessional use the norm.
2010 onward
Images, then disappearing content
Instagram, Snapchat, Stories
Cheap cameras and bandwidth made images the primary unit of sharing. Snapchat's contribution was ephemerality: content that expired removed the sense of a permanent record and encouraged frequent, casual posting, a pattern every major platform subsequently copied.
2016 onward
Algorithmic short video
TikTok, Reels, Shorts, YouTube
Feeds shifted from showing what your connections posted to showing what a recommendation model predicted you would watch, which decoupled reach from follower count entirely. Short vertical video with strong recommendation became the dominant consumption format, and the graph stopped being the main determinant of what anyone saw.
2023 onward
Synthetic video as its own format
Generated clips, micro-episodes, vertical drama
Generation models made moving images producible without a camera, a crew or a location, which changed what lengths were economic. Two formats emerged that had no real precedent: episodes running a minute or two, short enough to consume in a feed yet carrying a continuing narrative, and hour-long vertical dramas assembled from generated or heavily AI-assisted footage. Production cost stopped scaling with runtime, so quantity is no longer limited by budget or shooting schedule — the binding constraint moved to audience attention and to distinguishing generated footage from recorded footage.
Late 1970s – 1990s
Dialling into a single machinegone
BBS, modems, Usenet, IRC
Before ubiquitous internet access, one dialled directly into a bulletin board — often one user at a time, frequently charged by the minute. Usenet provided distributed topic-based discussion and IRC provided real-time chat. The organising principle was the topic, not the person: you joined a board about a subject, and identity was secondary.
Late 1990s – 2000s
Presence and real-time contactgone
ICQ, AIM, MSN Messenger, Yahoo Messenger
The buddy list introduced the idea of knowing who was available before contacting them. These clients were enormously popular and then vanished almost completely, their function absorbed first by social platforms and then by phone-based messaging apps.

What Disappeared

Ended as mainstream practice:
  • Dial-up bulletin boards — single-line systems reached by modem, and per-minute connection charges
  • Standalone instant messaging clients — ICQ, AIM and MSN Messenger, each with tens of millions of users, all discontinued
  • Web directories — hand-curated hierarchical catalogues of the web, made unworkable by scale
  • Flash-based interactive content — a large share of web games and animation, removed from browsers
  • Dial-up tones and connecting as a discrete act — going online was something you started and finished
  • The purely chronological feed — still available in places, but no longer the default on major platforms
Alive, but only in niches:
  • Usenet and IRC — both still operating; IRC's model continued into Slack and Discord
  • RSS — still functioning and still supported by podcast distribution, largely bypassed by algorithmic feeds
  • Forums — specialist communities persist where depth and searchable archives matter more than reach
  • Personal blogs — continuing, with distribution now mediated by platforms and search rather than direct subscription
