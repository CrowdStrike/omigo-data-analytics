Evolution of Machine Learning

The non-neural line of descent — from fitting curves to gradient-boosted ensembles that remain competitive on tabular data.

Dates are approximate and mark mainstream adoption rather than first release. Bars in red ended as mainstream practice; orange survives in specific niches only.

The Eras

EraWhat changed
2010 onward
ML becomes a library call
scikit-learn, consistent APIs
A uniform fit/predict interface across dozens of algorithms turned model selection into a loop. This lowered the barrier enormously and shifted the difficulty to the surrounding decisions — validation strategy, leakage, class imbalance, whether the metric matches the goal.
2014 onward
Gradient boosting leads on tabular data
XGBoost, LightGBM, CatBoost
Efficient regularised boosting implementations became the reliable choice for structured data and dominated competition leaderboards. Notably this happened during the deep learning boom: on ordinary tabular business data, gradient-boosted trees generally still outperform neural networks.
2017 onward
Automating the loop, then operating it
AutoML, feature stores, model monitoring
Search over models and hyperparameters became automated, and attention moved to everything after training: serving with low latency, detecting distribution drift, keeping training and serving features consistent, retraining on a schedule. The engineering around the model became larger than the model.
1950s – 1970s
Fitting functions to data
Regression, discriminant analysis, k-NN
The earliest methods came from statistics rather than computing: fit a line, separate two groups, classify by resemblance to known examples. These were interpretable by construction and remain the correct default when data is scarce or the coefficients must be explained.
1970s – 1980s
Rules written by handgone
Expert systems, MYCIN, inference engines
Rather than learning from data, experts were interviewed and their reasoning encoded as if-then rules. Systems worked impressively in narrow domains and then collapsed on the knowledge acquisition problem: experts could not articulate what they knew, and thousands of rules interacted unpredictably. This failure ended the first commercial AI wave.
1980s – 1990s
Learning tree structure
ID3, C4.5, CART
Decision trees learned their own splitting rules from data and produced a readable model. A single tree overfit badly, but the structure turned out to be an excellent building block — which is what the next decade exploited.
1990s – 2000s
Margins and kernels
SVMs, kernel trick, VC theory
Support vector machines brought strong theory: maximise the separating margin, and use kernels to work in high-dimensional spaces without computing coordinates there. For a decade SVMs were the default strong classifier on small and medium datasets, backed by real generalisation bounds.
Late 1990s onward
Combining many weak models
Bagging, random forests, AdaBoost, gradient boosting
The central insight of practical ML: averaging many imperfect models beats tuning one. Random forests decorrelate trees by randomising features and samples; boosting fits each new tree to the errors of those before it. Both are still near the top on tabular problems.
2000s
Features as the real work
Domain features, binning, target encoding
With algorithms available off the shelf, competitive advantage moved to constructing input variables. Practitioners spent most of their effort encoding domain knowledge into features, and the model was often the least interesting part of the pipeline.

What Disappeared

Ended as mainstream practice:
  • Expert systems and hand-coded rule bases — the dominant commercial approach of the 1980s, ended by the knowledge acquisition bottleneck
  • Perceptrons as a dead end — the 1969 critique effectively froze neural network research for over a decade
  • Manual feature engineering for text and images — SIFT, HOG, bag-of-words, all replaced by learned representations
  • Sampling down to fit in memory — a routine preprocessing step that distributed training removed
  • Single-model competition entries — once ensembles proved dominant, submitting one model stopped being competitive
Alive, but only in niches:
  • Support vector machines — still strong on small, high-dimensional datasets such as some biological problems
  • Naive Bayes — still a reasonable fast baseline for text classification despite its independence assumption rarely holding
  • Linear and logistic regression — not a niche at all: still the correct choice wherever coefficients must be defensible, as in credit and clinical decisions
