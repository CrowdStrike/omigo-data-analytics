Self-Driving & Fleet Learning — Domain-Specific Pitfalls

Long-Tail Scenario Collection

Long-Tail Scenario Collection
  • The split: 99.99% of miles driven are routine; the remaining 0.01% are the edge cases that actually cause fatalities and system failures.
  • The math: A fleet may need 100 million miles just to encounter one specific rare scenario (a mattress falling off a truck, a child chasing a ball in rain at dusk).
  • Drowning in normalcy: Collecting more routine miles does not help — you drown in normalcy while starving for the critical tail.
  • Imperfect fixes: Synthetic generation and targeted scenario mining are necessary but introduce their own distributional biases.

Fleet Disagreement as Signal

Fleet Disagreement as Signal
  • The outlier: When 999 of 1,000 cars proceed through an intersection and 1 hesitates, brakes, or alerts, that single outlier is a powerful signal.
  • Hidden causes: It may indicate a partially obscured stop sign, unusual pedestrian behavior, or a time-specific sun-glare condition.
  • Voting fails: Majority voting dismisses the minority report as noise, but in safety-critical systems it is often the most important data point.
  • The method: Fleet disagreement mining — detecting when vehicles diverge at the same location — finds latent hazards that standard metrics miss entirely.

HD Map Staleness

HD Map Staleness
  • Centimeter fiction: HD maps encode lanes, signs, and signals to centimeter accuracy, but construction zones appear overnight and markings shift by meters.
  • Stale ground truth: If the autonomy stack trusts the map for localization, planning, and perception validation, the car may confidently drive where a lane used to be.
  • Continuous decay: Map freshness degrades continuously, not in discrete jumps, making it hard to know when trust should be withdrawn.
  • Mismatch: The weeks-to-months update cycle is fundamentally mismatched with how fast the world changes.

Disengagement Report Bias

Disengagement Report Bias
  • Incomparable definitions: Some companies count only unplanned safety-critical takeovers; others include planned stops, software resets, or comfort interventions.
  • Apples to oranges: 0.1 disengagements/1000mi on easy suburban roads and 2.0 on dense urban streets measure different things.
  • PR metric: The number conflates operational domain difficulty, reporting standards, and actual capability — rankings based on it are statistically meaningless.

Shadow Mode vs Real Deployment Gap

Shadow Mode vs Real Deployment Gap
  • Open loop: In shadow mode the system says "I would have braked here" but never experiences the consequences of its own actions.
  • Counterfactual gap: The scenario that unfolds is always the one where the human drove — the system never sees what happens after its hypothetical braking.
  • Closed-loop surprise: Once deployed, the system's actions change the world state (drivers react, pedestrians adjust), creating dynamics it has never encountered.
  • Miscalibrated: Shadow mode performance can dramatically overestimate or underestimate real-world performance.

Edge Case Labeling Ambiguity

Edge Case Labeling Ambiguity
  • The scenario: A phone-distracted jaywalker 30 meters ahead — five trained annotators give four different labels (stop, slow down, change lanes, proceed).
  • No schema captures it: The "correct" action depends on speed, weather, traffic density, local norms, and pedestrian body language.
  • Averaged harm: Training on ambiguous labels yields a model that learns an average, which may be worse than any individual annotator's judgment.
  • Not a QC problem: High inter-annotator disagreement on safety-critical scenarios reflects genuine behavioral ambiguity that the labeling framework cannot resolve.
