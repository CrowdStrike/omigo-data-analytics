Cancer Screening

Screening detects cancer earlier and more often — but earlier detection, more detection, and more testing are not the same thing as saving lives.

Lead Time Bias

Earlier Diagnosis Inflates "Survival" Without Extending Life
  • The illusion: Screening finds cancer 2 years earlier, and "5-year survival" jumps from 40% to 70% — a headline improvement.
  • The reality: The death date is unchanged; the survival clock simply started sooner.
  • Wrong metric: Survival-from-diagnosis is mechanically inflated by any earlier detection, even one with zero treatment benefit.
  • The fix: Judge screening by age-adjusted mortality in screened vs unscreened populations, never by survival-from-diagnosis.

Overdiagnosis

Finding Cancers That Would Never Have Caused Symptoms or Death
  • The phenomenon: Sensitive screening detects indolent tumors that would never have progressed to symptoms in the patient's lifetime.
  • The evidence: Autopsies find prostate cancer in ~50% of 70-year-old men who died of entirely other causes.
  • The harm: Detecting these cancers didn't help — it triggered biopsies, surgery, and radiation for tumors that needed nothing.
  • The trap: Every overdiagnosed case counts as a screening "success" in detection statistics while delivering pure harm.

Sensitivity vs Specificity at Population Scale

95% Sensitive, 90% Specific, 0.5% Prevalence → 95.5% of Positives Are False
  • The math: At 0.5% prevalence, a 95%-sensitive / 90%-specific test yields a PPV of only 4.5% — a positive result means a 4.5% chance of actually having cancer.
  • Why: The 10% false-positive rate applies to the 99.5% who are healthy, swamping the true positives from the tiny sick fraction.
  • Scale effect: At population scale, specificity dominates — a one-point specificity gain removes far more false alarms than a one-point sensitivity gain adds true detections.
  • Design rule: Evaluate any mass screening test at the actual population prevalence, not on the balanced case-control set it was validated on.

Incidentalomas

Full-Body Scans Find "Something" in 40% of Healthy People
  • The finding rate: A full-body CT scan turns up an incidental abnormality in roughly 40% of perfectly healthy people.
  • The cascade: Most findings are benign, but each one demands follow-up — more imaging, more radiation, sometimes a biopsy with real complication risk.
  • The hidden cost: Every follow-up carries anxiety, expense, and iatrogenic risk for people who were never sick.
  • The lesson: Finding things is not intrinsically good — a detection only has value if acting on it improves outcomes.

Screening Interval Optimization

Annual vs Biennial: Marginal Benefit vs Doubled Harm
  • The benefit: Annual mammograms catch cancer about 6 months earlier on average than biennial screening.
  • The cost: Screening twice as often doubles radiation exposure, doubles false positives, and doubles anxiety episodes.
  • The asymmetry: The benefit grows marginally with frequency while the harms scale linearly — "more screening" is not monotonically better.
  • The method: The optimal interval is a math problem — expected life-years gained vs cumulative harm — not an intuition that more vigilance is safer.

Psychological Cost of False Positives

Three Weeks of Believing You Have Cancer Is Real Harm
  • The experience: "You might have cancer" → 3 weeks of fear waiting for the biopsy → "actually, it's benign."
  • The accounting gap: Those 3 weeks of anxiety are real health harm produced by a system operating exactly at its designed specificity.
  • Not an error: The false positive was later corrected, so no metric records a failure — yet the harm already happened.
  • The implication: False positives have health consequences even when corrected; count them as costs, not as zero.
