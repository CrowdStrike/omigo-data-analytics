Metric Granularity & Denominator Selection

Statistical tests require independent observations: page-level events within a session are correlated, user-level aggregates are not. The wrong granularity doesn't just give a different number — it makes the math invalid.

A/B Test at Session/Page Level Violates i.i.d. Assumption

Page Events Are Correlated Within a Session — Not Independent Samples
  • The mathematical requirement: Every classic test assumes independent draws — SE = σ/√n underestimates variance when observations are correlated.
  • Events cluster within users: Page views in a session share the same intent, query, and mood, and sessions within a user are auto-correlated — none are independent draws.
  • User-level totals ARE independent: One value per user per period comes from different people with no causal link, so a t-test on them is valid.

Independence Violation — Why Page-Level Data Can't Use a T-Test

T-Test Requires Independent Observations. Page Events Are NOT Independent.
  • The math requirement: SE = σ/√n only works for uncorrelated data; with intra-cluster correlation ρ the true SE inflates by the design effect shown in the chart.
  • Page views are correlated: Same user, same intent, same browsing sequence — each page view is a dependent observation, not an independent draw.
  • The inflation: 1000 users × 10 pages = 10,000 rows but effective n ≈ 1000, so the naive SE is ~3× too small and "significant" results are often noise.

Denominator Manipulation (Changing What You Divide By)

The Variant Changes the Denominator, Not the Behavior
  • CTR inflation: Showing fewer items per page halves impressions, so the same one click "doubles CTR" with zero behavior change.
  • Conversion/revenue inflation: Adding a pre-filter or longer flow drops low-intent users from the denominator — the rate rises while total revenue can fall.
  • Rule: If the variant can mechanically change the denominator, use one it cannot affect (unique users randomized, or user-days) — otherwise you're measuring arithmetic, not behavior.

Simpson's Paradox from Mix Shift

Aggregate Goes One Way, Every Segment Goes the Other
  • Classic case: Mobile and desktop conversion both improve, but mobile's traffic share grows — the aggregate declines because the lower-converting channel got more weight.
  • A/B test version: "No effect" overall can hide +8% for new users and -3% for returning — the aggregate buries both insights.
  • Why it happens: Whenever sub-populations with different base rates change their mix (channel spend, seasonality, growth demographics), the aggregate can move opposite to every sub-group.
  • Fix: Always segment by device, new/returning, geo, and channel — if segments diverge from the aggregate, act on the segments.

Novelty Effect — Testing Too Short

Day 1-3 Results ≠ Steady-State Behavior
  • The pattern: Any visible UI change spikes engagement for ~72 hours, then decays to steady-state (often +0%) — calling the test on day 3 ships a novelty artifact.
  • Reverse trap: Compounding effects like retention are invisible at day 3 but dominant by day 30 — short tests amplify the least valuable effects and miss the most valuable.
  • Cycle bias: Weekday-only or single-week tests catch different users and paycheck phases — same variant, different timing, different result.
  • Fix: Run at least 2 full business cycles and plot the effect by day: still growing means real, big-then-declining means novelty.

Wrong Level Hides the Real Problem

Aggregate "Error Rate" Hides Whether It's 1 User or 1000
  • Same rate, different severity: A "1% request error rate" can be one user in a retry loop or 100 different users each failing once — the request-level metric can't distinguish a broken bot from an outage.
  • Revenue version: "$45 average order value" may describe a bimodal distribution where nobody spends near $45 — acting on the average optimizes for a customer that doesn't exist.
  • Fix: Report a distribution (histogram or p10/p50/p90) and always give error rate as both % of requests AND % of users affected.

Time Window Selection Bias

Monthly Churn 5% Sounds Manageable. Annual = Lost Half Your Customers.
  • The compounding trap: 5% monthly churn compounds to losing 46% of customers per year — same metric, different timeframe, opposite organizational response.
  • The snapshot trap: A stable DAU/MAU of 25% can hide 100% monthly churn with constant inflow replacing outflow — the ratio is stable, the dynamics are terrible.
  • Window cherry-pick: Holiday-quarter revenue "grew 40%" but same-quarter-last-year comparison shows 3% — the window you choose IS the story you tell.
  • Fix: Report multiple timeframes simultaneously (daily, weekly, monthly, same-period-last-year); if the story changes between them, that's the real finding.

Per-Impression vs Per-User Economics

Ad Revenue Per Impression Grows While Revenue Per User Falls
  • The ad trap: More ads lift revenue per impression while driving DAU down — net revenue looks fine today, but the user loss compounds and RPM gains can't keep up.
  • Subscription version: A price hike lifts ARPU this month while pushing marginal subscribers to churn — in 6 months, fewer users at higher ARPU means lower total revenue.
  • Wrong timeframe: Per-impression and per-user metrics give instant feedback; the LTV damage they cause shows up months later when it's too late.
  • Fix: Pair efficiency metrics (per-X) with volume metrics (total-X) and LTV leading indicators — efficiency up with volume down means harvesting, not growing.

Geographic / Demographic Aggregation Hiding Inequity

"Average Latency 200ms" When Half Your Users Get 2 Seconds
  • CDN coverage bias: Two populations with 10× different latency average to a number neither experiences — the "average user" doesn't exist.
  • Global A/B test: A variant that helps the majority region and hurts a smaller one nets out positive while degrading your fastest-growing market.
  • Demographic aggregation: "92% accuracy" can hide 78% for a minority group — the aggregate passes review while the model underperforms for those who need it most.
  • Fix: Split metrics by geography, device tier, and demographic group, and set floor constraints ("no group below X") rather than average targets.

Survivor Bias in Denominator (Who Gets Counted)

Measuring Only Users Who Didn't Leave
  • NPS of active users only: A high score among survivors just means the unhappy 30% already churned out of the denominator.
  • Engagement among remaining users: When the least-engaged users leave, per-user engagement rises by construction — the denominator got filtered, nobody got more engaged.
  • A/B test version: If variant B churns low-spenders early, its day-30 "revenue per user" compares a higher-quality surviving subset, not a better treatment.
  • Fix: Use intent-to-treat — once randomized, users stay in the denominator forever; report revenue per RANDOMIZED user, not per active user.
