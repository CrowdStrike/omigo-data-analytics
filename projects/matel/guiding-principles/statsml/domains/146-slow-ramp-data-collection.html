Slow Feature Ramp & Impact on Data Collection

Features roll out at 1% → 100% over months, so ramp-phase data comes from a tiny, non-representative slice of users. Decisions made on 5% data get applied to everyone.

The core problem: Slow ramps are good engineering but bad data science: a 5% ramp generates 20× less data, and the first-ramped users (power users, specific regions/platforms) are not the full population. Models built during the ramp learn the biased subpopulation.

Insufficient Sample Size During Ramp — Can't Reach Significance

5% Ramp = 20× Longer to Reach Statistical Power
  • The math: Reaching 10,000 events takes 2 days at 100% ramp, 40 days at 5%, and 200 days at 1% — "launched" but unmeasurable for months.
  • The decision pressure: Leadership asks "is it working?" after 2 weeks, forcing either "we don't know yet" or a call on 1/20th the needed data.
  • Rare events are invisible: At 5% ramp a rare event's rate is effectively zero, so the feature appears to "do nothing" when the signal is just too sparse.
  • The calendar cost: By the time a 5% ramp accumulates enough data, seasonality, competitors, and user behavior have shifted — the answer may be stale.

Ramp Population Is Not Representative

Who Gets Ramped First ≠ Who Uses the Product
  • Engineering-biased ramp: Employees, then one region, then one platform — each phase has a different profile, and phase-1 data says nothing about phase 5.
  • Power user bias: Early recipients are more engaged and bug-tolerant, so models trained on them fail on the casual majority.
  • Regional bias: US-first data trains a "global" model that is validated on US behavior and worse for EU/APAC when it finally arrives.
  • The survivorship problem: Ramp users who hate the feature churn, so 5% data shows only survivors — the would-be churners appear only at 100%.

Model Trained on Ramp Data Fails at Full Scale

5% Data → Train Model → Deploy to 100% → Model Fails
  • The cycle: Train on 2 months of 5% data, look great, ramp to 100% — accuracy drops because the model learned the biased subpopulation's patterns.
  • Interaction effects: Network effects, social features, and recommendation diversity all behave differently at 5% than at 100%.
  • Load-dependent behavior: At 5% infrastructure has headroom; at 100% latency spikes and caches miss — degradation the training data never showed.
  • The irreversibility: By the time the model fails at 100%, bad predictions have reached every user — rolling back doesn't recover the lost trust.

Feature Interaction — Other Features Also Ramping

Multiple Features Ramping Simultaneously Create Data Chaos
  • The combinatorial problem: Features A, B, and C ramping simultaneously split users into 8 segments, each with different data characteristics.
  • Attribution impossible: A 3% metric lift can't be attributed to A, B, or their interaction — every measurement is confounded by other ramps.
  • Data contamination: Feature B's model trains on a mixture of with-A and without-A behavior — a blend that matches no final state.
