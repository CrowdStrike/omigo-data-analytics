Real-Time Object Detection

On-device, real-time visual inference must be fast, power-efficient, private, and correct — all at once, on hardware that fights back.

16ms Latency Budget

60fps Means the ENTIRE Pipeline Fits in 16ms
  • The budget: 60fps gives 16ms per frame for capture → preprocess → inference → post-process → render overlay — inference alone often eats half of it.
  • Hard failure: Any frame over 16ms is dropped, and dropped frames mean visible lag — on a head-mounted display that lag literally causes nausea.
  • No partial credit: A model that is 20ms and 95% accurate loses to one that is 12ms and 88% accurate — real-time systems must be fast AND correct, and fast wins ties.

Power/Thermal Constraints On-Device

2W Power Budget: Accuracy Traded for a Device That Stays Alive
  • The ceiling: Smart glasses run on a ~2W power budget shared with display, camera, and radios — compute gets a fraction of it.
  • Battery math: Running full-resolution YOLO continuously drains the battery in ~20 minutes; the "best" model is useless on a dead device.
  • The trade: A smaller quantized model makes more detection errors, but it runs all day — accuracy metrics must be evaluated jointly with watts, not in isolation.
  • Thermal throttling: Sustained load heats the device, the SoC throttles, and latency degrades over the session — benchmark numbers from a cold device are optimistic.

Training Images ≠ Live Camera Feed

Curated Datasets vs. What a Glasses-Cam Actually Sees
  • Training side: Benchmark datasets are curated — good lighting, centered objects, sharp focus, deliberate framing.
  • Live side: A worn camera delivers motion blur, partial occlusion, extreme angles, reflections, and low light — none of it curated.
  • The gap: The domain gap between ImageNet-style photos and a real glasses-cam feed is enormous; benchmark mAP does not transfer.
  • The fix costs: Closing the gap needs in-domain data collection and augmentation pipelines — expensive, but the alternative is a model that only works in the lab.

Motion Blur at Head-Turn Speed

User Turns Head → Whole Frame Is Smeared → Detector Goes Blind
  • The physics: A head turn during a ~50ms exposure smears the entire frame — no object in it is sharp.
  • Trained on sharp: A detector trained on crisp images has never seen this input distribution and fails silently, not gracefully.
  • Both options hurt: Either make the model blur-robust (harder training, bigger model) or skip unstable frames — and skipping means being blind ~30% of the time.
  • Worst timing: Head turns happen exactly when the user is looking for something new — the moments detection matters most are the blurriest.

Privacy Constraints Limit Processing

Must Detect Faces Before Processing — But Detection IS Processing
  • The rule: Raw video of bystanders can't be stored or transmitted — faces must be detected and blurred BEFORE any downstream processing.
  • The circle: Face detection is itself processing of the raw frames, so you must process the video to know what you're not allowed to process.
  • Practical escape: The face-blur stage must run on-device, in memory, with nothing persisted — which spends part of the latency and power budgets from Pitfalls 1 and 2.
  • Failure asymmetry: A missed face is a privacy violation, not just a metric miss — the blur stage needs near-perfect recall even when the detector behind it doesn't.

Occlusion from Device Geometry

The Device Blocks Its Own Camera — Misses in Predictable Places
  • Fixed dead zones: The glasses frame blocks ~10% of the visual field and the nose bridge occludes center-bottom — these zones never move.
  • Systematic, not random: Objects partially hidden by the device itself produce missed detections concentrated in the same regions every frame.
  • Metrics hide it: Aggregate recall looks fine; only a spatial error map reveals that misses cluster where the hardware occludes.
  • Design implication: Evaluate per-region, and either mask known dead zones from confidence scores or use temporal tracking to carry objects through them.
