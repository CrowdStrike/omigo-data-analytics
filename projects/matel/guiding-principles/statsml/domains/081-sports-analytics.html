Sports Analytics & Performance Metrics

Sports analytics combines tiny samples with extreme selection effects, venue biases, and era adjustments — advanced metrics project a precision that billion-dollar player valuations don't actually have.

Sample Size Per Player Per Season Is Tiny

Sample Size Per Player Per Season Is Tiny
  • The trap: A 17-game NFL season (~560 pass attempts) is a smaller sample than many medical trials, yet it drives multi-million dollar salary decisions.
  • Real-world failure: Marcus Mariota's 2016 "breakout" was noise on a ~500-attempt sample; his 2017 "regression" was just the expected variance, not decline.
  • Confidence intervals ignored: A passer rating of 95.0 over 17 games carries a ±12-point CI, yet gets treated as a precise measurement.

Survivorship Bias in Career Statistics

Survivorship Bias in Career Statistics
  • The trap: Career statistics exist only for players who survived cuts, injuries, and competition — the thousands cut in preseason never enter the data.
  • Real-world failure: MLB's stable ~.260 batting average excludes the AAAA players whose brief .180 stints never accumulate into "careers."
  • Draft pick analysis: The "8.2-year average first-round career" is conditional on making a roster — the bimodal reality means the average describes no one.
  • Hall of Fame illusion: Comparing current players to Hall of Famers ignores equally talented predecessors whose careers were cut short by wars, segregation, or untreatable injuries.

Hot Hand Fallacy vs Real Streaks

Hot Hand Fallacy vs Real Streaks
  • The trap: Gilovich et al. (1985) declared the hot hand an illusion, but Miller & Sanjurjo (2018) found a statistical error in the original — the effect is real but far smaller than perception.
  • Measurement difficulty: Detecting a ~3% effect against the huge variance of ~45% shooting requires thousands of observations.
  • Strategic confound: "Hot" players draw tighter defense, so observed streak percentages underestimate the true effect.

Park/Venue Effects on Player Comparisons

Park/Venue Effects on Player Comparisons
  • The trap: Venue effects are massive — comparing a Coors Field hitter to a Petco hitter (or a dome kicker to a Soldier Field kicker) at face value is comparing athletes under different physics.
  • Real-world failure: Larry Walker's Coors-adjusted average (~.295) was still elite, but the binary "Coors-inflated" narrative cost him years of Hall of Fame consideration.
  • Incomplete adjustments: Park factors are seasonal averages, so applying one correction to heterogeneous conditions removes some venue effect while adding its own noise.

Era Adjustment Problems

Era Adjustment Problems
  • The trap: Cross-era comparison requires simultaneous adjustment for rules, equipment, training, tactics, and an artificially restricted talent pool (segregation, no international players) — no single formula covers them all.
  • Real-world failure: Wilt's 50.4 PPG "adjusts" to the mid-30s, but the adjustment rests on unmeasurable counterfactuals.
  • Pace adjustment trap: Per-possession stats assume production scales linearly with possessions, but fatigue curves are non-linear.

Draft Pick Value vs Development System

Draft Pick Value vs Development System
  • The trap: Draft-position success conflates selection quality with development investment — high picks get better coaching, more playing time, and longer leashes, a system never tested on later picks.
  • Real-world failure: Draft value charts encode outcomes that already include preferential development — a self-fulfilling prophecy.
  • International comparison: Draft-free European academies show much flatter success curves by recruitment ranking, suggesting the draft gradient is partly an allocation artifact.

Injury Prediction: High False Positive Rate

Injury Prediction: High False Positive Rate
  • The trap: At a 5% injury base rate, even a sensitive predictor produces mostly false alarms — the vast majority of flagged players never get injured.
  • Real-world failure: An NFL team rested 35% of starters weekly on flags of which only ~10% panned out, losing games to unnecessary precaution.
  • Counterfactual problem: Resting a flagged player who stays healthy "confirms" the model, making injury prediction unfalsifiable in practice.

GPS Tracking Noise in Player Load

GPS Tracking Noise in Player Load
  • The trap: GPS "player load" metrics carry ±5-10% error per session — larger than the workload difference that triggers recovery protocols.
  • Real-world failure: A Premier League "20% workload spike" that forced a recovery day turned out to be satellite bounce off the stadium roof; the corrected distance was normal.
  • Acceleration artifacts: 10-18Hz sampling misses sprint-level dynamics, underestimating true mechanical load by 15-30% in high-intensity actions.
  • Indoor limitations: Indoor LPS systems have different error profiles than GPS, making cross-system workload comparisons meaningless.

Team Effects Inseparable from Individual

Team Effects Inseparable from Individual
  • The trap: Individual metrics are contaminated by teammates, coaching systems, opponent quality, and good-team game scripts — a receiver's stats depend on his quarterback.
  • Real-world failure: DeAndre Hopkins' production collapsed when Watson left — teams paying for the solo number overpaid for a system outcome.
  • Plus-minus limitations: Isolation metrics like +/- and Corsi need ~2,000 minutes to stabilize, so single-season values are noise for free-agent decisions.

Regression to Mean Confused with Decline

Regression to Mean Confused with Decline
  • The trap: Exceptional seasons regress toward the career average by mathematical necessity — the drop is not decline.
  • Real-world failure: The "Madden Curse" and the "sophomore slump" are both pure regression — athletes selected for outlier seasons revert to true talent.
  • Contract disasters: Teams pay free agents for the contract-year peak and receive the expected regression to multi-season talent level.
