Cluster-Level Randomization Ignored (Health / Epidemiology)

When the outcome is transmissible or shared within a natural group, randomizing at the individual level violates independence — the experiment is measuring spillover, not treatment effect.

The Core Problem

The Core Problem
  • Communicable diseases spread within clusters — households, wards, schools, villages. If you randomize a hygiene intervention at the person level within a household, one treated person's behavior changes exposure for everyone in that household.
  • The treated individual's outcome is not independent of the untreated family members' outcomes. They share air, surfaces, water, food preparation.
  • This isn't contamination (pitfall: control accidentally gets treatment). It's deeper — the outcome itself is a cluster-level phenomenon being measured at the individual level.
Key insight: The unit of randomization must match the level at which the outcome is generated. If infection risk is determined by household behavior, the household is the experimental unit — not the person.

Examples Where This Fails

Examples Where This Fails
  • Hand-washing trial: Randomize individuals within households to receive soap + training. But diarrheal disease transmission happens via shared surfaces. One person washing hands reduces pathogen load for all household members. Individual-level effect is diluted and biased.
  • Masking in hospital wards: Randomize nurses to mask/no-mask within the same ward. Respiratory pathogen transmission is ward-level — one masked nurse reduces airborne load for the whole ward. Control nurses benefit from treatment nurses' masks.
  • Vaccination in schools: Randomize students within a classroom. Herd immunity kicks in — unvaccinated students are protected by vaccinated classmates. The true effect of vaccination is underestimated because the control group benefits from the treated group.
  • Water purification: Randomize households within a village sharing one well. Treated households reduce pathogen recirculation into the shared water source, partially treating everyone.

Correct approach: Cluster-randomized trial (CRT). Randomize at household / ward / village / school level. All members of a cluster get the same assignment. Analyze with mixed-effects models or GEE accounting for intra-cluster correlation.

Statistical Consequence

Statistical Consequence
  • Design effect: DE = 1 + (m − 1) × ICC, where m = cluster size, ICC = intra-cluster correlation. If household has 5 people and ICC = 0.3, DE = 2.2 — you need 2.2× as many clusters as you'd naively compute.
  • Effective N: N_eff = N / DE. A trial with 500 individuals in 100 households (m=5, ICC=0.3) has effective N ≈ 227, not 500.
  • If you ignore this: Confidence intervals are too narrow, p-values are too small, you declare effects significant when they aren't. Classic Type I error inflation.

The tell: Ask "does one person's outcome in this cluster depend on whether other cluster members were treated?" If yes — individual randomization is invalid.
