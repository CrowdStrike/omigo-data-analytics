Power Generation / Multi-Source Energy

Each energy source has fundamentally different data characteristics — the complexity explodes when the grid must manage all of them simultaneously.

Solar Intermittency

A Cloud Drops Output 50% in Seconds — a Step Function, Not a Trend
  • Step function: A single cloud passing over a solar farm drops output 50% in SECONDS and restores it seconds after it passes.
  • Rolling deficit: At GW scale, a cloud bank crossing 5 solar farms in sequence creates a 500MW deficit that moves geographically.
  • Scale mismatch: Solar is "predictable" only at the daily/seasonal level; at minute-level it's stochastic chaos.

Wind Ramp Events

100% → 10% in 30 Minutes; Above ~25m/s, More Wind = Zero Power
  • Ramp events: Output can go from 100% to 10% in 30 minutes (wind dies) or 0% to 90% in 20 minutes (front arrives).
  • Hardest case: Ramp events are the hardest to predict and the most dangerous for grid stability.
  • High-wind cutoff: Above ~25m/s turbines shut down in protection mode — more wind ≠ more power.

Nuclear Baseload Inflexibility

The "Reliable" Source Becomes the Grid Management Problem
  • Constant output: Nuclear runs at constant output 24/7 by design and economics, and can't ramp up or down quickly.
  • 2am problem: When demand drops but nuclear keeps producing, excess power must be dumped (negative pricing) or renewables curtailed.
  • Inverted reliability: Combined with variable renewables, the inflexible source constrains what the flexible sources can do.

Gas Turbine Startup Lag

10-30 Minutes to Start — for a 15-Minute Spike
  • Cold start lag: A gas peaking plant takes 10-30 minutes to reach full power, but the demand spike it covers may last only 15 minutes.
  • Expensive hedge: Keeping turbines spinning at idle buys ~5-minute "hot start" capability at high cost and pollution.
  • Data challenge: Predicting whether to pre-start turbines vs being caught cold.

Hydro Depends on Snowpack from 6 Months Prior

Reservoir Level Today = Snowfall Last Winter
  • 6-month lag: Reservoir level today is a function of snowfall last winter — you can't generate hydro from an empty reservoir.
  • Committed early: A drought season means hydro capacity is lost MONTHS before it becomes unavailable.
  • Long causal chain: A warm winter means reduced hydro all the following summer — the lag between cause (weather) and effect (capacity) is uniquely long.

Grid Frequency Balancing — Generation Must EXACTLY Equal Demand

Hold 50Hz Within ±0.5Hz While Renewables Fluctuate Every Second
  • Exact balance: Generation > demand raises frequency, demand > generation drops it — it must be held within ±0.5Hz or equipment is damaged.
  • Second-by-second: Variable renewables fluctuate every second, forcing continuous ramping of other sources.
  • Data challenge: Predicting net demand (demand minus variable generation) at 1-second resolution.

Curtailment = Wasted Energy (But Necessary)

Useful Output = Capacity Minus Curtailment
  • Thrown away: When solar/wind produce more than the grid can absorb, renewable generation must SHUT DOWN.
  • The scale: California curtails 1-5 GWh of solar per DAY in spring.
  • Distorted data: A solar farm's capacity factor ≠ what sunshine alone predicts, because grid constraints reduce actual useful output.

Duck Curve — Demand vs Generation Mismatch

Net Demand Goes Minimum → Maximum in 3 Hours
  • Midday trough: Solar floods the grid and net demand (demand − solar) drops to near-zero.
  • Evening cliff: Solar disappears just as everyone comes home and turns on AC/cooking/TV — demand spikes from minimum to maximum in 3 hours.
  • Physical limit: The required MW/minute ramp exceeds what many generators can physically deliver, and the duck curve gets more extreme every year.

Battery Degradation — Non-Linear and State-Dependent

Current State Alone Can't Predict Remaining Life
  • Non-linear: Degradation is slow for the first 20% of life, faster for the next 20%, then falls off a cliff.
  • Path-dependent: Each cycle's degradation depends on ALL previous cycles (depth of discharge, charge rate, temperature, cycle count).
  • History required: A battery at "80% capacity" might have 5 years or 5 months left depending on how it got there.

Multi-Source Dispatch Optimization — Combinatorial Explosion

Six Sources, Uncertain Forecasts, a New Solution Every 5 Minutes
  • The decision: Every 5 minutes, dispatch across solar and wind (free but variable), nuclear (cheap but inflexible), gas (expensive but fast), hydro (reservoir-limited), and battery (fast but short-duration).
  • Different constraints: Each source has its own cost, ramp rate, minimum output, start time, emission rate, and contractual obligations.
  • Stochastic core: Optimizing all of them under uncertain renewable forecasts is a massive stochastic optimization solved in real-time, 24/7.
