Electricity / Smart Grid

Statistical pitfalls in grid analytics — temporal aliasing, net metering deceptions, and the cascade from weather forecast to capacity planning.

15-Minute Interval Hides Sub-Minute Spikes

15-Minute Interval Hides Sub-Minute Spikes
Smart meters report the average consumption over 15-minute intervals. A household drawing 0.5 kW for 14 minutes then spiking to 20 kW for 1 minute produces a reported average of ~1.8 kW. The 20 kW spike — the one that blew the transformer — is completely invisible in the interval data. Capacity planning, fault detection, and equipment protection all fail when the true instantaneous load is hidden behind temporal averaging.

Net Metering Obscures Consumption

Net Metering Obscures Consumption
Homes with rooftop solar report only the net value (consumption minus generation). At noon a meter shows −2 kW, but the actual household consumption is 3 kW and the solar panels generate 5 kW. The utility sees −2 kW, yet the load on internal wiring and the local transformer is a full 3 kW. Capacity planning based on net meter data systematically underestimates the true electrical load on infrastructure.

EV Charging as Unpredictable New Load

EV Charging as Unpredictable New Load
One household adds an EV: +40 kW demand when charging. A residential transformer is designed for a neighborhood peak of ~100 kW. Five EVs charging simultaneously push demand to 200 kW — double the rated capacity. Historic load data contains no signal for this because EV adoption is a structural break that appears neighborhood by neighborhood with no warning in the training window.

Meter Tampering / Theft

Meter Tampering / Theft
Commercial customers bypass meters to steal electricity. In the data this appears as "reduced consumption." A naive model interprets the drop as "efficiency improved" — but it is actually theft. Globally, 3–5% of all electricity is stolen. The signal is invisible in legitimate data because the meter itself is compromised. Anomaly detection must compare against physical proxies (transformer load vs. sum of downstream meters) to catch it.

Demand Response Event Distortion

Demand Response Event Distortion
The utility sends a "reduce consumption" signal. Participants reduce load during the event window. But in the following 2 hours, consumption rebounds as deferred loads catch up (HVAC re-cooling, delayed laundry). Net energy savings over a 4-hour window ≈ zero. Event-day data shows an artificial pattern — the dip and rebound — that does not generalize to normal days and biases any model trained on it.

Weather Forecast Error Cascade

Weather Forecast Error Cascade
Demand forecasts depend on temperature forecasts. A temperature forecast error of ±3 °F cascades into a demand forecast error of ±8%. One day at 100 °F vs. 97 °F produces a 15% difference in peak demand. The entire capacity planning chain amplifies the weather forecast error at each step — temperature → demand → reserve margin → dispatch cost — making the final decision far less reliable than the original weather input.
