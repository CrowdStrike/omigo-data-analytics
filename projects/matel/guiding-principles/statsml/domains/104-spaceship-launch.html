Spaceship Launch / Rocket Data

One-shot systems where data decisions are irreversible, bandwidth is limited, and ground truth only exists after you've already committed.

One-Shot System: No Second Chance If Data Is Wrong

GO/NO-GO on Incomplete Data, Where "Wrong" = Catastrophic
  • Irreversible: Unlike ML where you can retrain, a rocket launch is IRREVERSIBLE once the engines ignite.
  • Challenger 1986: Engineers had O-ring cold-temperature data, but it was AMBIGUOUS at the observed 36°F (no prior launches that cold) — they launched, at the cost of 7 lives.
  • The challenge: You must make a GO/NO-GO decision with INCOMPLETE data, and "wrong" = catastrophic.

Telemetry Bandwidth Limits During Ascent

1000+ Sensors, 1-5 Mbps Downlink — Priorities Frozen Before Launch
  • The bottleneck: The rocket has 1000+ sensors but the telemetry link transmits only ~1-5 Mbps to ground.
  • Pre-committed priorities: Which sensors get real-time downlink is decided BEFORE launch, based on what you THINK will be important.
  • Blind spots: An anomaly in a low-priority sensor is discovered post-flight (if the vehicle survives) or never (if it doesn't).

Sensor Saturation at Extremes

Sensors Are Least Reliable at the Most Critical Moment
  • Saturated readings: A vibration sensor rated to 50G reports "50G" when actual vibration hits 80G at max-Q.
  • Ambiguity: Ground sees "vibration at limit" and can't tell 50G (okay) from 200G (vehicle breaking apart).
  • Everywhere at once: The same saturation hits temperature (thrust chamber), pressure (propellant tanks), and acceleration (staging events).

Countdown Abort Decision from Incomplete Data

30 Seconds to Classify: Sensor Failure, Noise, or Real Problem?
  • No time to diagnose: At T-minus 30 seconds an anomalous reading could be sensor failure (benign), a real problem (abort!), or transient noise (ignore).
  • Asymmetric costs: Aborting on a false alarm (2% historical rate) wastes $50M; launching with a real problem loses a $2B vehicle and possibly lives.
  • The discipline: Decision theory under extreme time pressure with uncertain data.

Post-Launch Debris Tracking (Space Situational Awareness)

Collision Probability Computed From Two Uncertain Positions
  • Barely detectable: The radar cross-section of a 1cm bolt is at the edge of detection — 30,000+ orbital objects tracked with ~5m position uncertainty.
  • Uncertain probabilities: A "1-in-10,000" collision estimate with uncertainty in BOTH positions could really be 1-in-100 or 1-in-1,000,000.
  • No resolution: You can't tell which — the uncertainty of the inputs swamps the estimate itself.

Re-Entry Communications Blackout

3-8 Minutes of Zero Telemetry at the Most Dangerous Phase
  • Plasma gap: Re-entry plasma blocks radio signals for 3-8 minutes — ground has ZERO telemetry during the most dangerous phase.
  • Unknowable state: The vehicle is either fine (can't confirm) or disintegrating (can't detect until too late).
  • Worst-moment gap: All pre-blackout data must predict the post-blackout state; anything that goes wrong DURING blackout leaves no data.

Ground Truth Only Exists After Recovery

Committing the Mission on Proxy Measurements
  • Delayed truth: Whether the payload deployed, the orientation was right, and thermal protection worked is only KNOWN after recovery or payload activation hours/days later.
  • Proxy measurements: During flight, telemetry SUGGESTS things are fine — but telemetry is indirect measurement.
  • No mid-flight check: Direct confirmation requires physical inspection or an end-to-end functional test, which can't happen until the mission is essentially over.

Test Data ≠ Flight Data (Every Flight Is Unique)

50 Ground Tests Can't Validate Flight #1
  • Controlled vs unique: Ground testing is repeatable with known inputs; every flight has unique weather, vibration profile, thermal environment, and trajectory perturbations.
  • Design ≠ instance: Testing validates the DESIGN but can't validate THIS SPECIFIC flight condition.
  • Tiny n: Anomalies that never appeared in 50 ground tests can appear on flight #1, and flight-data sample size is often n=1-5.
