Hedge Fund Pitfalls

Quantitative strategy traps that destroy alpha in production

Alpha Decay Once Discovered

Alpha Decay Once Discovered
A signal works, a fund trades on it, market participants adjust, and the signal disappears. The half-life of alpha is months for simple strategies (e.g., momentum) and years for complex ones (e.g., structural arbitrage). Your backtest uses historical alpha that no longer exists because the act of trading on it changed the market. Every published anomaly has a "discovery date" after which returns decline dramatically. Academic papers announcing an edge are effectively the edge's obituary.

Capacity Constraints

Capacity Constraints
A strategy works at $10M AUM. At $1B: the market impact of your own trades erases the edge. Your order to buy moves the price up before you finish buying. Sharpe ratio degrades monotonically with assets under management. The backtest doesn't model your own market impact because historically, you weren't in the market. Every strategy has a capacity ceiling. The best strategies (highest Sharpe) typically have the lowest capacity. Funds that grow AUM often watch their returns decline not because the strategy stopped working, but because they got too big for it.

Factor Crowding

Factor Crowding
Everyone discovers "momentum" as a factor. Everyone trades it. It becomes a crowded trade. Then one fund liquidates under stress and the entire crowded position unwinds violently. Everyone loses simultaneously because they all held the same trade. During the 2007 quant crisis, market-neutral funds lost 20%+ in days because factor correlations spiked to nearly 1.0. Correlation in crisis approaches 1.0 for crowded factors. Diversification across crowded factors is illusory diversification.

Backtest Overfitting / Multiple Testing

Backtest Overfitting / Multiple Testing
Try 1,000 strategies in backtest. By chance alone, ~50 will have Sharpe > 2. Publish those 50 as "discoveries." On live trading: 48 of them were noise that happened to look like signal in the historical data. "The best backtest is the one you never trade." The more strategies you test, the higher the bar must be for statistical significance. A Sharpe of 2 means nothing if you tested 1,000 alternatives — you need a Sharpe of 3+ to survive the multiple testing correction. Most quant shops don't adequately account for the number of hypotheses they've implicitly tested.

Execution Slippage

Execution Slippage
Backtest assumption: buy at the close price. Reality: your order takes 30ms, moves the market, gets partial fills, faces adverse selection. The strategy "makes" 8% in backtest, 3% after execution costs. The 5% gap is the difference between theory and reality. Every backtest implicitly assumes you can trade at the observed price, but the observed price existed precisely because you didn't trade. The act of trading creates a different price. Market impact models exist but are imprecise, especially for less liquid instruments.

Data Snooping from Strategy Iteration

Data Snooping from Strategy Iteration
You see a drawdown in 2015 during your backtest. You add a "regime filter" to avoid it. The backtest now looks great — it avoids the 2015 drawdown. But you only added the filter because you saw the drawdown. This is implicit overfitting to the training data. Out-of-sample: the filter doesn't help because future drawdowns won't look like 2015. Every time you modify a strategy based on backtest observations, you're fitting to noise. The more iterations, the more overfit. The final backtest equity curve tells you nothing about future performance because it's the product of dozens of such decisions.

Regime Detection

Regime Detection
Value investing worked from 1945 to 2020. Then it didn't for a decade. "The regime changed." But you can't detect a regime change until you've already lost money in it — the detection IS the loss. Regime is a post-hoc narrative applied to explain why your model stopped working. There's no reliable way to predict regime changes in advance. By the time you have enough data to statistically confirm a new regime, you've already suffered the losses. The concept of "regime" is unfalsifiable: any failure can be explained as a "regime change."

Leverage Amplifies Noise

Leverage Amplifies Noise
2x leverage turns an 8% return into 16%. But it also turns a 4% drawdown into 8%. Noise is amplified equally with signal. The signal-to-noise ratio does not improve with leverage — only the magnitude changes. A strategy with Sharpe 0.5 at 1x leverage still has Sharpe 0.5 at 3x leverage, but now a 2-sigma event wipes you out instead of being a bad quarter. Leverage doesn't create edge; it magnifies whatever is already there, including the randomness. Many fund blowups are mathematically inevitable given their leverage ratios and the true (not backtested) distribution of returns.
