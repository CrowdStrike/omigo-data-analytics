Death Over-Attribution During Outbreaks

During outbreaks, deaths of the already-terminal get attributed to the novel pathogen — a common cold can kill a stage-4 cancer patient, but that doesn't make it a deadly pandemic.

The core problem: Systematically attributing deaths to the salient new cause while ignoring competing risks inflates mortality statistics and distorts policy. The statistical sin: confusing "died WITH" for "died FROM."

Competing Risks Erasure — The Person Was Already Dying

"Died With X" ≠ "Died From X" — But Gets Counted the Same
  • How it works: A terminal cancer patient with a 3-month prognosis contracts the virus and dies 2 weeks later — the certificate lists the virus, and the cancer vanishes from the statistics.
  • Competing risks: The virus didn't kill a healthy person; it slightly accelerated death in someone already dying — the counterfactual is whether they'd be alive 6 months later anyway.
  • The "last straw" fallacy: The proximate trigger (any minor infection) is not the underlying cause, but attribution systems record the trigger.
  • Why it matters: "5,000 deaths" creates panic; "5,000 people already dying died slightly sooner" does not — the framing drives policy.

Age-Related Baseline Mortality Ignored

Very Old People Die — With or Without the Outbreak
  • Base rate of death: A nursing home loses ~25% of residents per year from all causes — outbreak deaths must be compared to expected mortality, not to zero.
  • Naming anonymous deaths: The outbreak gives a name to deaths that would otherwise be "natural causes" — 22 deaths in a home averaging 20/year isn't newsworthy, but "20 died after outbreak" is.
  • Pooled vs stratified: A pooled "3% mortality rate" terrorizes the young (near-zero risk) while the burden sits in age groups already at high baseline mortality.
  • The denominator problem: A 95-year-old already has ~2-3% chance of dying in any 28-day window, so "died within 28 days of positive test" relabels baseline deaths as outbreak deaths.

Comorbidity Stacking — 4+ Conditions, Each Individually Fatal

When Someone Has 5 Fatal Conditions, Picking One as "The Cause" Is Arbitrary
  • The typical case: A multi-morbid elderly patient dies with the virus listed as cause — but remove any one comorbidity and they might have survived it.
  • Single attribution is bureaucratic: Death certificates demand one "primary cause," forcing a single label onto cascading multi-system failure — the structural flaw that enables over-counting.
  • Substitution test: If a common cold or seasonal flu would have produced the same outcome, the cause is fragility, not the specific pathogen — the pathogen is interchangeable.

Availability Bias + Novelty = Panic Attribution

Novel Threats Get All the Credit; Familiar Killers Stay Invisible
  • Salience drives attribution: The novel pathogen gets 24/7 coverage while far larger familiar killers stay invisible — novelty times media attention equals perceived threat.
  • Anchoring to the timeline: A death already scheduled by terminal illness becomes an "outbreak death" merely because the outbreak was concurrent.
  • Feedback loop: Inflated statistics drive panic, panic drives more testing of dying populations, more deaths get the outbreak label, statistics inflate further.

"Died Within 28 Days of Positive Test" — A Definition That Guarantees Over-Counting

Administrative Definitions Replace Clinical Judgment
  • The definition: Any death within 28 days of a positive test counts as an outbreak death — including the bus accident and the pre-existing liver failure.
  • Why it was chosen: Administrative definitions are fast and consistent; clinical causation is slow and requires judgment — counting speed won over causal truth.
  • Scale of over-count: Comparing the definition to clinically-judged primary cause typically shows 20-30% over-attribution, reaching 40-50% in elderly populations.
  • Perverse incentive: When funding is tied to outbreak patient counts, there is an economic incentive to test every dying patient.

Excess Mortality — The Only Honest Metric (and Its Limitations)

Compare Total Deaths to Expected Deaths — Everything Else Is Attribution Theater
  • What it tells you: "Did more people die than expected?" bypasses all attribution problems — if excess mortality is near zero while attributed deaths are high, deaths were relabeled, not caused.
  • The gap is the measurement: Attributed deaths minus excess all-cause mortality directly measures over-attribution.
  • Limitations: Excess mortality also includes collateral deaths caused by the response (delayed care, suicides), not the pathogen itself.
  • Harvesting effect: Below-average mortality following a wave means deaths were pulled forward by weeks — diagnostic of over-attribution to the pathogen.

Policy Cascade — Inflated Numbers Drive Disproportionate Response

Over-Attributed Deaths → Panic → Policy That Causes More Harm Than the Disease
  • The cascade: Inflated numbers drive panic-based policy whose collateral damage can kill more people than the pathogen.
  • Second-order mortality: Delayed cancer diagnoses, skipped cardiac care, suicides, and overdoses are never attributed to "the response to inflated statistics" — but that is their causal chain.
  • One-sided accounting: Outbreak deaths are visible and named; collateral deaths are scattered across categories and never counted against the intervention.

The Correct Framework — Attributable Fraction, Not Binary Cause

Disease X Contributed 15% to This Death, Not 100%
  • Attributable fraction: The honest question is "what fraction of this death is attributable to X?" — not the binary "did X cause it?" that counts a 15% contribution as 100%.
  • Years of life lost: Counting a terminal patient's final weeks and a healthy 40-year-old's four decades each as "1 death" hides orders-of-magnitude differences in impact.
  • How to do it right: Measure mortality in patients with no other fatal conditions — that is true pathogen lethality; the rest is interaction with comorbidity.
  • Why it's not done: Nuance doesn't justify emergency powers; the incentive structure at every level favors binary over-attribution.

No Baseline Established — Seasonal Spike Reported as Outbreak

"10,000 Flu Cases This Month!" — But 7,000 Happen Every Month Regardless
  • The missing baseline: Respiratory infections happen year-round, but if you only start counting in winter, every case looks like it belongs to the "outbreak."
  • Testing creates cases: Mass testing during an outbreak "finds" the background hum of illness that was always there but never measured — cases didn't surge, testing did.
  • The honest comparison: Same test, same population, same month, previous year — the true outbreak signal is the difference, not the gross count.

Incidence Inflation — Reporting Gross Instead of Net

Total Cases Minus Background Rate = Actual Signal. Nobody Does the Subtraction.
  • Gross vs net: Headlines report gross totals; nobody subtracts the normal-month baseline, so the reported number can be several times the true signal.
  • Why baselines aren't established: Boring background rates aren't funded, tracked, or newsworthy — and their absence is the systematic failure that enables panic.
  • How to fix it: Continuous year-round surveillance published before any outbreak makes the excess immediately calculable and removes the ability to report gross totals as if the baseline were zero.
