Data Anonymization & De-identification

Anonymization techniques that look robust in theory — k-anonymity, aggregation thresholds, name removal — crumble against adversaries armed with auxiliary datasets and behavioral fingerprints.

k-Anonymity Fails With Auxiliary Data

k-Anonymity Fails With Auxiliary Data
  • The trap: k-anonymity guarantees each record is indistinguishable from k-1 others on quasi-identifiers, but assumes the adversary only has those specific attributes.
  • Real-world failure: A hospital released k=5 anonymized records. An attacker cross-referenced pharmacy loyalty card data and uniquely identified 23% of patients.
  • Why it persists: Organizations audit against a fixed set of quasi-identifiers rather than modeling adversary knowledge, which grows as more datasets become linkable.
  • Scale of damage: With 15 demographic attributes, k-anonymity requires k>10,000 to resist modern linkage attacks, making data useless.

Differential Privacy Budget Trade-offs

Differential Privacy Budget Trade-offs
  • The trap: Epsilon budget is finite. Each query consumes budget; once exhausted, no further analysis is possible without degrading guarantees.
  • Real-world failure: US Census 2020 used ε≈19.61 for redistricting — researchers argued this provided minimal protection while distorting small-area statistics enough to affect funding.
  • Composition problem: Running 100 queries at ε=0.1 each yields total ε=10 under basic composition, far less private than per-query guarantees suggest.
  • Utility death spiral: Maintaining ε=1 total across 50 queries means each gets ε=0.02, adding noise that makes results meaningless for subgroups under ~50,000.

Quasi-Identifier Re-identification

Quasi-Identifier Re-identification
  • The trap: Combinations of innocent attributes create unique fingerprints. Latanya Sweeney showed 87% of the US population is uniquely identified by zip+DOB+gender.
  • Real-world failure: Massachusetts GIC released "anonymized" hospital data. Sweeney purchased voter rolls for $20 and re-identified Governor Weld's medical records.
  • Modern expansion: Quasi-identifiers now include browser fingerprints, typing patterns, device characteristics — none traditionally considered PII.
  • Combinatorial explosion: 30 binary attributes yield 2^30 (>1 billion) combinations, guaranteeing uniqueness even in large populations.

Synthetic Data Mode Collapse

Synthetic Data Mode Collapse
  • The trap: GANs/VAEs producing synthetic "privacy-safe" data often collapse to modal values, erasing rare but critical subpopulations.
  • Real-world failure: A health insurer's synthetic claims data collapsed rare disease codes (ICD-10 appearing <50 times) into common nearby codes, making rare disease research impossible.
  • Privacy paradox: Rare records that mode collapse eliminates are the most re-identifiable — "privacy" comes from destroying sensitive data rather than protecting it.
  • Detection difficulty: Standard metrics (mean, variance, correlation) look fine. Only targeted subgroup analysis reveals the collapse.

Temporal De-anonymization

Temporal De-anonymization
  • The trap: Even with identifiers removed, event timing creates unique behavioral signatures as identifying as fingerprints.
  • Real-world failure: Researchers de-anonymized credit card streams by matching temporal spending patterns to social media check-ins — 4 spatiotemporal points identified 90% of 1.1M individuals.
  • Compounding risk: A user's Tuesday 8am login, Thursday 6pm purchase, Saturday 10am browse is a near-unique temporal fingerprint within weeks.
  • Failed countermeasure: Adding time jitter (±hours) preserves enough ordinal structure for sequence-matching attacks to succeed at >60% accuracy.

Zip + DOB + Gender = 87% Unique

Zip + DOB + Gender = 87% Unique
  • The trap: Three fields in virtually every demographic dataset create a near-unique identifier. This is demonstrated mathematical certainty, not theory.
  • Real-world failure: Health data releases include age, gender, and region believing these are "too broad." Year-level age + 5-digit zip + gender uniquely identifies 87.1% of the US population.
  • Granularity illusion: Broadening zip to 3-digit prefix and age to 5-year bands still leaves groups of ≤5 for 18% of records — failing k=5 for millions.
  • International variation: In smaller countries (Netherlands, Singapore), the combination is even more identifying due to smaller geographic units.

anonymization prize dataset Re-identification

anonymization prize dataset Re-identification
  • The trap: streaming service released 100M ratings from 480K subscribers with names removed, believing ratings couldn't identify people.
  • Real-world failure: Narayanan & Shmatikov (2008) cross-referenced with IMDb reviews. With 8 ratings (even ±14-day and ±1 star noise), they identified 99% of users.
  • Why it matters: Movie preferences correlate with political views, sexual orientation, health. One plaintiff was a closeted mother whose history would have outed her.
  • Legacy impact: streaming service cancelled Prize 2; this became the canonical example of why "removing names" is not anonymization.

Location Traces Re-identification Without Names

Location Traces Re-identification Without Names
  • The trap: "Anonymous" GPS/cell data is almost never anonymous. Home (nighttime) and work (daytime) clusters together identify nearly everyone.
  • Real-world failure: De Montjoye et al. (2013) showed 4 spatiotemporal points identify 95% of 1.5M individuals. Even coarse resolution keeps uniqueness above 50%.
  • Aggregation doesn't help: Origin-destination pairs with 1-hour bins create unique commute fingerprints. Your specific departure time is likely just you.
  • Commercial reality: NYT journalists traced an "anonymous" phone in a broker dataset to a Pentagon official via home-to-work commute patterns.

Consent ≠ Anonymous (Behavioral Fingerprints)

Consent ≠ Anonymous (Behavioral Fingerprints)
  • The trap: Users consenting to "anonymized" data collection believe they're protected. But behavioral patterns (mouse movements, typing cadence) form unique biometric signatures surviving anonymization.
  • Real-world failure: Mouse movement patterns on a single webpage identify return visitors with 95% accuracy — no cookies, no login, no IP needed. Keystroke dynamics are even more unique.
  • Consent gap: Users consent to "anonymous analytics" without understanding behavioral patterns ARE identifying — consenting to identified tracking masquerading as anonymous.
  • Cross-session linking: Behavioral biometrics re-link "anonymous" sessions across days, browsers, and devices with >80% accuracy even after deleting all identifiers.

Aggregation Threshold Gaming

Aggregation Threshold Gaming
  • The trap: "We only report when group has ≥5" (common in census/health data). Adversaries isolate individuals by subtracting overlapping group queries.
  • Real-world failure: Query "diabetics in zip 02138 aged 45-50" (8, released) then "...male" (3, suppressed) — subtraction reveals 5 females, bypassing threshold.
  • Differencing attacks: Multiple aggregate queries let attackers solve equation systems to recover individual values. Census tables at multiple geographic levels are particularly vulnerable.
  • Threshold inconsistency: Agencies use thresholds of 3, 5, 10, or 20 for the same data. Different thresholds across releases leak suppressed cell information.
