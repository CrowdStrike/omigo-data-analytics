Curse of Sampling

When data is a mixture of sub-populations, random sampling loses the rare modes — and every fix (stratification, oversampling) distorts something else.

The fundamental problem: A random sample draws from each mode in proportion to its prevalence, so rare modes get 0-2 samples. Re-sampling to fix this distorts the natural base rates the model needs. Every sampling strategy helps one thing and hurts another.

Rare Class Gets Zero Samples

Positive Rate 0.1% → Random Sample Almost Certainly Misses It
  • The math: At 0.1% fraud rate, a 10K sample expects ~10 positives — and since fraud clusters, often 0-3.
  • Worse than random: Rare events follow different patterns (3am, $0.01 test charges), so sampling under-represents exactly the tail you need.
  • Size doesn't save you: Segmenting fraud into 5 types at 100 each needs a 500K sample — for rare classes, collect exhaustively instead of sampling.
  • Real case: A 2000-patient trial at 0.5% prevalence gets ~10 positives total — no subgroup analysis possible.

Stratified Sampling Distorts Natural Base Rates

50/50 Class Balance = Model Learns Wrong Priors
  • The "fix": Oversample fraud to 50/50 and the model learns P(fraud) ≈ 50% — deployed where the real rate is 0.1%, every posterior is miscalibrated.
  • Threshold breaks: "Flag if P > 0.5" was tuned on balanced data; the production operating point is completely different.
  • No new information: Oversampling duplicates the same few fraud examples — the model memorizes them and misses novel patterns.

Sub-Populations Disappear in Samples

5 Modes in Population → Sample Captures 2-3. Others Vanish.
  • The problem: A 5% mode (e.g., executives in income data) yields ~50 points in a 1000-sample — enough to hint the mode exists, not to characterize it.
  • Cascading loss: Cross 4 dimensions (income × age × location × device) and most cells get 0-3 observations — the most distinctive sub-groups get the fewest.
  • Model sees unimodal: With 50 points, the rare mode looks like tail noise; the model treats a real population as outliers.

Geographic Bias — Some Locations Systematically Under-Sampled

Online Sampling Over-Represents Urban, Young, English-Speaking
  • The mechanism: Online collection requires internet, literacy, and willingness — your "random sample" is random only within the reachable subset.
  • App data: 50M users represent app-users, not people; non-users are invisible.
  • Health data: Clinic data represents that clinic's catchment; models fail on demographics never in it.
  • The interaction: Geographic bias × rare disease means a condition absent from training geography gets zero sensitivity globally.

Age/Demographic Strata Have Different Distributions

Same Feature, Different Shape Per Age Group — Sample Loses the Structure
  • Income by age: Each age band has its own shape (some bimodal); the aggregate has 5+ modes, and 200 per band isn't enough to fit per-group models.
  • Hidden interactions: A correlation of +0.4 in one age group and -0.2 in another averages to a Simpson's-paradox +0.15 — detecting this needs 1000+ per group.
  • The impossible requirement: 30+ observations per sub-population means intersectional strata (1000+ cells) need 30K+ samples — most datasets are tiny relative to their structure.

Time-Based Sampling Misses Regime Changes

Sample From "Last Month" — Miss the Other 11 Months' Patterns
  • Seasonal modes vanish: E-commerce has distinct monthly regimes; sample one month and the model drifts the other eleven.
  • Day-of-week: Weekday and weekend sessions have different modes — sample one and miss the other, sample both and get an unexplained mixture.
  • Regime changes: No single time window captures a non-stationary process (e.g., pre/post COVID behavior).
  • Rare temporal events: Black swans occur every 5-10 years and cause most of the losses — any shorter sample says "this never happens."

SMOTE and Synthetic Oversampling — Inventing Data That Doesn't Exist

Interpolating Between Rare Examples Creates Points in Empty Space
  • The geometry: SMOTE interpolates between minority neighbors, but the line can cut through majority territory — creating "minority" points that look like the majority.
  • No new information: 5000 synthetic points are linear combinations of the original 50 — zero new knowledge about the rare class.
  • Fake validation: Testing on synthetic-balanced data scores well because you tested on interpolations of your own training data; deployed recall collapses.

Sampling Bias in A/B Tests — Non-Representative Enrollment

Who Enters the Test ≠ Who Will Use the Feature
  • New-user bias: A 2-week test enrolls 100% of daily users but few light users — results generalize to heavy users only.
  • Opt-in bias: Cookie-accepters skew younger and more ad-tolerant; the unrepresented privacy-conscious segment reacts differently.
  • Geography: "Passed in US" tells you little about Asia/Europe where behavior differs.
  • Survivor enrollment: Early enrollees (early adopters) get the most measurement time and drown out mainstream users.

Feature Distribution in Sample ≠ Feature Distribution in Population

Sample Sees Unimodal. Population Is Multimodal. Model Breaks on Deployment.
  • The scenario: Training data comes from one dominant mode, looks normal-ish; production hits the modes you never saw.
  • Invisible in validation: The validation set shares the sampling bias, so accuracy looks fine until deployment.
  • Wrong transforms: A log-transform chosen because the sample "looks right-skewed" is the wrong fix for a bimodal population.
  • Weak tests: Shapiro-Wilk "can't reject normality" on an under-sampled trimodal feature — low power against missing modes, and "can't reject" ≠ "is normal."

The Bootstrap Doesn't Fix Representation — It Amplifies Bias

Resampling From a Biased Sample Gives You Confident Wrong Answers
  • The limit: Bootstrap resamples what you have — if the sample missed a mode, no amount of resampling can discover it.
  • Precisely wrong: A narrow CI around a biased estimate can sit entirely outside the true value.
  • False comfort: "Stable across 10,000 resamples" means the sample is self-consistent, not that it represents the population.
  • When it helps: Estimating SE within a well-sampled population — not when structure (modes, subgroups) is under-represented.
