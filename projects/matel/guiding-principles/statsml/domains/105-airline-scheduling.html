Airline Scheduling / Cascading Delays

Aircraft, crew, and passengers are shared resources — one delay propagates through the entire network, and the data records symptoms far from their causes.

One Delayed Flight Cascades to 50+

Aircraft and Crew Are Reused All Day — Delay Propagates Through Every Leg
  • The chain: Flight 1 (NYC→CHI) delayed 90 min → the same plane flies Flight 2 (CHI→LAX) 90 min late → Flight 3 (LAX→SEA) 90 min late.
  • Crew branches: The crew on that first flight is also delayed — their NEXT flight on a different aircraft is delayed too.
  • Exponential propagation: One initial event fans out through aircraft and crew rotations into parallel cascades across the day.

Weather at a Hub Affects the Entire Network

Hub Topology Amplifies Local Weather into Network-Wide Chaos
  • The event: A thunderstorm at Atlanta (Delta hub) delays or cancels 200 flights.
  • The amplification: Passengers connect THROUGH Atlanta from 100 cities to 100 other cities — 10,000 itineraries disrupted.
  • Modeling burden: Predicting "will my flight be delayed?" requires modeling EVERY other flight at EVERY hub the route passes through.

Crew Timeout Regulations Force Cancellations

A Delay Doesn't Just Shift the Schedule — It Removes Capacity
  • The rule: A pilot legally can't fly after 8-10 hours of duty.
  • The cascade: Flight delayed 3 hours → crew "times out" at destination → no crew for the return flight → cancellation.
  • Second-order cost: Replacement crew must be flown in (deadhead), occupying a seat on another flight and reducing available inventory.

Passenger Rebooking Creates Secondary Overload

"Oversold by 40%" Is a Symptom of a Cancellation 6 Hours Ago Elsewhere
  • The mechanism: 200 passengers from a cancelled flight get rebooked onto the next 3 flights → those flights are now oversold → bumps → more rebookings.
  • Duration: The cascade continues 24-48 hours after the initial event.
  • Misleading data: "Flight oversold by 40%" looks like terrible planning — it's actually the consequence of a cancellation on a different route hours earlier.

Maintenance Slot Missed — Aircraft Out of Service

Arriving 3 Hours Late Removes the Aircraft for the Entire Next Day
  • The setup: Aircraft has scheduled maintenance at midnight in DEN; a delay means it arrives at 2am instead of 11pm.
  • The miss: The maintenance window is gone (crew went home) → aircraft out of service until the next available slot, 18 hours later.
  • The fallout: 4-6 flights cancelled that have NOTHING to do with the original delay.

Fuel Tankering Decisions from Stale Weather

The Data Was Correct When Computed, Stale When Executed
  • The decision: At departure, carry extra fuel (heavy, expensive) or refuel at destination — depending on destination fuel price and availability.
  • The input: A weather forecast from 3 hours ago.
  • The failure mode: Weather changed → diversion → insufficient fuel for the diversion → emergency.

Ground Truth Takes 24-48 Hours to Settle

"Was This Flight Delayed?" Depends on Which Timestamp You Pick
  • Four clocks: Gate departure, wheels-off, wheels-on, and gate arrival are all different times for the same flight.
  • The contradiction: A flight that left the gate on time but sat on the taxiway 90 min is "on time" by departure metric and "90 min late" by arrival.
  • Metric gaming: DOT metrics use specific definitions, and airlines optimize to the METRIC, not the passenger experience.
  • Classification stakes: Labeling an event "cancellation" vs "extreme delay" is a decision with financial implications.

Schedule Recovery Optimization Is NP-Hard

Real-Time Recovery Is Heuristic — and Heuristics Strand Passengers
  • The problem size: After a disruption, reassign 500 aircraft, 3000 crew members, and 50,000 passengers to minimize total cost.
  • The constraints: Crew legalities, aircraft maintenance, passenger connections, gate availability, fuel, curfews — a combinatorial optimization unsolvable optimally in real time.
  • The consequence: Every airline uses heuristics → suboptimal recovery → some passengers stranded for days.
  • Real case: Southwest meltdown Dec 2022 — 16,700 flights cancelled over 10 days because the recovery algorithm failed.
