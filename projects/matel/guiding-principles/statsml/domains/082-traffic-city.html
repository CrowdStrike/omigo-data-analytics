Traffic/City Planning - Domain-Specific Pitfalls

Traffic data lies in specific ways — behavior responds to interventions, sensors see fragments, simulations are too orderly, and the inverse problems are ill-posed.

Induced Demand

Build a Lane → Relief → More Driving → Same Congestion in 3-5 Years
  • The mechanism: New highway capacity reduces congestion briefly, which activates latent demand — people who avoided driving now drive.
  • The model gap: Forecasts predict "less congestion" because they hold demand fixed and don't model the behavioral response to the improvement itself.
  • The timeline: The relief dip lasts 1-2 years; within 3-5 years congestion returns to the original level with more total vehicles on the road.
  • The lesson: Any intervention that changes incentives invalidates models trained on pre-intervention behavior.

Sensor Coverage Gaps

The "Full Picture" Is Interpolated From Sparse, Biased Measurements
  • Point sensors: Cameras and loops sit at intersections — everything between them is guesswork, and incidents mid-block are invisible.
  • GPS bias: Phone traces over-represent smartphone owners and app users; elderly, low-income, and cyclist trips are systematically undercounted.
  • Extrapolated counts: Manual traffic counts sampled 2 days per year get scaled to annual estimates — one atypical count day distorts a whole year's planning number.

Simulation ≠ Reality

SUMO/VISSIM Agents Follow Rules. Real Drivers Don't.
  • The gap: Microsimulation agents obey lane discipline, signals, and car-following models; real streets have road rage, illegal U-turns, double parking, and jaywalking.
  • Orderly vs chaotic: The simulation's clean flow overstates capacity and understates variance — its "optimal" signal timing assumes compliance that doesn't exist.
  • Policy failure: A plan validated only in simulation is validated against the rule-following world, so it degrades or fails when deployed to the rule-breaking one.

Special Event Disruption

Concerts, Games, Protests, Construction: 50+ Days/Year Off-Model
  • Pattern override: A stadium event or protest completely replaces the normal daily traffic pattern — it's not noise around the mean, it's a different regime.
  • Training blind spot: A model trained on "normal days" fails on the 50+ event days per year, which are exactly the days congestion management matters most.
  • Two-part problem: The system must first detect that an event is happening, then model that event's unique spatial and temporal impact in real time.

Multi-Modal Interaction

Optimizing for Cars Hurts Everyone Else Sharing the Same Space
  • Shared space: Cars, bikes, pedestrians, buses, and scooters occupy the same streets with different speeds, trajectories, and priorities.
  • One-mode optimization: Timing signals for car throughput lengthens pedestrian waits and squeezes cyclists — the objective function silently picks a winner.
  • Data determines policy: If sensors mostly count cars, the data is car-centric, and car-centric data produces car-centric policy by default.
  • The requirement: City planning must model all modes simultaneously, with measurement coverage for each.

Origin-Destination Matrix From Partial Data

You Know WHERE Traffic Is — Not Where It Came From or Where It's Going
  • The inverse problem: Estimating an origin-destination matrix from link counts is massively underdetermined — thousands of OD pairs, a few hundred count locations.
  • Non-uniqueness: Many different OD matrices reproduce the exact same observed link volumes, so the data cannot distinguish between them.
  • Ill-posed: The chosen solution comes from the prior or regularizer, not the data — different assumptions give different "answers" that all fit the counts.
