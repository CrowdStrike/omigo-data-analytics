Sports Betting Pitfalls

Statistical traps that separate profitable bettors from losing ones

Vig/Juice Hides Your Edge

Vig/Juice Hides Your Edge
The bookmaker takes a 4-10% margin on every bet. At standard -110 odds on both sides, you must win 52.4% of bets just to break even. A model that is "52% accurate" actually loses money after vig. Most bettors evaluate model accuracy without subtracting the house cut. The vig is invisible in accuracy metrics but devastating to bankroll. To profit, you need not just an edge over 50%, but an edge over 50% + the vig — and that gap is larger than most realize.

Closing Line Value (CLV)

Closing Line Value (CLV)
The closing line — the odds at game time — represents the market's final consensus. If you bet a team at -3 and the line closes at -5, you captured 2 points of value regardless of whether that game wins or loses. CLV is the only proven long-term predictor of sports betting profitability. Profitable bettors consistently beat the closing line. A bettor winning 60% of bets but always getting worse numbers than closing will revert. A bettor at 48% wins but beating closing will profit long-term.

Sharp vs Public Money

Sharp vs Public Money
Sportsbooks move lines based on who bets, not how much total money arrives. A $10,000 wager from a known sharp (professional bettor with a winning track record) moves the line more than $100,000 from recreational bettors. When you see "line movement" data without knowing the source of the money, it's uninterpretable. Books have internal ratings of every account. "80% of money is on Team A" means nothing without knowing if that includes sharp action or is purely public sentiment.

Injury/News Latency

Injury/News Latency
When an injury is announced, lines move within seconds via automated systems. Your model running on yesterday's roster data is already stale the moment it produces a prediction. The information edge half-life in sports betting is measured in minutes, not days. By the time a human reads the injury report, processes it through a model, and places a bet, the market has already fully adjusted. The only way to exploit injury news is to be faster than the market — which means sub-second reaction times.

Model vs Market Efficiency

Model vs Market Efficiency
NFL point spreads are accurate to within 2-3 points of actual outcomes. The line represents the aggregated intelligence of thousands of sharp bettors, proprietary models, and market makers. Your model must be better than this collective intelligence to have an edge. If your model's predictions correlate with the closing line but don't consistently beat it, you have no edge — you're just recreating what the market already knows. Most "successful" models in backtests are just proxies for the market line with added noise.

In-Play / Live Betting Volatility

In-Play / Live Betting Volatility
Live odds change with every possession. A single touchdown flips the spread by 7+ points instantly. Any model attempting to predict the "final outcome" during the game is reacting to the same information the market has already priced in — often faster. The live market incorporates score changes, time remaining, and possession in real-time. Your model seeing "Team A scored" and updating its prediction is doing what the market did 200 milliseconds ago. Live betting edges require speed and proprietary data (video feeds, tracking data) that retail bettors don't have.

Correlated Parlays

Correlated Parlays
Two legs of a parlay may be correlated: "team covers the spread" and "the game goes over the total" are not independent events. A team blowing out their opponent both covers AND pushes the total higher. Books price parlay legs as if they're independent, multiplying odds naively. Finding genuinely correlated value — where two outcomes are more likely to co-occur than the independent pricing implies — is a real edge. But proving the correlation statistically requires large samples that are hard to accumulate in practice.

Small Sample Sizes

Small Sample Sizes
NFL teams play 16-17 games per year. "This team is 8-2 against the spread at home" represents n=10 over five entire seasons — with different rosters, coaches, and contexts each year. This is not statistically meaningful. The narrative around ATS records, "revenge games," and "teams coming off a bye" is built on samples so small that random variance explains almost everything. A 95% confidence interval on a 60% ATS record over 30 games: [41%, 77%]. Totally consistent with zero skill. Narrative masquerading as signal.
