Firewall/Policy Pitfalls

Why firewall rulesets drift into unauditable, overly permissive, gap-ridden policies that no longer match documented intent.

Rule Order Matters (First-Match-Wins)

Rule Order Matters (First-Match-Wins)
Firewall rules are evaluated top-down with first-match-wins semantics. Rule 50 says "allow traffic X"; rule 200 says "deny traffic X" — rule 200 is NEVER hit because rule 50 already matched. This is a shadow rule. Reordering rules changes the entire security posture without changing any individual rule's definition. The same set of rules in a different order produces a completely different security policy.

Shadow/Dead Rules

Shadow/Dead Rules
Studies show 25–40% of firewall rules are never triggered. They are leftovers from decommissioned servers, old projects, temporary exceptions that became permanent. Nobody knows if removing them will break something, so they accumulate indefinitely. Each dead rule increases complexity, slows processing, and makes auditing nearly impossible. The ruleset becomes archaeological sediment — layers of forgotten intent.

Overly Permissive Rules Impossible to Tighten

Overly Permissive Rules Impossible to Tighten
A rule says "allow any-to-any on port 443." It should be tightened to specific IPs, but 500 services MIGHT use it. You can't determine actual usage without monitoring. Monitoring generates 10TB/day of logs. You can't store or analyze 10TB/day. So the overly permissive rule stays. It's a one-way ratchet: easy to loosen, practically impossible to tighten.

Log Asymmetry

Log Asymmetry
Denied traffic is logged (because it's an "event"). Allowed traffic is often NOT logged (too much volume). Your data shows ONLY the blocked attacks, not what got through. You're analyzing failures-to-penetrate, not successes. This is survivorship bias applied to security: you see what was stopped but are blind to what succeeded. Your "threat landscape" is actually your "things-we-already-handle landscape."

Policy Drift from Exceptions

Policy Drift from Exceptions
Security says "deny all." Business says "we need this one exception." After 100 individually justified exceptions, the policy is swiss cheese. Each exception was reasonable in isolation, but collectively they gutted the security posture. Nobody tracks the cumulative effect. The original "deny all" intent is still documented, but the effective policy is "allow most."

Multi-Vendor Policy Inconsistency

Multi-Vendor Policy Inconsistency
Palo Alto at perimeter, cloud provider Security Groups in cloud, Kubernetes NetworkPolicies in containers. Three different syntaxes, three different models, no unified view. Each vendor has different defaults (deny-all vs allow-all), different granularity, different logging. The gap BETWEEN them is unprotected — traffic that leaves one domain and enters another may match no policy in the transition.
