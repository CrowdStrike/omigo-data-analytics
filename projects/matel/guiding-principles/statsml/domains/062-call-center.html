Call Center

Statistical pitfalls in call center analytics — metric gaming, survivor bias in IVR data, and the deception of averages.

Handle Time vs Resolution Quality

Handle Time vs Resolution Quality
Short call = efficient? Or: cut customer off, unresolved, they call back tomorrow. Long call = inefficient? Or: complex issue fully resolved, never calls again. AHT alone is misleading.

Agent A: 3 short calls (3 min each) but 2 callbacks generated. Total time invested: 9 + 6 = 15 min for one resolution.
Agent B: 1 long call (12 min) but zero callbacks. Total time invested: 12 min with full resolution.

The "efficient" agent actually costs more in total handle time.

Agent Gaming Metrics

Agent Gaming Metrics
If metric = calls/hour: agents hang up fast, transfer aggressively, avoid complex issues. If metric = resolution: agents keep "easy" calls, escalate hard ones. Every metric is gamed the moment agents know about it.

The moment a new metric is introduced, behavior shifts to optimize that metric — often at the expense of the outcome the metric was meant to measure. Goodhart's Law in action: when a measure becomes a target, it ceases to be a good measure.

Repeat Caller Identification

Repeat Caller Identification
Same person calls 3 times about same issue from different phones. System sees 3 separate interactions. Can't link them without sophisticated identity matching.

"First call resolution" metric is inflated by miscounting repeats. If you can't identify that caller #47, #112, and #203 are the same frustrated person calling from home, mobile, and work phone, your FCR rate is a fiction.

IVR Dropout = Lost Data

IVR Dropout = Lost Data
Customer navigates IVR for 5 minutes, gives up, hangs up. Their INTENT is never recorded. They become invisible demand. Only successful IVR completions generate data.

The frustrated majority is dark matter — they exist, they have needs, but your analytics system cannot see them. You only model the patients who survived the waiting room, not those who left.

Sentiment from Voice Tone

Sentiment from Voice Tone
Angry voice = negative sentiment? Or: excited happy voice about a complaint? Sarcasm? Cultural differences in vocal expression?

Voice-based sentiment analysis is 60-70% accurate at best; worse for non-native speakers. A Southern US speaker saying "Well bless your heart" or British understatement reads completely wrong. Sarcasm inverts meaning entirely.

Seasonal Surge Modeling

Seasonal Surge Modeling
January: returns from Christmas. April: tax questions. September: school enrollment. Each surge has different character.

A model trained on "average month" fails catastrophically in surge months. You need surge-specific models because the call mix, caller demographics, issue complexity, and resolution paths all change simultaneously during surges — it's not just "more of the same."
