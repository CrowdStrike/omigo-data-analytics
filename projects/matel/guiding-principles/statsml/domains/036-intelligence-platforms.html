Intelligence Platform Pitfalls (intelligence-style)

Statistical and analytical traps in entity resolution, link analysis, and intelligence fusion

Entity Resolution Across Dirty Sources

Entity Resolution Across Dirty Sources
The same person appears as "John Smith" in system A, "J. Smith" in system B, and "John W. Smith Jr." in system C — with three different addresses. Which records are the same entity? False matches create wrong conclusions (connecting innocent people to criminal networks). Missed matches create blind spots (splitting one person's activities across unlinked records). Error rates of 5-15% are common even with best-in-class entity resolution. Every downstream analysis inherits this uncertainty.

Analyst Labeling is Subjective

Analyst Labeling is Subjective
One analyst labels an event as "suspicious." Another analyst labels the same event "routine." Inter-annotator agreement is typically 60-70% for complex intelligence assessments. Your model's accuracy ceiling is the agreement rate of its labels. If humans disagree 30% of the time on what's "suspicious," no model can exceed 70% accuracy against that ground truth — and that's the upper bound. The labels aren't objective facts; they're subjective interpretations colored by training, experience, priors, and institutional culture.

Temporal Knowledge Graph Evolution

Temporal Knowledge Graph Evolution
Entity relationships change over time: Person A works for Company B in 2020, then Company C in 2022. "Connected to" depends on at what time you query. A static graph model that says "A is connected to B" without temporal qualification conflates historical and current relationships. Querying "as of date X" is essential but computationally expensive and rarely implemented correctly. Most knowledge graphs are snapshots masquerading as timeless truth, mixing relationships from different eras.

Adversarial Entities Deliberately Hiding

Adversarial Entities Deliberately Hiding
Targets use aliases, shell companies, encrypted communications, cash payments, and cutouts. They know what you're looking for and actively avoid creating the patterns you're trained to detect. The ABSENCE of data is often the signal — but you can't easily model absence. A complete lack of financial transactions for a known wealthy individual IS suspicious, but it doesn't appear in any dataset as a "feature." Sophisticated adversaries create anti-patterns: appearing deliberately normal to avoid detection.

"Connecting the Dots" Confirmation Bias

"Connecting the Dots" Confirmation Bias
An analyst has a theory, searches the knowledge graph for confirming connections, and finds them. In a dense graph, everything connects to everything within 3 hops. The connections are REAL but MEANINGLESS without a base-rate comparison. "These two suspects are connected through a shared associate" sounds significant — until you realize that in a graph of 10M people, ANY two people are connected within 3-4 hops. The question isn't "is there a path?" but "is this path shorter/stronger than expected by chance?"

Classification Confidence ≠ Actionability

Classification Confidence ≠ Actionability
The model says 85% probability this entity is suspicious. But the false positive cost = raiding an innocent person's home, freezing legitimate assets, destroying a reputation. 85% is not enough for high-cost interventions. You need 99.9%+ confidence for actions with severe consequences. A model that's "85% accurate" means 15 out of 100 flagged entities are innocent. At scale (flag 10,000 entities), that's 1,500 innocent people harmed. Model accuracy that's impressive in a Kaggle competition is completely insufficient for operational intelligence decisions.

Data Provenance and Chain of Custody

Data Provenance and Chain of Custody
Intelligence comes from multiple sources with vastly different reliability. HUMINT from one informant with unknown motivations ≠ SIGINT from intercepted communications ≠ OSINT from microblogging platform ≠ FININT from bank records. Treating all sources equally is wrong. Each source has different error modes: HUMINT can lie, SIGINT can be spoofed, OSINT can be fabricated, FININT can be structured to deceive. A model that takes all inputs as equivalent ground truth will be systematically exploited by adversaries who know which sources you trust.

Feedback Loops from Intervention

Feedback Loops from Intervention
You investigate an entity based on suspicious signals. They notice the investigation (or are warned). They change behavior — no crime is observed. Conclusion: "not suspicious." But they changed because you investigated. Your intervention influenced the outcome you're trying to measure. You cannot evaluate the counterfactual: what would they have done without your investigation? This creates a fundamental evaluation problem: successful interventions look like false positives in retrospect. The better your system works, the worse it looks in metrics.
