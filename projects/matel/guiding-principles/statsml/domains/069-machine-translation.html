Machine Translation

Neural translation pitfalls: metrics that don't measure quality, data deserts, and meaning that can't be verified at scale.

BLEU Score ≠ Quality

High N-Gram Overlap Can Mean Bad Translation; Low Overlap Can Mean Great One
  • What BLEU measures: N-gram overlap with a reference translation — not meaning, not fluency, not correctness.
  • Zero for perfection: A perfectly natural translation using different words than the reference scores near 0.
  • High for garbage: Unnatural output that happens to match reference n-grams scores high.
  • The consequence: Systems ranked by BLEU and by human raters disagree — optimizing BLEU optimizes reference-matching, not translation.

Low-Resource Language Data Desert

"Universal" Translation = Works for 20 Languages, Fails for 6,980
  • The skew: ~80% of parallel training data covers English, Chinese, and major European languages.
  • The tail: About 7,000 languages exist; most have near-zero parallel corpus to train on.
  • False universality: Benchmarks report averages over high-resource pairs, hiding total failure on the long tail.
  • No shortcut: Transfer learning and pivoting through English help marginally — data below a viability threshold means the pair simply doesn't work.

Domain Terminology Drift

One Word, Different Meaning Per Domain — General Models Mistranslate All of Them
  • Same word, new meaning: Medical "culture" = lab culture; legal "party" = litigant; financial "interest" = a rate.
  • General model default: Trained mostly on general text, it picks the everyday sense and mistranslates domain terms catastrophically.
  • Stakes scale with domain: One wrong word in a legal contract translation is a liability; in a medical instruction it can be harm.
  • The fix isn't free: Domain-adapted models need domain parallel data — exactly what specialized fields rarely publish.

Gender/Formality Ambiguity — Guessing Missing Information

The Source Doesn't Contain the Answer, but the Target Language Requires One
  • Formality: English "you" must become formal or informal in 50+ languages — the source gives no signal.
  • Gender: English "they" or "the doctor" must map to a gendered pronoun or form in many targets.
  • Forced guessing: The model must output information not present in the input — it cannot be "correct," only lucky.
  • Systematic bias: Trained on skewed corpora, models default to masculine forms ("doctor" → he, "nurse" → she), turning a guess into a bias.

Context Window Insufficient for Document Coherence

Sentence-by-Sentence Translation Loses Cross-Sentence References
  • The scenario: A pronoun in paragraph 3 refers to a noun introduced in paragraph 1 — outside the model's window.
  • The failure: "It" gets translated with the wrong gender or number because the antecedent is invisible.
  • Beyond pronouns: Terminology consistency, register, and discourse markers all drift when each sentence is translated in isolation.
  • Metric blindness: Sentence-level BLEU can't see the incoherence — every sentence scores fine, the document reads broken.

Meaning Preservation Is Unmeasurable at Scale

Only a Bilingual Human Can Verify Meaning — and No Human Can Check 1M/Day
  • The core problem: There is no automated test that proves a translation preserves meaning; proxies measure overlap or fluency, not semantics.
  • The only oracle: A bilingual human reading both sides — expensive, slow, and inconsistent between raters.
  • The scale gap: Production systems emit millions of translations per day; humans can audit a vanishing fraction.
  • The consequence: Quality at scale is fundamentally unverifiable — silent meaning errors ship, and no dashboard shows them.
