Stock Market Pitfalls

Data quality and structural issues that invalidate equity market analysis

1. Survivorship Bias in Indices
The S&P 500 today is not the S&P 500 of 2000. Failed companies are removed and successful ones added retroactively. Historical index returns are inflated by 1-2% annually because the losers are excluded from the dataset after the fact. If you backtest a strategy on "current S&P 500 constituents" using historical prices, you're only looking at the survivors — companies that didn't go bankrupt, get acquired at distressed prices, or delist. This creates a systematic upward bias that doesn't reflect actual investable returns.
2. Earnings Restatements / Point-in-Time Data
A company reports $2.00 EPS. Later, it restates to $1.50. Your database shows $1.50 for that quarter — but on the actual decision date, the market knew $2.00. Using restated data introduces look-ahead bias: your model "knew" the correct number before it was public. Point-in-time databases preserve what was known when, but they're expensive and rare. Most free data sources show the final restated values, making any historical analysis subtly contaminated with future information.
3. Market Microstructure Noise
At sub-second timescales, "price" is not a clean signal. It bounces between bid and ask (bid-ask bounce), rounds to tick sizes, reflects hidden orders you can't see, and is affected by the specific exchange routing. OHLC bars hide massive intra-bar complexity — a "flat" bar might have seen wild swings within it. Any analysis using high-frequency data must account for the fact that observed prices are noisy estimates of true consensus value, with discrete jumps and structural artifacts unrelated to information.
4. HFT Front-Running / Adverse Selection
Your limit order sits in the book. When you're wrong about direction, HFT firms pick it off instantly. When you're right, your order never fills because faster traders cancel their opposing orders. Your actual fills are adversely selected: the trades you execute have worse expected returns than your theoretical signal suggests. The gap between "signal return" and "filled order return" can be 30-50% of the alpha. This is invisible in any backtest using mid-prices or close prices.
5. After-Hours / Pre-Market Gaps
Stock closes at $100. Earnings released at 4:30 PM. Opens at $85 the next day. Your stop-loss at $95 never triggered — the price gapped right past it. Any risk model that assumes continuous price movement (stop-losses, trailing stops, position sizing based on intraday moves) is fundamentally broken for overnight risk. Approximately 30-40% of total stock returns occur during non-trading hours. Models that ignore overnight gaps are using incomplete data and underestimating tail risk dramatically.
6. Dividend Adjustments
On the ex-dividend date, a stock paying a $2 dividend drops by approximately $2 at the open. This is not a signal — it's a mechanical adjustment. If you don't adjust historical prices for dividends and splits, every ex-date looks like a crash in your data. A stock that paid $2/quarter dividends for 10 years shows a "decline" of $80 in unadjusted prices. Adjusted vs. unadjusted prices can lead to completely different conclusions about trends, support levels, and returns.
7. Index Reconstitution Effects
When a company is added to the S&P 500, all index funds must buy it simultaneously. The stock jumps 3-5% in the days surrounding addition. This is a predictable short-term return from a structural flow, not a fundamental value change. The stock often reverts over the following weeks. Similarly, deletions from an index cause predictable selling pressure. These reconstitution effects create phantom signals in price data that look like momentum or value signals but are purely structural.
8. Corporate Actions
Mergers, spinoffs, share buybacks, stock splits, rights issues, and special dividends all change the meaning of "price" for a ticker. A 2:1 stock split cuts the price in half overnight — not a crash. A spinoff creates a new entity while the parent drops by the spinoff value. Historical analysis must account for every corporate action or the price series is discontinuous. Ticker symbols get reused. Companies merge and change names. Without a corporate actions database, any long-term price analysis is unreliable.
