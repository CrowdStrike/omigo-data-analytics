Virtual Reality / AR / Spatial Computing

Spatial computing generates rich data, but nearly every VR/AR metric conflates hardware limitations — sickness dropout, comfort ceilings, tracking noise — with user behavior.

Motion Sickness Dropout Biases All Metrics

Motion Sickness Dropout Biases All Metrics
  • The trap: 40-70% of VR users get motion sickness; the sensitive ones quit early or never return, so every engagement metric describes only the resistant survivors.
  • Real-world failure: A VR fitness app touted 28-minute sessions and 85% weekly retention while 45% of new users quit within 5 minutes and never came back.
  • Demographic skew: Women experience VR motion sickness at 2-3x the male rate, giving every voluntary-participation study an invisible gender bias.
  • Content contamination: "Users prefer teleport locomotion" comes only from users who tolerated smooth locomotion long enough to compare — the ones who got sick and quit aren't in the data.

Session Length Capped by Hardware Comfort

Session Length Capped by Hardware Comfort
  • The trap: Headset weight, heat, and eye strain cap session duration regardless of content, making time-based engagement metrics measure hardware tolerance instead of interest.
  • Real-world failure: A VR social platform's "engagement decline" from 42 to 31 minutes was actually summer heat making long headset sessions unbearable.
  • Comparison invalidity: Concluding "VR is less engaging" than 2-4 hour flat-screen sessions compares a discomfort-limited medium to an interest-limited one.

Hand Tracking Noise Creates Phantom Interactions

Hand Tracking Noise Creates Phantom Interactions
  • The trap: Hand tracking registers false-positive interactions from stray finger movement, tracking snaps, occlusion, and IR interference.
  • Real-world failure: A training app's "3.2 Help presses per session" turned out to be 78% phantom presses from hands passing near the button while gesturing.
  • Analytics pollution: VR interaction heat maps cluster around camera dead zones and occlusion-prone positions — the "UX insight" is hardware geometry.

Gaze ≠ Attention (Peripheral Vision in VR)

Gaze ≠ Attention (Peripheral Vision in VR)
  • The trap: Eye tracking measures foveal gaze, but VR's 90-110° field of view means much of the processing happens in unmeasured peripheral vision.
  • Real-world failure: An ad study read "0.3 seconds of gaze" as low engagement, yet brand recall was 68% because users processed the billboard peripherally.
  • Foveated rendering confound: Degraded peripheral resolution forces users to look directly at things, inflating "gaze time" for hardware reasons, not interest.
  • Vergence-accommodation conflict: Fixed lens focal distance makes VR dwell times non-comparable to real-world attention research.

Spatial Audio Position Errors Compound Over Time

Spatial Audio Position Errors Compound Over Time
  • The trap: Head-tracking drift accumulates over minutes, misaligning audio from visuals; users unconsciously turn toward sound, creating false "attention" signals.
  • Real-world failure: In a VR theater study, drift made "looking at the stage" require a 5-8° head offset — comfortable users scored "inattentive," drift-fighters scored "engaged."
  • HRTF mismatch: Generic HRTFs cause 10-30° localization errors, so "orients toward audio cues" partly measures HRTF fit, not attention.
  • Multi-user contamination: In social VR, turn-toward-speaker feedback loops create convergence patterns misread as engagement.

Room-Scale vs Seated: Incomparable Cohorts

Room-Scale vs Seated: Incomparable Cohorts
  • The trap: Room-scale and seated users have fundamentally different movement, interaction, and task-time profiles, yet analytics pool them into one cohort.
  • Real-world failure: "40% of users never explore the left wing" correlated perfectly with play-space size — seated users couldn't get there fluently with thumbstick locomotion.
  • Accessibility confound: Seated VR includes wheelchair users, the elderly, and small-apartment dwellers — physical space is a proxy for age, wealth, and ability.
  • Interaction mode shift: Seated laser-pointer input is inherently higher friction than room-scale reach-and-grab, so pooled efficiency metrics are meaningless.

Early Adopter Bias (Enthusiasts ≠ General Population)

Early Adopter Bias (Enthusiasts ≠ General Population)
  • The trap: Current VR users are self-selected enthusiasts whose behavior and friction tolerance bear no resemblance to the mainstream audience you're designing for.
  • Real-world failure: A standalone headset's UX, built on research from PC-tethered enthusiasts, left 35% of mainstream units never set up past the initial tutorial.
  • Divergent tolerance and taste: Enthusiasts accept 5-minute setups and want long complex experiences; mass-market users abandon at any friction and prefer short passive content.

FPS Drops Create Non-Random Missing Data

FPS Drops Create Non-Random Missing Data
  • The trap: Below 72Hz, tracking degrades and users change behavior to reduce scene load — so data goes missing in exactly the most complex, most interesting scenarios.
  • Real-world failure: "Users move less in crowded spaces" was actually FPS drops triggering reprojection that makes movement feel sick — a hardware artifact read as preference.
  • A/B test contamination: A "rich vs simple environment" test really compares nausea levels; it concludes users prefer simple when they prefer not being sick.

Social VR Metrics Contaminated by Griefing

Social VR Metrics Contaminated by Griefing
  • The trap: Griefing — space invasion, blocking, noise spam — inflates "interaction" counts, so high engagement numbers can measure toxicity, not connection.
  • Real-world failure: A platform's "12.3 interactions per session" success metric hid that for female-presenting avatars, a large share of interactions were harassment events.
  • Misread proximity: "Users get closer over time" tracked griefers learning to invade space below moderation thresholds, not growing trust.
  • Session exit attribution: Analytics can't distinguish a satisfied user leaving from one fleeing harassment — both log identical session metrics.

Demo Effect: Amazing First Time, Boring by Session 5

Demo Effect: Amazing First Time, Boring by Session 5
  • The trap: VR's first-use "wow factor" decays rapidly over sessions 1-5, so first-session metrics massively overestimate long-term engagement.
  • Real-world failure: A VR museum scored 9.2/10 with first-timers but 5.8/10 with repeat visitors — the demo NPS measured novelty, not content value.
  • Retention cliff misdiagnosis: The universal session 3-5 retention drop is medium novelty wearing off, not a content problem that more content can fix.
  • Investment trap: Investors fund on first-session demo wow, then face steady-state engagement 40-60% lower than what they experienced.
