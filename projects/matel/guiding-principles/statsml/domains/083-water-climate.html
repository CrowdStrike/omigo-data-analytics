Water/Climate - Domain-Specific Pitfalls

Weather and climate data mix chaotic dynamics, sparse sensors, and shifting distributions — the statistical traps here break both forecasts and engineering standards.

Chaotic Systems — The Butterfly Effect

Small Initial Error → Completely Different Forecast After 10 Days
  • Sensitivity: A tiny error in initial conditions grows exponentially, so two nearly identical starting states diverge into completely different forecasts within days.
  • Hard limit: Deterministic point prediction is physically impossible beyond roughly 2 weeks, no matter how good the model or the compute.
  • The fix: Run probabilistic ensembles — many perturbed initial states — and report the spread, not a single path.
  • False precision: A single-path 10-day forecast is not a prediction, it is one arbitrary member of a wide fan of possible futures.

Sparse Station Networks

One Station Every 50-500km — Interpolation Invents Smoothness
  • Coverage gap: Developed countries have a weather station every 50-100km, developing regions every 500km, and the open ocean has almost none.
  • Smooth lies: Interpolating between stations produces a smooth field that misses local topography — a mountain and a valley 10km apart have completely different weather.
  • Invisible error: The interpolated map looks plausible everywhere precisely because it can only show what the sparse stations saw.
  • Downstream risk: Models trained on gridded "observations" inherit interpolation artifacts as if they were ground truth.

Climate ≠ Weather Confusion

Climate Is 30-Year Statistics; Weather Is Tomorrow
  • Different objects: Climate is the 30-year distribution of weather; weather is one draw from it — a claim about one says little about the other.
  • Both directions wrong: A climate projection says nothing about next Tuesday, and next Tuesday says nothing about the climate trend.
  • The classic error: "It's cold today" does not disprove warming — a single cold draw is fully consistent with a rising mean.
  • Signal vs noise: Daily variance is far larger than the trend slope, so the trend is only visible after averaging over decades.

Extreme Events From Limited History

Estimating the 100-Year Flood From 50-70 Years of Records
  • The definition: A "100-year flood" means 1% annual probability — but most gauges have only 50-70 years of records, shorter than the return period itself.
  • Massive uncertainty: Fitting a tail quantile from ~50 data points gives confidence intervals so wide the estimate can be off by a factor of two.
  • Cruel asymmetry: The rarest events are the most consequential AND the least estimable — exactly where you need precision, you have almost no data.
  • Hidden extrapolation: The estimate depends heavily on which extreme-value distribution you assume, and the data cannot tell you which one is right.

Downscaling Artifacts

100km Global Model → 1km Local Prediction Invents Detail
  • Resolution gap: Global climate models run on ~100km grids, but planning decisions need 1km local predictions — the missing detail must come from somewhere.
  • Method, not reality: Statistical downscaling fills the gap with patterns that live in the method's assumptions, not in the physics.
  • Bias correction backfires: Adjusting model output to match local observations can produce physically impossible scenarios (e.g., negative rainfall, broken energy balance).
  • False confidence: The 1km map looks authoritative precisely because its fine texture is fabricated deterministically.

Non-Stationarity From Climate Change

The Past Is No Longer a Guide — Return Periods Drift
  • Broken standards: Engineering standards are set from the "historical 100-year flood level," but climate change shifts the underlying distribution.
  • Wrong by construction: Every statistical return period assumes a stationary distribution — if the distribution is shifting, the number is wrong the day it is published.
  • Quiet escalation: A modest shift in the mean multiplies the tail probability, so yesterday's 100-year event becomes a 20-year event.
  • What to do: Model the trend explicitly (non-stationary extreme-value fits) instead of pooling all history as if it came from one distribution.
