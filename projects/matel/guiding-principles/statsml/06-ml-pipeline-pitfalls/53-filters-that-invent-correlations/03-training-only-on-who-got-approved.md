# Training Only on Who Got Approved

**Page type:** detail page (three card-sections, each a two-column layout table: text left 50%, canvas right 50%)
**HTML title tag:** Training Only on Who Got Approved — Filters That Invent Correlations

**Subtitle:** Labels only exist for the applicants some earlier gate let through, so the model learns the shape of that gate instead of the shape of the world.

## You Only Ever See Who Got In

**Tags:** `label availability` (red), `the setup` (blue)

- **No loan, no label** — a rejected applicant never borrowed, so no repaid/defaulted outcome exists
- **Training set is the approved set** — the only rows with a target are the ones the gate admitted
- **Lender A's rule** — approve on a decent credit score OR a strong income, either compensating
- **A compensatory gate** — a weak score is admissible when income is strong, and the reverse
- **The deleted corner** — low-score plus low-income applicants are absent by construction
- **Inside the approved pool** — a low score implies the income that bought the approval
- **The invented trade-off** — score and income look like substitutes among the approved
- **In the applicant pool** — the two are drawn independently and show no relationship at all
- **Seeded figure** — all 400 applicants r = +0.027; the 175 approved r = −0.540; 225 rejected

*Example:* Credit score and income drawn independently 400 times give r = +0.027, but the 175 who clear score + income > 1.05 give r = −0.540 — a substitution no applicant's finances contain. (Illustrative Example.)

**Key idea:** Approval is caused by both features, so keeping only approved rows conditions on a collider — the labelled sample is not a sample of applicants, it is a sample of the gate's output.

## The Model Then Learns the Wrong Trade-Off

**Tags:** `consequence` (orange), `quantified` (blue)

- **The truth model** — probability of default = 0.30 − 0.25 × score, with income entirely absent
- **What the model infers** — income gains protective power it does not have in the truth model
- **Low score, high income** — 392 rows, 77 defaults, a 19.6% rate, the worst cell in the table
- **High score, low income** — 388 rows, 37 defaults, a 9.5% rate, the safest cell in the table
- **High score, high income** — 970 rows, 127 defaults, a 13.1% rate, the bulk of the training set
- **Why the two differ** — low income forces a higher score to clear the bar, so that cell is safer
- **Low score, low income** — 0 rows, so nothing can teach the model that corner's real risk
- **Arithmetic closes** — 392 + 388 + 970 + 0 = 1,750 and 77 + 37 + 127 + 0 = 241
- **Correlation among approved** — score and income measure r = −0.513 despite being independent

*Example:* Lender A's approved book shows income associated with lower default, so a model trained on it discounts a low-score, high-income applicant — yet that cell defaults at 19.6% against 9.5% for high-score, low-income, the exact opposite of what income appears to buy. (Illustrative Example.)

**Key idea:** The gate did not merely shrink the sample, it deleted a whole cell — and a cell with zero rows cannot be learned, only guessed at, no matter how much data the other three cells hold.

## What to Do When the Gate Owns Your Labels

**Tags:** `remedies` (green), `honest limits` (blue)

- **Log every rejected application** — store its features even though no outcome will ever arrive
- **Version the gate itself** — a moved threshold changes which corner is missing from that vintage
- **Reject inference** — estimates missing outcomes, but rests on assumptions you cannot test
- **Random-approval holdout** — approve a small random slice regardless of score or income
- **Why it is the only cure** — it alone creates the missing labels, at a known budgeted cost
- **Propensity weighting** — weight approved rows by inverse approval probability toward the pool
- **Its limit too** — a weight is undefined where approval probability is zero, so the gap stays
- **Scope the claim** — the model is valid only for applicants resembling those already approved

*Example:* Lender A approves 2% of applications at random for one quarter; those rows are the only ones that can measure default risk in the low-score, low-income corner, because no amount of modelling recovers a label that was never generated.

**Key idea:** Reject inference is an assumption, not a measurement — only a random-approval slice generates real labels outside the gate, and it is the one remedy that costs money instead of credibility.
