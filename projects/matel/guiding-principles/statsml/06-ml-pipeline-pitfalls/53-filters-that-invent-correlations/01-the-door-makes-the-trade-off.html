The Door Makes the Trade-Off

Intuition first: when a group is admitted through a door, two unrelated abilities inside that group can look like rivals — no one changed, one corner of the picture is simply missing.

Two Skills That Only Look Like Rivals

the mechanismintuition
  • Two independent skills — before the door, skill A and skill B are unrelated
  • The entry rule — "good at A or good at B" admits all but the weak-at-both
  • One corner deleted — the low-A, low-B corner is empty by construction
  • Inside the survivors — a weak A must have carried a strong B to get in
  • The illusion — the remaining dots slope downhill, so the skills look like rivals
  • Nobody changed — every true A and B is untouched; the picture lost a corner
  • The collider — entry is caused by both skills, so filtering on it links them
  • Seeded figure — all 500: r = +0.038; the 257 admitted: r = -0.501; 243 rejected

Two skills drawn independently 500 times give r = +0.038 overall, but the 257 who clear the bar A + B > 0.95 give r = -0.501 — a trade-off nobody has.

Key idea: The correlation is created by looking only at survivors — the entry decision sits downstream of both skills, so keeping only admitted rows glues the two skills together.

Why Pro Basketball Rosters Look Like a Trade-Off

real anchorqualitative only
  • In the general public — height and an outside shooting touch have no real link
  • On a professional roster — the tallest players tend to be weaker outside shooters
  • The door — a roster spot demands being exceptional at something, not average
  • Empty corner — a short player without an elite shot never arrives at all
  • Three cells left — tall/poor-shooting, short/great-shooting, tall/great-shooting
  • Downhill by omission — those three cells trace a falling line with no trade-off
  • A second, real cause — tall players train inside and take far fewer outside shots
  • Honest verdict — part selection filter, part position assignment, not one cause

A 5'10" player without an elite outside shot never reaches a roster; a 7'0" player never needs one — so the short-and-cannot-shoot cell is empty by construction. (Qualitative pattern only; no correlation or player statistic is quoted, because none is sourced here.)

Key idea: Selection explains part of the pattern and role specialisation explains part of it — claiming selection alone is the same overreach as claiming the trade-off is real.

How to Tell a Real Relationship From a Manufactured One

the testdiagnostic
  • Ask the entry question — what had to be true for a row to exist in this table?
  • The dangerous filter — a gate both features push on manufactures a link
  • The safe filter — a gate on an unrelated column leaves the relationship intact
  • Wrong population — the correlation you want lives before the door, not after
  • Unseen rejects — if the rejected rows are invisible, this table cannot measure it
  • General form — conditioning on a downstream consequence of two causes
  • Classic tell — a surprising negative link inside a selected or elite group
  • Move the bar — a wider door shrinks the fake effect, a stricter door grows it

On the same seeded 500 points, the baseline is r = +0.038 at n = 500; a bar of A + B > 0.60 gives r = -0.285 at n = 401; > 0.95 gives r = -0.501 at n = 257; > 1.30 gives r = -0.541 at n = 113.

Key idea: If the effect moves when you move the entry bar and vanishes when you remove it, the effect belongs to the door — not to the two features.
