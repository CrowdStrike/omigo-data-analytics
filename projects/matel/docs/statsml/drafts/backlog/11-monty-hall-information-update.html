Monty Hall as Information Update Paradox

Backlog item for 29-statistical-paradoxes. Hard to detect, hard to explain, but real.

The Classic Setup

Three doors. One has a prize. You pick door 1. The host (who knows what's behind each door) opens door 3 — it's empty. Should you switch to door 2?

Yes. Switching wins 2/3 of the time. Staying wins 1/3. Most people's intuition says 50/50 — but the host's action gave you new information that changes the probability landscape.

Why It's Hard

The core issue: new information arrived mid-decision, and ignoring it is the mistake. People treat the situation as if nothing changed ("it's still just two doors"). But the host's reveal was not random — it was informed — and that makes the remaining door more likely to be the winner.

Data / Business Parallels

  • A/B/C test mid-flight: You launched 3 product variants. After week 1, variant C is clearly dead (p<0.001 worse). Do you reallocate its traffic? Most teams say "let the test run" — ignoring the information update. The probability that A or B is better has shifted, just like switching doors.
  • Hiring pipeline: You have 3 candidates. One bombs the technical round (door opened). The remaining two are NOT equally likely to be best — the one who was in the "uncertain" pile before now carries the probability mass from the eliminated candidate.
  • Troubleshooting: Three possible root causes. You definitively rule one out. The other two are NOT 50/50 — whichever one you initially suspected less is now MORE likely (because the eliminated option redistributed its probability to the alternatives, not equally).
  • Feature selection: You're choosing between 3 candidate features. One gets eliminated by collinearity check. The remaining two don't have equal prior — the one you were less sure about just got more probable because the space of options narrowed with informed elimination.

Why It's Hard to Detect in Practice

  • The "new information" often arrives subtly (a competitor launches, a segment churns, a feature gets deprecated)
  • Teams don't re-evaluate probabilities when options are eliminated — they treat remaining options as if nothing changed
  • The original decision framework stays frozen while the world updated around it
  • Unlike other paradoxes, this one requires you to notice that information ARRIVED and then ACT on it

Potential One-Liner

"When an option is eliminated by someone who knows the answer, the remaining options are no longer equally likely."
