Tech Industry Broscience

Industry Dogma — Practices adopted as gospel from conference talks, never tested with control groups

Practices Adopted as Gospel Without Evidence They Work
  • "Microservices are always better": Zero evidence this is true at small scale. A major tech company uses them (10,000 engineers). Your 8-person startup: the overhead of service mesh, API gateways, distributed tracing, and deployment coordination EXCEEDS the value of isolation. But: "Big tech does it" = adopted without questioning whether YOUR context matches.
  • "You need ML for this problem": Most classification problems in business: a few hand-written rules outperform any ML model for months. But: "we use AI" sounds better in fundraising decks than "we use if-else statements." The tool is chosen for narrative value, not effectiveness.
  • "Test coverage should be >90%": Zero evidence that 90% coverage threshold improves quality. Tests that assert nothing count toward coverage. 90% coverage of trivial code + 0% of critical code = number met, quality unchanged. The METRIC (coverage %) is the target, not the GOAL (working software).
  • "Standup meetings improve productivity": No controlled study has ever demonstrated this. Companies with standups grow → people conclude standups cause growth. Post-hoc correlation adopted as causation. The RITUAL persists because nobody has ever rigorously tested "teams without standup: what happens?"
  • "Hire from top universities": Zero evidence that university prestige predicts job performance (one major company studied this internally — no correlation after 2 years). But the BELIEF persists because: survivorship (you only see the successful Ivy Leaguers, not the average ones) + status signaling (hiring from Harvard makes the MANAGER look good).

Why tech broscience persists: (1) Success stories are visible, failures are silent (survivorship). (2) Practices spread by CONFERENCE TALKS not controlled experiments. (3) The people adopting them can't run controlled trials on their own company (n=1). (4) Questioning the orthodoxy is career-risky ("you don't believe in agile?"). (5) The practices SOUND rigorous (they have frameworks, certifications, consultants) which is the FORM of science without the substance.
