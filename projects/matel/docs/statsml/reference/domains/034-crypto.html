Crypto Pitfalls

Data integrity and structural issues unique to cryptocurrency markets

1. Wash Trading Inflating Volume
Exchanges fake 50-95% of reported volume to attract traders and appear more liquid than they are. "Volume" used as a feature in any model is mostly lies. You cannot reliably distinguish real liquidity from manufactured appearance of liquidity. Academic studies comparing exchange-reported volumes to independent estimates show massive discrepancies. A model trained on "high volume = significant move" is learning from fabricated data. Only regulated exchanges (and even those imperfectly) report reliable volumes.
2. DEX vs CEX Data Incompatibility
A Binance order book and a Uniswap AMM pool use completely different price discovery mechanisms. "Price" on a DEX is the output of a mathematical formula (x*y=k). "Price" on a CEX is the last matched trade in a limit order book. These are fundamentally different objects. You cannot naively combine them. Slippage on a DEX depends on pool depth; slippage on a CEX depends on order book shape. Arbitrageurs connect them, but with latency and gas costs that create persistent discrepancies.
3. MEV (Maximal Extractable Value)
Validators and miners can reorder transactions within a block for profit. Your transaction's placement is not random — it's adversarial. A sandwich attack places a buy before yours and a sell after, extracting value from your trade. The "execution price" you get depends on who else is in the same block and how the validator ordered them. Your model's predicted execution price is unreliable because it can't predict MEV extraction. This is invisible in historical price data but very real in live trading.
4. Rug Pulls / Scam Tokens in Training Data
Approximately 90% of new tokens are scams: rug pulls, honeypots, or pump-and-dumps. If your model trains on "all tokens," it's learning from mostly fraudulent projects with manufactured price action. The "universe" of tokens is poisoned. A token that goes up 1000x in a week then drops to zero is not "momentum" — it's a coordinated fraud. Excluding scams requires manual labeling that doesn't scale. Including them means your model learns patterns of fraud, not legitimate markets.
5. 24/7 Markets With No Official "Close"
There is no official daily close in crypto. "Daily return" depends entirely on which timestamp you pick as the cutoff. Different data providers use different times: midnight UTC, 4 PM EST, or arbitrary exchange-specific times. This means different "daily" statistics for the same asset depending on your data source. A "daily candle" from CoinGecko won't match one from Binance. Rolling calculations (moving averages, daily volatility) produce different values with different cutoff times. This is rarely acknowledged.
6. Oracle Manipulation
DeFi protocols rely on price oracles to determine collateral values, liquidation thresholds, and swap rates. A flash loan attack can temporarily manipulate the oracle price, exploit a protocol based on the false price, and profit — all in a single atomic transaction. The "price" your model sees in historical data may have been temporarily manipulated for exactly this purpose. These manipulations appear as spikes followed by instant reversions. They're not market signals — they're attacks exploiting protocol design.
7. Airdrop/Farming Distortion
Protocols distribute free tokens to attract users. Users create fake activity to qualify — hundreds of wallets, fake transactions, artificial volume. "Usage metrics" are 90% farming bots. TVL (Total Value Locked), transaction count, unique addresses, daily active users — all of these are inflated by incentive farming. When the airdrop ends, "users" disappear overnight. Any model using on-chain activity as a fundamental signal is measuring farming incentives, not genuine adoption. The signal is the incentive program schedule, not user behavior.
8. Regulatory Event Risk
An SEC announcement, a country banning crypto, or an exchange being charged — these cause the entire market to move 20%+ in minutes. No feature in your model predicts regulatory action. These are binary, unpredictable events with market-wide impact. A model trained on "normal" market conditions has no way to anticipate or handle these regime-breaking events. They're not tail risks in the statistical sense (they're not distributed normally) — they're structural breaks that invalidate the model's assumptions entirely.
