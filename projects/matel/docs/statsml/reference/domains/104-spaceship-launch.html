Spaceship Launch / Rocket Data

One-shot systems where data decisions are irreversible, bandwidth is limited, and ground truth only exists after you've already committed.

Pitfall Visualization
1. One-Shot System: No Second Chance If Data Is Wrong
Unlike ML where you can retrain: rocket launch is IRREVERSIBLE once engines ignite. Decision to launch based on data that may be incomplete, ambiguous, or wrong. Challenger disaster (1986): engineers had data showing O-ring risk at cold temps but data was AMBIGUOUS at the observed 36°F (no prior launches that cold). Decision: launched anyway. Data existed but wasn't conclusive. Cost: 7 lives. The data challenge: you must make a GO/NO-GO decision with INCOMPLETE data, and "wrong" = catastrophic.
2. Telemetry Bandwidth Limits During Ascent
Rocket has 1000+ sensors but telemetry link can transmit ~1-5 Mbps to ground. Must prioritize: which sensors get real-time downlink vs. stored for post-flight? Priority decision made BEFORE launch based on what you THINK will be important. If an unexpected anomaly occurs in a low-priority sensor: data exists on-board recorder but ground can't see it real-time. Discovery post-flight (if vehicle survives) or never (if it doesn't).
3. Sensor Saturation at Extremes
Vibration sensor rated to 50G. During max-Q (maximum dynamic pressure): actual vibration hits 80G. Sensor reports "50G" (saturated at max). Ground sees "vibration at limit" — can't tell if it's 50G (okay) or 200G (vehicle breaking apart). The MOST critical moment = the moment your sensors are least reliable (because they're at or beyond design limits). Same for temperature (thrust chamber), pressure (propellant tanks), acceleration (staging events).
4. Countdown Abort Decision from Incomplete Data
T-minus 30 seconds: one sensor shows anomalous reading. Is it: sensor failure (benign), real problem (abort!), or transient noise (ignore)? You have 30 seconds to decide. No time for diagnosis. Historical false alarm rate for this sensor: 2%. But 2% of "abort for false alarm" = $50M wasted. 0.01% of "launch with real problem" = $2B vehicle lost + possible lives. Decision theory under extreme time pressure with uncertain data.
5. Post-Launch Debris Tracking (Space Situational Awareness)
After deployment: must track every piece of debris (bolt, panel, stage) to prevent collision with other satellites. But: radar cross-section of a 1cm bolt = barely detectable. Tracking 30,000+ objects in orbit with ~5m position uncertainty. Collision probability calculations with uncertain positions: "these two objects have 1-in-10,000 chance of collision" — but uncertainty in BOTH positions means the real probability could be 1-in-100 or 1-in-1,000,000. You can't tell.
6. Re-Entry Communications Blackout
Vehicle re-entering atmosphere: plasma builds around it → radio signals blocked for 3-8 minutes. Ground has ZERO telemetry during the most dangerous phase. Vehicle is either: fine (can't confirm), or disintegrating (can't detect until too late). All pre-blackout data must be sufficient to predict post-blackout state. If something went wrong DURING blackout: no data exists. It's a gap in your time series at the WORST possible moment.
7. Ground Truth Only Exists After Recovery
Did the payload deploy correctly? Was the satellite orientation right? Did thermal protection work? You only KNOW after recovery (if vehicle returns) or after payload activation (hours/days later). During flight: telemetry SUGGESTS things are fine, but telemetry = indirect measurement. Direct confirmation requires physical inspection or end-to-end functional test — which can't happen until the mission is essentially over. You're committing based on PROXY measurements.
8. Test Data ≠ Flight Data (Every Flight Is Unique)
Ground testing: controlled environment, known inputs, repeatable. Flight: unique weather, unique vibration profile, unique thermal environment, unique trajectory perturbations. No two flights are identical. Testing validates the DESIGN but can't validate THIS SPECIFIC flight condition. Anomalies that never appeared in 50 ground tests can appear on flight #1 because the combination of conditions is novel. Sample size for flight data: often n=1-5 (how many times has THIS vehicle type flown?).
