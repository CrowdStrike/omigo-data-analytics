Power Generation / Multi-Source Energy

Each energy source has fundamentally different data characteristics. Managing a grid with all of them simultaneously is where the complexity explodes.

Pitfall Visualization
1. Solar Intermittency
A single cloud passing over a solar farm drops output 50% in SECONDS. Recovery: seconds later when cloud passes. This isn't gradual — it's a step function. At GW scale: a cloud bank moving across 5 solar farms in sequence creates a rolling 500MW deficit that moves geographically. Forecast error from cloud position = massive generation error. Solar is "predictable" only at the daily/seasonal level; at minute-level it's stochastic chaos.
2. Wind Ramp Events
Wind farm output can go from 100% to 10% in 30 minutes (wind dies) or 0% to 90% in 20 minutes (front arrives). These "ramp events" are the hardest to predict and the most dangerous for grid stability. Also: at very HIGH wind speeds turbines must shut down (protection mode) — output goes from 100% to 0% ABOVE a threshold. More wind ≠ more power above ~25m/s.
3. Nuclear Baseload Inflexibility
Nuclear runs at constant output 24/7 (by design and economics). Can't ramp up/down quickly. When demand drops at 2am but nuclear keeps producing — must DUMP excess power (negative pricing) or curtail renewables. Nuclear's "reliability" becomes a grid management PROBLEM when combined with variable renewables. The inflexible source constrains what the flexible sources can do.
4. Gas Turbine Startup Lag
Gas turbine peaking plant takes 10-30 minutes to reach full power from cold start. But: the demand spike it's meant to cover may last only 15 minutes. By the time it's running: the spike is over. Solution: keep turbines spinning at idle (expensive, polluting) for "hot start" capability (~5 min). The data challenge: predicting whether to pre-start turbines vs being caught cold.
5. Hydro Depends on Snowpack from 6 Months Prior
Reservoir level today = function of snowfall last winter. A drought season means hydro capacity is committed MONTHS before it becomes unavailable. Can't generate hydro from an empty reservoir. Planning: must predict water availability seasons ahead. A warm winter — reduced hydro all of the following summer. The lag between cause (weather) and effect (generation capacity) is uniquely long.
6. Grid Frequency Balancing — Generation Must EXACTLY Equal Demand
Grid frequency = 50Hz (or 60Hz). If generation > demand: frequency rises. If demand > generation: frequency drops. Must be held within ±0.5Hz or equipment is damaged. With variable renewables: generation fluctuates every second. Must continuously balance by ramping other sources. The data challenge: predicting net demand (demand minus variable generation) at 1-second resolution.
7. Curtailment = Wasted Energy (But Necessary)
Solar/wind producing more than grid can absorb — must SHUT DOWN renewable generation — energy thrown away. California curtails 1-5 GWh of solar per DAY in spring. The data shows "generation capacity X" but actual useful output = X minus curtailment. Capacity factor of a solar farm ≠ what you'd expect from sunshine alone; grid constraints reduce it. The waste is economically and environmentally costly but operationally necessary.
8. Duck Curve — Demand vs Generation Mismatch
Midday: solar floods the grid, net demand (demand - solar) drops to near-zero. Evening: solar disappears, everyone comes home, turns on AC/cooking/TV — demand spikes from minimum to maximum in 3 hours. Must ramp gas/hydro from 0 to max in that 3-hour window. The "duck curve" shape gets more extreme every year as solar grows. The RATE of change (MW/minute needed) exceeds what many generators can physically ramp.
9. Battery Degradation — Non-Linear and State-Dependent
Grid batteries (Li-ion) degrade based on: depth of discharge, charge rate, temperature, cycle count. Degradation is NON-LINEAR (first 20% of life: slow; next 20%: faster; then cliff). But: each cycle's degradation depends on ALL previous cycles (path-dependent). You can't predict remaining life from current state alone — need full usage history. A battery at "80% capacity" might have 5 years or 5 months left depending on how it got there.
10. Multi-Source Dispatch Optimization — Combinatorial Explosion
Grid operator must decide every 5 minutes: how much from solar (free but variable), wind (free but variable), nuclear (cheap but inflexible), gas (expensive but fast), hydro (medium but reservoir-limited), battery (fast but limited duration). Each source has different: cost, ramp rate, minimum output, start time, emission rate, contractual obligations. Optimizing across all simultaneously with uncertain renewable forecasts = massive stochastic optimization problem solved in real-time, every 5 minutes, 24/7.
