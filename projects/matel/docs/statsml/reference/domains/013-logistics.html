Logistics / Supply Chain

Data pitfalls unique to supply chain analytics, demand forecasting, and inventory optimization

Core tension: Supply chain data is generated by a system that reacts to its own predictions. Stock-outs suppress observed demand, promotions distort baselines, and small signals amplify through multi-tier networks. Naive models trained on this data will systematically underestimate demand and misallocate inventory.

1. Bullwhip Effect

Small demand changes amplify upstream

A 5% change in consumer demand can become an 80% swing at the raw materials level. Each tier in the supply chain over-reacts to perceived changes, adding safety stock and batching orders.

  • Mechanism: Each node forecasts independently and adds buffer
  • Result: Variance amplification at every stage
  • Data pitfall: Upstream data looks volatile but downstream is stable
  • Fix: Share point-of-sale data across all tiers; do not let each node forecast independently

2. Censored Demand

Out of stock = zero sales, not zero demand

When inventory hits zero, sales drop to zero. But demand did not disappear - customers simply left. A model trained on sales data will systematically underestimate true demand for frequently out-of-stock items.

  • Mechanism: Sales = min(demand, available stock)
  • Result: Downward bias in demand forecasts
  • Data pitfall: Zero sales periods treated as zero demand
  • Fix: Flag stockout periods; use censored regression or impute demand from pre-stockout rates

3. Promotion Distortion

Promotions pull demand forward, not create it

A promotion showing 3x lift looks like success. But the following two weeks show 0.5x sales. Total demand across 3 weeks is roughly the same - customers just bought earlier. The model sees a spike followed by a trough and treats them as separate events.

  • Mechanism: Pantry loading and deal timing shift purchases in time
  • Result: Overstated promotion ROI; post-promo "dips" trigger replenishment alerts
  • Data pitfall: Each week modeled independently loses the pull-forward pattern
  • Fix: Model demand over the full promotion + recovery window (typically 3-4 weeks)

4. Substitution Invisible

Substitution corrupts demand signals for both products

When Product A is out of stock, customers buy Product B instead. Now A shows zero demand (wrong - it was constrained) and B shows a spike (wrong - it was substitution, not genuine preference). Both signals mislead the model.

  • Mechanism: Customer switches to available alternative
  • Result: A gets less future stock (death spiral); B gets overstocked
  • Data pitfall: Model sees "A demand dropped, B demand surged" - both wrong
  • Fix: Track stockout co-occurrence; model substitution matrices from assortment experiments

5. Asymmetric Cost

Minimizing forecast error does not minimize cost

Overstock costs $0.50/unit/day (holding). Stockout costs $5/unit/day (lost sale + reputation + expediting). The optimal inventory point is NOT at the demand forecast - it is shifted toward overstocking because the penalty for being under is 10x the penalty for being over.

  • Mechanism: Holding cost << stockout cost in most supply chains
  • Result: MSE-optimal forecasts lead to frequent stockouts
  • Data pitfall: Optimizing accuracy (symmetric loss) when the business problem has asymmetric cost
  • Fix: Use quantile regression (e.g., 95th percentile) or custom loss weighted by cost ratio
