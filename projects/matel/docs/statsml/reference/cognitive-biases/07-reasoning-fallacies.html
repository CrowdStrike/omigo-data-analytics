Reasoning Fallacies

Five systematic errors in probabilistic and causal reasoning that corrupt data analysis.

1. Gambler's Fallacy

5 heads in a row → "tails is due!"

The belief that past independent events affect future probabilities. Each coin flip has no memory. After HHHHH, P(next H) is still exactly 0.50.

The confusion arises from conflating the probability of a sequence (unlikely) with the probability of the next event (unchanged).

In ML: "The model was wrong 5 times in a row, so it's due for a correct prediction." No — if the model is miscalibrated, it stays miscalibrated regardless of streak length.

2. Conjunction Fallacy

P(A AND B) ≤ P(A) always.

Adding conditions can never increase probability. Each filter shrinks the eligible population. "Male, age 45-50, income $80k-100k, homeowner, 2 kids" sounds specific and therefore likely — but specificity guarantees rarity.

In practice: Over-segmenting cohorts yields n=3 cells that look significant by chance. The conjunction of many filters guarantees tiny, unstable samples.

3. McNamara Fallacy

Measuring only what's easy to measure.

Named after Robert McNamara's Vietnam-era reliance on body counts as a proxy for winning. The metric was easy to collect but irrelevant to the actual objective (territorial and political control).

Steps: (1) Measure what's easy. (2) Disregard what's hard. (3) Presume the hard-to-measure doesn't exist. (4) Declare what can't be measured unimportant.

In ML: Optimizing accuracy on a balanced test set while ignoring fairness, latency, and user trust.

4. Goodhart's Law

"When a measure becomes a target, it ceases to be a good measure."

Once people optimize for a metric, they find ways to game it that diverge from the underlying goal. The metric improves while reality degrades.

Wells Fargo: "Accounts opened" was the KPI. Employees opened millions of fake accounts. The metric soared; customer trust collapsed.
In ML: Optimizing click-through rate → clickbait. Optimizing engagement → outrage amplification.

5. Lead Time Bias

Earlier detection ≠ longer life.

If a disease is detected earlier (e.g., by screening), the measured "survival time from diagnosis" increases even if the patient dies on the exact same date. The clock simply started earlier.

This makes screening programs appear to extend life when they may only extend the period of being a diagnosed patient.

In ML / A/B testing: Measuring "time since signup to churn" for early-flagged users vs late-flagged users shows the same illusion — earlier detection inflates apparent duration.
